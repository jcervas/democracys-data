## Chapter 1. The Vocabulary of the People (pp. 1–23)

The title comes from Schattschneider (1942, 52): "the people are a sovereign
whose vocabulary is limited to two words, 'yes' and 'no'." The book's first
line of argument is that **party nominations give political meaning to those
words**.

Three questions: "How much control do rank-and-file voters exert over the
selection of party nominees? What other actors hold power over nominations,
and what are they hoping to get from it? And what do candidates of the same
party fight over in nomination contests?"

The one-paragraph answer, worth having verbatim: "even though voters are the
sovereign deciders in most nominations, most pay too little attention to make
informed choices among the frequently obscure politicians who run. . . . **Many
voters are, only minutes after casting their ballots, unable to recall whom they
voted for.**"

**The methodological self-description is unusually frank and should travel with
any citation of this book:** "Our study is atypical of election studies in
political science in that its most important evidence comes not from
quantitative data but from field interviews."

### The four opening cases

**PA-13 — "too many candidates spoil the election."** The case the book returns
to most often.

- **Valerie Arkoosh**, a Penn medical school professor who had never run for
  office, raised **close to $2 million** from the medical community. **Medical
  money was 83 percent of her fundraising**, and the American Society of
  Anesthesiologists alone gave **$300,000**. She "was (except for three
  self-funding millionaires in other races) the most successful fundraiser in
  the entire pool of **313 candidates** for winnable open seats in 2013–2014."
  **She finished last.**
  - Her insider's diagnosis: only "a small universe" of activists willing to
    knock doors, and she recruited them too late. "**Our big mistake was that by
    the time we ramped up our field [i.e., ground campaign] it was too late.**"
- **Brendan Boyle** "spent the least money, deployed the largest number of
  volunteer campaigners, and won the four-person primary by a comfortable
  12-point margin." Half his cash and most of his volunteers came from labor.
  Philadelphia unions "put five hundred workers on the streets to knock doors
  for Boyle over the month before the election, and on Election Day they had
  monitors at all polling locations. At one of these locations, we observed vans
  dropping off loads of people wearing union tee shirts and heading into the
  polls to vote."
  - The IBEW's John J. Dougherty Jr., per the *Philadelphia Inquirer*:
    "Dougherty has the capacity to dictate or strongly influence who else gives
    to his candidates. . . . He can get seven or eight other union Political
    Action Committees to give to you, or not to give to you."
- **Daylin Leach** is the instructive loser. In six terms he "had, like Boyle, a
  virtually flawless record of union support and yet received only **$7,500**
  from union sources. **The decision of unions to cold-shoulder one good friend
  to consolidate support behind another was a critical feature of this race.**"
  Leach afterward: "It's weird because you run for office time after time, [and
  then] you run in a primary and suddenly good friends of yours who have always
  been helpful to you are with someone else . . . it's sort of emotionally
  jarring."
- **Marjorie Margolies** had TV-talk-show recognition, gratitude for her pivotal
  1993 vote on Clinton's budget, and a son married to Chelsea Clinton. A party
  official's reason for backing her is the book's frankest quote about
  self-interest: "**She's got the Clintons. If Hillary's the nominee [for
  president in 2016] and becomes president, for this district that's huge, so
  that's frankly why I'm supporting her.**"
- On the one real issue — Boyle's vote to increase regulation of abortion
  clinics — feminist groups spent **$58,000** on mailers and web advertising,
  and "the only TV advertisement criticizing Boyle on abortion was a
  **five-second hit** in a thirty-second Leach message consisting of many quick
  hits. It would have been easy for inattentive voters to miss this
  controversy."

Three MontCo candidates split their county; Boyle "had Philly to himself and

50 percent** of House primary voters could not recall one candidate's name,
against **11 percent** in the concurrent gubernatorial primary; **36 percent**
could recall more than one House name against **71 percent** for governor.

The modal voter, in the authors' telling, "may have been a woman who recalled
voting for Brendan Boyle but could think of nothing she liked or disliked about
him or any other candidate. 'I'm sorry I wasn't more helpful,' she apologized.
'**I really don't follow politics. You should have asked me about meatballs. I
know a lot about meatballs!**'"

Two party insiders in MontCo "said they expected Arkoosh or Leach to be pressured
out of the race and sounded as if they might themselves exert pressure on them to
do so." A MontCo candidate: "**We all thought that at the end of the day,
somebody was going to drop out. [But] we all stayed in, thinking someone [else]
would drop out.**" In Philadelphia the IBEW cleared the field instead — an IBEW
official "told us that the union took steps to be sure Boyle would be the only
candidate from the Philly section but declined to say what the steps were."

**NJ-1 — party or union control?** Donald Norcross won **72 percent** of a
three-candidate race after being endorsed by all three county Democratic
committees, putting him on the county line on every ballot. His brother George
was "an informal leader in the state Democratic Party," and Donald had spent
**sixteen years** as president of the Southern New Jersey AFL-CIO Central Labor
Council. "Donald is the union movement, and the union movement and Democratic
politics are nearly always hand-in-hand." The off-line ballot position is called
**"ballot Siberia."** Sources "could recall no cases in which a party-endorsed
candidate in a House primary failed to win."

**AR-2 — Little Rock.** French Hill (establishment banker) 55 percent, Ann
Clemmer 22, Colonel Conrad Reynolds 22. **Arkansas requires 50 percent to be
nominated**, so Hill "faced more danger than these vote totals suggest" — a
runoff against a firebrand was the risk, and he avoided it.

- Spending: **Hill $800,000; Clemmer $140,000; Reynolds $80,000.**
- Hill was an ideal Tea Party target — he had publicly supported TARP and led a
  sales tax increase campaign. An anti-establishment operative: "He's a banker
  that [sic] doesn't think *you* pay enough taxes . . . In the right hands, that
  knife will cut."
- Why the attack never landed: money. Hill "raised 'the vast majority' of his
  campaign money from people who either knew him personally or from business
  dealings or worked for people who knew and supported him."
- **Clemmer's case is the sharpest illustration of Proposition 1.** She had
  passed a notably strong anti-abortion law, but "two pro-life activists to whom
  we spoke . . . were lukewarm toward her," viewing her legislative leadership on
  it as weak and noting she had taken up the issue late "in significant part for
  its value in attracting evangelically oriented voters." She had the Susan B.
  Anthony List endorsement; the **local** anti-abortion activists were for Hill.
  Business donors also preferred the neophyte: "I don't know what more they
  [business donors] want. I've never voted against anything you've been for." A
  second source: "I don't think she is even on their [business's] radar. Hill is
  'one of them' in a very personal, go-to-dinner kind of way."
- Reynolds "legally change[d] his name from Conrad Reynolds to Colonel Conrad
  Reynolds so that his military background would appear on the ballot." Club for
  Growth stayed out: one of its directors, Steve Stephens, was an Arkansan
  personally friendly with Hill — and **Hill's finance chair was Warren Stephens,
  the younger brother of the Club for Growth director**. "They see each other at
  the country club. I don't think [Stephens] is coming after [Hill] with a
  hacksaw."

**NC-6 — Greensboro.** The counter-case, where an insurgent *did* win.
**Mark Walker**, music minister of a large Baptist church, in a **nine-candidate
field** against Phil Berger Jr., son of the state senate president pro tem.
Berger took **34 percent**, short of North Carolina's **40 percent** threshold.
Walker finished second with **25 percent on just eleven thousand actual votes**
(Berger seventeen thousand), then won the runoff.

- "This small number of voters is well within the range of what a
  volunteer-driven campaign of voter contact — in contrast to a money-driven
  mass-advertising campaign — can reach."
- The machine: a core of **twenty-five to thirty-five volunteers** giving two to
  three hours once a week throughout the primary, plus **an additional hundred**
  in the last weeks. Roughly **40 percent from Walker's own congregation, 40
  percent from other congregations**, and the rest from **"Conservatives for
  Guilford County, described as a Tea Party group by another name."**
- Phone callers "were not scripted by the campaign," which left it "to the
  individual volunteering to use the authenticity of their own connection to
  [Walker]."
- The comparison the authors draw: Reynolds made the same anti-establishment
  attack in AR-2 and failed, because "many voters cannot tell the difference
  between anti-establishment and establishment candidates in multicandidate
  fields unless both mount strong campaigns."

### The five propositions, stated together

1. Groups "typically care most about selecting candidates committed to their
   particular agendas; they care much less about liberal-conservative ideology."
   Named exceptions: **MoveOn and DailyKos supporting Daylin Leach**, "for these
   groups, the motive for involvement was liberal ideology."
2. Policy demanders have developed **vetting and vouching** practices.
3. Groups **coordinate** across group boundaries. Illustrated by *Pirates of the
   Caribbean*: Elizabeth Swann is elected Pirate King because every other captain
   votes for himself except Jack Sparrow, who votes for her. Three lessons: who
   wins a multicandidate election can come down to who coordinates; a coordinated
   minority can prevail over an unorganized majority; and **"the larger the number
   of players who fail to coordinate, the easier it is for even a very small
   minority to form an alliance and win."**
4. **Most winning candidates anchor their campaigns in major support from a single
   policy-demander group or association of these groups.** Anchoring is formally
   defined later as "support from a single policy-demanding group that is, by
   itself, sufficient to make the recipient candidate electorally viable."
5. Electorates have limited ability to give policy direction.

The fairness note the authors attach: readers disturbed by all this "should keep
in mind that they are not the whole story of congressional politics. Candidates
who have been assisted to office by a group are unlikely to remain in office by
attending to the interests of that group alone."

### The study design

- Boatright (2014, 104) on the state of the field: "**Many of our ideas about
  [House] primaries are in fact inferences drawn from other types of elections or
  from theoretical literature.**"
- Three data collections: **field interviews; content analysis of newspaper
  coverage; exit surveys in four contests.** Plus FEC files, local newspapers and
  blogs, Wikipedia, and Google. **UCLA undergraduates assisted in many phases.**
- **346 interviews**, "an average of 6.9 interviews for each of the **fifty**
  nominations for which we did interviews." On-the-ground interviews during the
  primary period covered most cases; post-election interviews in Washington and
  by phone covered **seven** of the missed ones; **five cases have no interviews
  at all** and "remain a limitation."
- The honesty about limits is worth quoting: "even in our best-understood races
  . . . we cannot claim to have a definitive interpretation of what happened. In
  several cases, a single interview changed our understanding of a race
  significantly, and we recognize that other interviews-that-didn't-happen might
  have had similar impact." And: "**we strive to resist the temptation to attempt
  to explain 'why' any particular race came out as it did.** . . . The
  counterfactuals are too many and too complex."
- **The disclaimer that governs everything:** "none [of the propositions] has been
  validated by any sort of scientific hypothesis test. Each is simply our
  interpretation of the large and small pieces of evidence that emerged from our
  investigation. **The term for this kind of research is *exploratory*.**"

### Case selection — and the resolution of the N puzzle

This is where the 55/53/50/49 confusion I flagged in earlier chapters finally
resolves.

- The 435 districts hold **more than eight hundred** party nominations every
  cycle. Most are lightly contested.
- **"Winnable open-seat"** = open (incumbent retired or died) **and** winnable
  (the nominee has a good chance in the general). "Most House members launch their
  House careers in a winnable open-seat nomination, making it the most important
  gateway to the House of Representatives."
- **The 55 cases come from 51 different open-seat districts. Forty-seven were safe
  for one party and hence winnable only by that party; four districts were
  competitive and hence winnable by both parties.** 47 + (4 × 2) = 55.
- Winnability was set by **Cook's Partisan Voting Index**, adjusted for 2014 being
  a good Republican year: winnable for Democrats if the PVI was Democratic or
  even; winnable for Republicans if the PVI was Republican, even, **or up to
  D+4**.
- Special elections called when a sitting member died or resigned are included.
- **"Of our fifty-five nominations, fifty-two were determined by voter primaries;
  two were determined by party conventions; and one was decided by a party
  convention following an inconclusive primary."**

So: **55 nominations → 53 involved a primary** (52 + the inconclusive one) →
Table 4.1's "fifty-three winnable open-seat primaries." Of those 53, **three had
only one candidate**, leaving 50 contested → chapter 7 could source newspapers
for **49**. Every number the book uses is now accounted for.

## Chapter 2. "Politicians There Will Always Be" (pp. 24–44)

History, rules, and a literature review. **The rules section and its four figures
are the most directly reusable material in the book for a data course.**

### The history, in one line

The chapter's argument is that the direct primary did not do what its advocates
said it would, and that the people who passed it knew that.

- The first public nomination for federal office — Jefferson, 1796 — was made by
  "a caucus of his supporters in Congress." Late-1790s nominations moved to
  volunteer "party committees" advertising public meetings in newspapers. A Bucks
  County, Pennsylvania notice is quoted in full; its stated object was "to collect
  information respecting the persons proper to be chosen to the several elective
  offices, and generally to condense the rays of political light and reflect them
  strongly on all around."
- Washington was "one of the haters" — he wrote that if the Democratic-Republicans
  recommended a broomstick and called it "a true son of liberty," the stick "would
  command their votes in toto!"
- The 1830s caucus-convention system worked because first-stage meetings "were
  small enough and the attendees were familiar enough to one another that genuine
  deliberation occurred." Participation "was limited to white men. But for this
  class of citizen, the nomination process operated in an authentically democratic
  fashion."
- Post–Civil War growth broke it. Caucuses of "a few score" became "thousands."
  Party leaders "did all they could to ensure that there was little for voters to
  do once they arrived . . . than to rubber stamp a previously negotiated slate"
  (Reynolds 2006, 31). Charles Merriam (1908, 7): nominations were "open to every
  abuse that unscrupulous men, dazzled by almost incredible wealth and dictatorial
  power, could devise and execute."
- La Follette: "if bad men control the nominations, we cannot have good
  government. . . . we must abolish the caucus and convention by law, place the
  nomination of all candidates in the hands of the people . . . and make all
  nominations by direct vote at a primary election."

**The key historical claim, and it is a good one for a data course** — Ware
(2002, 80): direct primary laws "were passed by partisan-controlled state
legislatures at a time when parties were still at the height of their powers;
**they were not imposed on parties by either anti-party reformers or anyone else,
but were attempts by partisans to solve problems they themselves faced.**"

Unsurprisingly, then, "the primary laws crafted by these legislatures contained
features that advantaged party-backed candidates. One was a requirement for
ballot designs that, like the New Jersey 'county line' . . . displayed the names
of party-endorsed candidates at the top of the ballot and others in a separate
and more crowded space."

Merriam again, in 1908, as primaries spread: "Some 'bosses' are wondering why
they feared [the direct primary]. Some 'reformers' are wondering why they favored
it." And Oscar K. Davis in 1924: "the bosses easily control the party
organization, and get out their vote in the primaries, while the masses of voters
pay no attention to it. . . . **A ridiculous minority, instead of a substantial
majority, constantly rules.**"

The chapter's title comes from H. J. Ford: "The direct primary may take advantage
and opportunity from one set of politicians and confer them upon another set, but
**politicians there will always be so long as there is politics**" (cited in Key
1965, 394–95).

California is offered as the proof: primaries let voters "clean railroad interests
out of state government," after which "progressive legislators turned to
ensconcing themselves in power. When voters threw them out too, lobbyists beyond
the reach of primaries took over as the leading middlemen of California politics"
(Mowry 1951; Masket 2009).

### The five rule sets — the single most useful table in the book for a rules chapter

The 55 nominations took place under **five different sets of rules**:

| Rule set | Nominations | Detail |
|---|---|---|
| **Single-party primary** (plurality) | **28** | Winner needs "only a single vote more than their nearest rival." **In 13 of these 28, the winner won with less than a majority.** |
| **Single-party primary with runoff** | **14** | Runoff triggered if no candidate hits a required share. **A runoff was needed in 9 and not needed in 5.** |
| **Single-party primary with convention** | **3** | Convention required if no candidate reaches **35 percent**. **One had a convention, two did not.** |
| **Party convention** | **2** | In one case final; in the other a primary runoff could have followed if the loser had petitioned. **No petition was made, so voters had no opportunity to weigh in on either.** |
| **All-party primary** (top two) | **8** | **Five ended with one Democrat and one Republican as the top two; in three the general election featured one Republican versus another.** |
| **Total** | **55** | |

The authors' own caution: "fifty-five is a small number of cases for studying the
effects of five different systems."

**Two complications with all-party primaries, recorded because they are a coding
decision, not a fact.** Because D and R votes pool into one total, vote shares
"can't be straightforwardly compared to shares from a single-party primary." So
the authors **"treat each all-party primary as if it were two separate
single-party primaries that just happened to be on the same ballot."** In CA-31,
four Democrats and three Republicans become "one Democratic primary with four
candidates and one Republican contest with three," and only the Democratic side is
analyzed because the district was safely Democratic. **"Pete Aguilar is counted as
the winner, because he was the Democrat with the most votes, even though
Republican Paul Chabot got more total votes than any candidate, including
Aguilar."** Margins and spending shares are likewise computed within party.

And when the top two are same-party, "the general election is structurally
equivalent to a primary runoff in a safe district," so **CA-25R, LA-5R and WA-4R
are included in all analysis of runoffs**.

### The four figures — distributions of the 53 primaries

**FIGURE 2.1. Field size in fifty-three winnable open-seat primaries.** Two
panels: all candidates, and candidates winning 10+ percent.

- Field size ran **from one candidate (3 cases) to ten or more**.
- **Only eleven primaries had just two candidates. Thirty-nine had three or more.
  Three had only one.**
- "Runoff and all-party systems tended to attract more candidates than simple
  party primaries."
- Why weak candidates matter even though they cannot win, in three ways: they
  **draw votes from a leading candidate** ("spoilers"); they **command some share
  of news coverage**, since debate stories "may strive to give each candidate
  equal attention, regardless of their chances of winning"; and they **increase
  the cognitive burden on voters**, "possibly discouraging some voters from making
  the effort to engage."

**FIGURE 2.2. Vote margins in fifty winnable open-seat primaries.** Winner's share
minus runner-up's, against presidential general-election margins since WWII.

- **Median House primary margin ≈ 13.7 points. Median presidential margin ≈ 5.0.**
- "Close to **a fifth** of House primaries were decided by 5 percentage points or
  less, versus almost **half** of presidential elections."
- "Close to **40 percent** of House primaries were decided by 20 points or more,
  versus only **10 percent** of presidential elections."
- The three single-candidate primaries are omitted, "which reinforces the point
  that House nominations tend to be one-sided."

**FIGURE 2.3. Size of House primary electorates.** Three panels. Excludes the
three single-candidate contests.

- Most districts in 2013–14 had about **710,000 citizens** and around **525,000
  eligible** voters.
- **Median votes cast in the primary: about 52,000.**
- **Median votes won by the winning candidate: about 20,000.**
- **Median winning margin: about 6,200 votes.**

The volunteer arithmetic the authors build on this is the chapter's best passage
and is entirely reproducible: **"thirty phone callers working fifteen hours a week
for four weeks and reaching one voter every five minutes could contact more than
twenty thousand voters."** Alternatively **"one hundred callers working eight
hours a day over the three days before the election could reach close to thirty
thousand voters."** Both operations together: "a small volunteer force could reach
more than fifty thousand voters." Against a median winning margin of 6,200, that
is decisive.

**FIGURE 2.4. Campaign spending.** Two panels.

- Most winners spent between **$250,000 and $1.5 million**; **median about
  $734,000**.
- Median winner gets about 20,000 votes, so **"this candidate spends just under
  $37 per vote."**
- Individual donors were capped at **$2,600** and "average contribution was about
  $1,000." **To raise $734,000 in $1,000 amounts requires 730 individual
  contributions** — "a number that illustrates how valuable the help of organized
  groups can be."
- Relative spending by the winner, against the runner-up:
  - **33 winners spent at least one-third more** than the runner-up.
  - **9 spent about the same.**
  - **8 won despite spending at least one-third less.**
  - The authors' reading: "**a pattern that shows that spending the most money is
    not essential.**" Those eight are the volunteer campaigns — Walker, Knight,
    Moolenaar, Boyle.
- And the chicken-and-egg caveat, stated plainly: "Some money raised by successful
  candidates in House primaries comes from donors who anticipate the candidates'
  success and want to curry favor. Money that candidates raise from such benefit
  seekers may be unnecessary for winning — **the effect of being likely to win
  rather than a genuine cause of winning.** We cannot solve this chicken-or-egg
  problem."

### The literature review, compressed

Two traditions.

**Candidate-centered.** Fowler and McClure, *Political Ambition* (1989): "Ambition
for a seat in the House, more than any other factor — more than money,
personality, or skill at using television . . . is what finally separates a
visible, declared candidate from [those who merely consider running]." Jacobson
(2013, 24): "The local party organization's influence on congressional nominations
varies but is typically feeble . . . Usually, the nomination is not something to
be awarded by the party but rather a prize to be fought over . . . by freebooting
political entrepreneurs." Boatright (2014, 94): "there is little evidence . . .
that parties can do very much to influence primary outcomes." Herrnson (2012, 41)
and Herrnson, Panagopoulos and Bailey (2019, 8). Aldrich, *Why Parties?* — the
party's "effective monopoly on the resources" broke in the 1960s, leaving
candidates as "autonomous political actors" (2011, 282, 303). Ehrenhalt (1991)
went "so far as to assert that contemporary candidates 'self-nominate'."

The authors' critique of Fowler and McClure is precise and worth keeping: the book
"reports, for example, that union leaders made strenuous efforts to get Slaughter
into the race, even doing a poll and assigning campaign staff before she had
decided to run; but in the book's account, the union's early support was slotted
as **context** for Slaughter's critical decision to run rather than as a
theoretically important influence of a key interest group."

Bonica (2017) is singled out as the strongest recent candidate-centered work:
early fundraising is rooted in candidates' personal and professional networks,
measured by shared profession or shared wealthy ZIP code. **"Political-party
organization plays no role in Bonica's paper. It is not even mentioned."**

Hirano and Snyder, *Primary Elections in the United States*, find primaries provide
"meaningful political competition — and hence political accountability — in
one-party states and districts that would otherwise lack both," and that winnable
open-seat primaries are "typically lively affairs, involving two or more serious
candidates with strong resumes" (307). "Like Bonica, Hirano and Snyder offer no
positive role for party organization in contemporary primaries, nor do they
identify any role for interest groups."

**Party-as-network.** The rethink. Older view: a party is "a team of politicians
who cared single-mindedly about winning elections and controlling government for
the personal benefits it afforded" (Downs 1957). New view: a party is "a network
of interest groups, activists, campaign professionals, and others sharing an
interest in the success of their party."

- **Bawn et al. (2012), "A Theory of Political Parties"** — the authors' own prior
  paper, which "motivated the present study." Defines a party as **a coalition of
  policy-demanding groups seeking to nominate candidates friendly to their
  agendas**, and suggests parties back politicians "not just on their electoral
  prospects but also on the likelihood that, if elected, they will champion the
  party's policy demands."
- **Dominguez (2011)**, 32 House primaries: a candidate's chance of winning
  **varied from 20 percent to 87 percent** as her share of endorsements from
  informal party sources rose from 0 to 100 percent (540).
- **Hassell (2017)**, Senate and House 2004–2014, measuring network support as
  donations from donors who also gave to the party's congressional campaign
  committee: "Parties are not merely neutral players in the process. Their
  involvement . . . has a significant and substantial influence on nomination
  outcomes" (20).
- **Masket (2009)**, four California communities, coining **informal party
  organization (IPO)**: "Nominations are made in primaries that typically have low
  turnout, little advertising, essentially no media attention, and no rival party
  labels among which to choose. For these reasons, nominations are often easily
  controllable by political insiders . . . I argue that these IPOs are the heart,
  soul, and backbone of contemporary political parties" (5). IPOs are "parties by
  other means."
- **Cowburn (2024)** on factional division 2006–2020 — the finding most relevant
  to the corpus: "primary voters in Republican contests did not consistently favor
  either insurgent or establishment candidates, but . . . whichever candidate
  emerged from contests between the two types tended to represent the district
  **more conservatively than it had previously been represented**." Cowburn
  concluded that rightward movement "**appears disconnected from primary voters'
  preferences**" (163).
- **Blum** distinguishes "consociational factions" that work within the party from
  "insurgent factions" that "take a combative posture toward their host party."

### The three storylines

- **Voters** — "the formal sovereigns in the selection of nearly all House nominees
  in our study — all but the three chosen by party convention."
- **Policy demanders** — candidates do raise their own resources, "**but in a
  departure from candidate-centered expectations, candidates do not raise
  resources by dint of general energy, ambition, and skill; they do so by
  developing skills and commitments that make them good agents for people wanting
  particular things from government.**"
  - The "one of us" motive: for religious, social and ethnic groups the interest
    "had a very general form: to send 'one of us' to Congress — that is, someone
    who can be trusted to deal with the unforeseen ways in which group interests
    can intersect with national policy."
  - And the observation that groups are not party loyalists: "**none of the groups
    acted on behalf of party interests per se**" — even local party organizations
    "could be most interested in the nomination of the candidate who would give the
    best chance of keeping control of local patronage jobs and contracts. **Only
    the congressional-campaign arms of the two parties . . . were completely
    consistent in their commitment to optimizing party strength in Congress.**"
- **Factions** — "muted in the Democratic nominations . . . but present in
  **twenty-seven of thirty-five** Republican ones and **intense in about half**."
  Insurgents beat establishment candidates in **twelve of the twenty-seven**, "most
  successfully in districts in which establishment coordination was absent. **This
  last result is the most important and also most difficult to interpret** — did
  coordination contribute to the defeat of insurgents, or did insurgents run more
  often in districts where establishment coordination was absent?"

### For the corpus

**Chapter 2's four figures are the most corpus-ready material in the entire book,
and every one of them is rebuildable from public data.** Field size, winning
margin, electorate size and spending, across 53 primaries. The corpus already has
FEC bulk files (`campaign-finance`, `independent-expenditures`) and returns
(`clerk-source`, `county-returns`). A chapter could reproduce all four
distributions for a recent cycle and put them beside the book's 2014 versions.

**The single best number to build a chapter around is the arithmetic on p. 32.**
Median primary electorate ~52,000 votes; median winner ~20,000 votes; median
margin ~6,200 votes; median spend ~$734,000, which is **just under $37 per vote**.
Then: thirty volunteers on phones for four weeks reach twenty thousand voters. The
whole argument of the book — that organized groups decide nominations — rests on
those four numbers being small. That is a chapter about **denominators**, which is
what the corpus is for, and it needs no interviews at all.

**The five-rule-set table is now the spine of the rules chapter I flagged under
chapter 5.** Chapter 2 supplies the counts (28 plurality / 14 runoff / 3
convention-trigger / 2 convention / 8 top-two) and the consequence that matters:
**in 13 of the 28 plurality contests the winner had less than a majority**, and in
2 cases **no voter had any say at all**. Chapter 5 supplies the state-by-state
mechanics. Together they are a complete chapter with no interview dependency.

**The all-party-primary recoding is a first-class teaching example of a decision
that changes the answer.** The authors split one ballot into two "primaries,"
count Aguilar as the winner though Chabot got more votes, and compute margins and
spending shares within party. It is defensible and clearly explained — and it means
their "winner" is not the person who got the most votes. That is exactly the kind
of documented, consequential coding choice `levels-of-aggregation` and
`census-access` already teach.

**Ware's finding is the historical hook for the whole thing:** the direct primary
was written by the party politicians it supposedly disempowered, and they wrote
ballot-order rules like the county line into it. The reform and the workaround
arrived in the same statute.

## Chapter 3. What Do Groups Want? (pp. 45–81)

Opens with the book's first numbered proposition, printed in italics under the
chapter title:

> **Proposition 1.** *The policy-demanding groups that participate in House
> nominations typically care most about selecting candidates committed to their
> particular agendas; they care much less about liberal-conservative ideology.*

The chapter's job is to make that plausible. The authors are explicit that it
does not do more than that: the seven cases "even taken together do not amount
to systematic evidence. Our claim is only that the accounts of the seven
nominations demonstrate the empirical plausibility of the first proposition."

### The opening case: CA-35, Norma Torres

- Torres spent **$175,000** on her primary — "the second-lowest spending total
  for any winning candidate in a contested primary in our study," and still
  **five times** what her nearest competitor spent.
- She took **67 percent** against three other Democrats.
- The district was safe enough that **no Republican entered the all-party
  primary**.
- Two labor councils — Los Angeles, and San Bernardino–Riverside — both
  endorsed. Fifteen California and national union PACs then contributed
  **$50,000**.
- Unions practice "a norm of deference to each union in its own domain." A
  union president: "If it's a school board race, I'm usually going to say 'I'm
  probably not the best person to come to first. You should reach out to your
  teachers or you should reach out to [the teachers union].'"

The chicken-and-egg question the authors say "comes up in many of our cases":
did labor back Torres because she would win anyway, or because she championed
their issues? "The answers to this perennial question are yes and yes."

### Why groups care who wins a nomination

Two reasons, given as the theoretical spine.

1. **Individual members have real power.** Quotes Chen (2019, 29) at length on
   the many levers one member has — cosponsor, amend at markup, signal
   positions, work a compromise, "speak up or stay quiet at a committee
   meeting," change language in conference. And Hall and Wayman (1990, 797):
   "for the most part the purposive legislator is free to choose among the
   abundant alternatives with only modest constraints imposed by constituents,
   colleagues, or other actors."
2. **Factional division inside each party.** The book's terms are
   **establishment** and **insurgent**. Establishment factions "consist of
   majorities of members who support each other's agendas in exchange for
   support of their own." Insurgent factions are smaller, feel slighted, and
   want a different direction. Following Blum, these are "parties within the
   parties."

### Three worked examples of group motivation

**HI-1, Mark Takai and the Hawaii State Teachers Association.** The most
concrete list of asks anywhere in the chapter, and the best single illustration
of what "particularistic" means. The union wanted: waivers on No Child Left
Behind; reauthorization of the Every Student Succeeds Act; Title I funding "as
much money as we can get for both English language learners, special education
and those things"; and more resources for immigrant children arriving from
several Pacific islands. The official hoped Takai would land on House Education,
but if he went to Armed Services instead, hoped he would negotiate "a more
generous reimbursement formula to school districts that educated large numbers
of children from federal military installations." Two officials separately said
**upward of a thousand teachers** would turn out to work for him.

An official on why they backed him: "He [Takai] always really, really supports
our issues. Always. . . . He would take time to call us, email us, text us . . .
'what are you guys telling us,' 'what do you folks want us to do,' 'how do we
get the votes,' 'how are we going to get there.' 'Let's help each other.'"

Note the authors' aside: "None of these union leaders expressed the slightest
dissatisfaction with their party's leadership in Washington."

**NC-7 and NC-6, agriculture.** Farm support converged on David Rouzer in the
Seventh. The contrast with the Sixth is the useful number:

> Agricultural interests contributed **$288,000 to Rouzer but only $20,000 to
> Berger**, suggesting that they saw Rouzer as a potential **champion** and
> Berger as a **mere ally**.

"Champion" versus "ally" is the chapter's operative distinction and it is
measured in dollars.

**Club for Growth, as the factional example.** Its 2014 website: "Our mission is
economic freedom." A spokesperson on strategy: "is to build a sustainable
pro-growth majority of the majority, and the way we do that is by focusing on
what we call 'A' races: **safe open Republican House seats**." Supported four
candidates in safe open-seat contests in 2014; both winners — **Barry
Loudermilk (GA-11) and Gary Palmer (AL-6)** — joined the Freedom Caucus.

### Ideology or policy demands?

The authors take the standard Downsian left-right model (Downs 1957) seriously
and then set it aside. They are careful that ideological *rhetoric* was
everywhere: "Most Republican candidates presented themselves as conservatives,
often, for emphasis, as *real* conservatives." These invocations were "so
ubiquitous as to be almost meaningless."

Four places where left-right logic did hold, listed as bullets:

- **Exit polls (chapter 7) show House primary voters are largely unresponsive to
  candidates' ideological positions.** Consistent with Ahler et al. (2016).
  Cites Hall and Thompson (2018, 2025) for the contrary general-election
  finding, plus Jain and Lavelle, Bonica and Grumbach (2025).
- Groups gave ideology "some strategic weight" only in the small number of
  competitive districts where general-election voters might reject an extremist.
- **National party committees (DCCC and NRCC) "appeared to care more about
  ideology than any other actor in the nomination process,"** typically
  preferring electable moderates (Hassell 2017) — again limited to competitive
  districts.

**The X-Y-Z hypothetical** is the cleanest statement of the argument. Three
Democrats: X liberal on climate and abortion but conservative on labor rights;
Y liberal on climate and abortion, somewhat conservative on abortion [*sic* —
the text says Y is "liberal on labor and climate change but somewhat
conservative on abortion*]; Z extremely liberal on all three. Under an ideology
model, moderate labor should be indifferent between the two moderates. In fact
labor may prefer extreme Z "*if it perceived Z to be more strongly committed to
labor rights.*"

The real case matching this: **IA-1.** Labor backed Pat Murphy, the "liberal"
pro-labor champion, over Monica Vernon, the more moderate pro-labor
alternative, "despite reason to be concerned about losing the general
election." Murphy won the primary and lost to Republican Rod Blum.

### Faction is not ideology either

The insurgent groups' agendas "were not only different from each other but
potentially in conflict":

| Group | Agenda |
|---|---|
| Club for Growth, FreedomWorks | libertarian, small-government |
| Madison Project | strict adherence to their view of the Constitution |
| Family Research Council, Eagle Forum | pro-family, tending to conflict with libertarian ideals |
| Tea Party chapters | fiscal conservatism **and** opposition to immigration — the second conflicting with libertarian ideals |

Left-right also misses **party leadership change** as a goal. The Madison
Project's 2014 site sought candidates who "will not only effectively articulate
all the tenets of constitutional conservatism, but have the courage to fight for
those values in the face of opposition from Republican Party bosses. We seek
candidates who will change GOP leadership, not those who will be changed by the
party leadership."

The establishment side is symmetric. *Time* on the US Chamber of Commerce: it
"has cemented itself as the GOP Establishment's heaviest hitter in the fight to
reclaim the party from Tea Party zealotry. It has forked over about **$15
million** to boost business-friendly candidates in 2014 [primaries], more than
any other Republican group. And it has amassed an undefeated record in nearly a
dozen races." The authors' gloss: the chamber wanted "business-friendly"
candidates, "not ideologically moderate ones."

### The factional scorecard — the study's own counts

The most useful single passage for anyone wanting numbers out of this book:

> Insurgent candidates ran in **twenty-seven of thirty-five Republican
> contests**. In **twelve** of these twenty-seven, the insurgents won.
> Establishment candidates prevailed, often easily, in the other **twenty-three**
> GOP nominations (defeating insurgents in **fifteen** cases and winning without
> insurgent competition in the remaining **eight**).

So: 35 Republican contests, 12 insurgent wins, 23 establishment wins, of which 8
were uncontested by any insurgent.

On the Democratic side factional division "was present as well in 2013–2014 but
was much less contentious." The most important factional group was **EMILY's
List**, which "wanted to change the balance of power in the party, in its case
to equal numbers of male and female House members" — but was "not associated
with ideological language in the way that Republican insurgents were," and its
candidates were not consistently left or right of their male opponents. Points
forward to Justice Democrats in 2018 and Ocasio-Cortez.

### The seven nominations

**1. GA-1, Buddy Carter (pharmacist).** The purest interest case in the book.

- Carter raised roughly **$430,000** from pharmacy owners, pharmacy PACs and
  employees of pharmacy-related businesses — **44 percent of all money he
  raised**. Also made personal loans of **$500,000**.
- Bob Johnson (physician, insurgent) drew Citizens United, Senate Conservatives
  Fund, the Madison Project and Club for Growth. Club's **independent
  expenditure of $390,000 was about a third of Johnson's total spending**.
  Medicine-related money came to about **$490,000, or 43 percent** of Johnson's
  fundraising.
- Business and professional PACs *other than* pharmacy, medicine and finance:
  **Carter ~$90,000, Johnson $2,000, McCallum $1,000**.
- Result: six entered, three strong. Primary — Carter 36, Johnson 23, McCallum
  21. Runoff — **Carter 54–46**, despite being outspent by both rivals.
- Why pharmacists: "Everything inside a pharmacy is controlled by public policy.
  From how many technicians you can have in a pharmacy, to where you have to
  place certain drugs, how you're reimbursed, you know, it's all controlled by
  people who are elected." And the complaint that "there are no pharmacists in
  Congress. Twenty-something doctors, a hundred something attorneys. You have
  two almond farmer ranchers."
- Carter's own account of why Club for Growth funded his opponent: as a state
  legislator he had worked on legislation limiting Big Pharma's ability to force
  pharmacists onto an industry price schedule. **"Anger over this legislative
  record, Carter said, made Big Pharma his enemy and led it to provide Club with
  the money it used against him in the primary."** Club for Growth declined to
  be interviewed. The authors flag this as their own inference: much of what
  "presents itself as ideological principle in House primaries — and in American
  politics more generally — is better understood as group interest dressed up in
  rhetorical garb."
- Ideology barely surfaced. Johnson's one extreme moment: "I'd rather see
  another terrorist attack, truly I would, than to give up my liberty as an
  American citizen." Retracted the next day — "I said something stupid." Carter's
  team made an attack ad about it and **pulled it within forty-eight hours**.
  "Neither candidate, it seemed, wanted to engage on the question of who was
  ideologically moderate or extreme."
- On agriculture in the same race: farmers back whoever "will be the most
  proactive for the industry," and want a congressman who will say "'You're not
  deporting our workers. We don't care what you have to say to the media or
  other Republicans, but these guys are gonna be here to pick the damn crops
  every time we need a crop picked.'"

**2. MA-5 special primary, October 2013 — "A Free-for-All of Massachusetts
Liberals."** The cleanest demonstration that ideology can be held constant.

All six candidates were, per sources, "Massachusetts liberals" who "would vote
the liberal line on nearly every issue." A leading party insider: "I'm not sure
there's any issues [in this election]." And yet **some five million dollars and
thousands of hours of volunteer time** went into it. What were they fighting
about? Group attachment:

| Candidate | Group base | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Katherine Clark (won) | women's issues | NARAL, Planned Parenthood in state races; Legislator of the Year 2013 (Women's Bar Association); EMILY's List indirect expenditure "nearly doubled Clark's funding for voter contact and communication in the final weeks" |
| Peter Koutoujian (2nd) | Armenian American | ANCA endorsed, organized a phone bank, helped fundraise; **~37% of his individual contributions came from persons with ethnically Armenian names**, most ending in "ian" — the authors note this may understate, because of women who changed names on marrying |
| Carl Sciortino (3rd) | LGBTQ | viral ad with his "curmudgeonly and endearing Tea Party father"; married his partner ten days before the election; LGBTQ Victory Fund; **47% of individual contributions from out of state**, by far the largest share in the race |
| Will Brownsberger (4th) | none | "an iconoclastic truth-teller"; "he's got this passionate following of people who really agree with that" |
| Karen Spilka | labor | many union endorsements, **$7,500 from union PACs**; "if Spilka was a labor candidate, she was not a strongly supported one" |

The ANCA press release quote is a good one for teaching what a group actually
does: "'We have a chance to elect someone who has the same story we all have;
who speaks not just as our friend, but as one of us,' explained ANCA National
Board Member Richard Sarajian . . . 'I have one task for you. I want you to call
every single friend, relative, or other person that you know in Peter
Koutoujian's district and get them out to vote.'"

The local blogger's formulation of what groups want is the chapter's thesis in
one sentence: "[Groups] want to know that they've got a candidate who's really
going to carry their water . . . and it's not so much how you vote on the bill
at the end of the day but how hard did you work to get that line item in the
bill that is going to benefit [our group] in a significant way." Sometimes
changes "imperceptible to the human eye . . . [can be] really important to a
particular group." A Massachusetts group leader, more simply: **"We look for
champions."**

**3. AL-1 and AL-6 — "Commitment and Competence in Two Alabama Districts."**
Both were establishment-vs-insurgent runoffs. The establishment won the First,
the insurgent the Sixth. The authors use the pair to show that groups back
*competence at advancing the agenda*, not factional loyalty as such.

*AL-1 (Mobile special, late 2013), Bradley Byrne (est.) v. Dean Young (ins.):*
- Business PACs gave Byrne about **$230,000** across primary and runoff; the US
  Chamber made an additional **independent expenditure of $200,000**. **"Not a
  single business PAC contributed to Young."**
- Runoff spending: **Byrne ~$835,000 v. Young ~$125,000.**
- Byrne won the runoff with **52.5 percent** — "a much closer margin than would
  be expected from the enormous spending difference."
- The national Chamber's involvement was initiated by Alabama business leaders,
  not Byrne. And House Majority Leader **Eric Cantor personally worked the
  PACs**: "Eric got directly involved with his political team and literally went
  to some of the national [business] PACs and said, 'I know you don't usually do
  this, but we think this race is so important' . . . a lot of those PACs that
  gave money to me in the runoff I never talked to, I never even met."
- Young's complaint that the insurgent groups never came: "Where are y'all? . . .
  We've got the people; we just don't have the resources [Byrne has] with
  establishment groups." Club for Growth said it was watching; FreedomWorks
  never waded in. **The authors' verdict: "their reluctance to accept Young
  emerges as the pivotal factor in this intraparty showdown."**
- Even a Ted Cruz-aligned PAC (Stop Spending Now) backed Byrne: "It's a great
  talking point to say you'll be like Ted Cruz. But Ted Cruz is a very smart, a
  very thoughtful person. And we think Bradley Byrne is better on all of these
  points."
- Press framing was entirely factional and the authors say the substance was
  not: the AP reported the two had "virtually identical positions on the
  high-profile issues at the core of Capitol Hill dysfunction"; *Mother Jones*
  called the contrast "largely cosmetic."

*AL-6 (Birmingham, spring 2014), Paul DeMarco (est.) v. Gary Palmer (ins.):*
- Chad Mathis entered with Club for Growth support before the incumbent
  announced retirement — "It is quite possible that the incumbent resigned only
  because a Club-backed candidate was coming after him." Mathis finished fourth.
- Scott Beason, sponsor of a take-your-guns-to-work law and an anti-immigration
  bill, "both anathema to business," finished third and **missed the runoff by
  4,200 votes**.
- Palmer ran the Alabama Policy Institute — nine full-time experts, "a respected
  policy advisor to the poorly staffed Alabama state legislature." He had
  studied with James Dobson in California in the 1980s.
- Corporate PACs: **six gave Palmer ~$40,000; fifty gave DeMarco $170,000.**
- Club for Growth's endorsement was worth **~$300,000 by the authors' estimate**
  — but "most of this money, however, arrived late in the runoff campaign,
  **after polls indicated that Palmer had pulled into a solid lead.**"
- The national Chamber, which had gone all-in for Byrne in AL-1, "did indeed
  leave DeMarco to fend for himself." Palmer outraised DeMarco in the runoff and
  **won with 64 percent**.
- The interpretation, which is the chapter's sharpest analytic claim: both Young
  and Palmer were anti-establishment and would have been reliable votes for
  leadership change. But factional PACs "gave Young the cold shoulder in a race
  where they could probably have enabled him to win," and gave heavily to Palmer
  "in a race that he was winning without them." Why? "Young seemed to care more
  about C-SPAN performances than policy work, whereas Palmer had decades of
  experience as an expert advocate." Therefore **"the metric on which candidates
  compete in House primaries is not general ideological position but the ability
  to work effectively in Washington for the agendas . . . that groups want."**
- Young's own style, quoted: if elected, listeners should get "a big ole thing
  of popcorn and a big Super Gulp and lean back and turn on C-SPAN."

**4. MI-4 — "Friendship and Hometown Loyalty."** The case where no policy demand
appears at all.

- Paul Mitchell, self-financing outsider, spent much of a **$3.6 million** budget
  attacking State Senator John Moolenaar as a career politician and "secret
  supporter of Obamacare."
- Moolenaar had the Michigan State Chamber of Commerce (**IE of $200,000**) and
  **Dow Chemical ($100,000)**, the largest employer in his home city of Midland
  — and still could raise only **$1.2 million**.
- Four weeks out, the first public poll had **Mitchell 50, Moolenaar 27**. The
  result was **Moolenaar 53, Mitchell 36**, third candidate 11. A 23-point
  deficit became a 17-point win against three-to-one spending.
- The explanation four sources gave: **Bill Schuette**, who had represented
  Midland for thirty years and was then state attorney general. A journalist:
  "Mitchell was an outsider billionaire from Saginaw who decided 'I want to come
  in and buy this nomination.' And basically Schuette said, 'I'm not going to
  allow it to happen.' And he picked up Moolenaar by the scruff of the neck,
  carried him across the finish line, plunked him down in the nomination."
- What the machine was: "Party chairs, the district chair, the inner party
  mechanism of who controls the grassroots and the activists. They don't exert
  real control like Tammany Hall did, it's more they know who the activists are,
  they know who to put in different places, they know the system and who to talk
  to to get things done."
- And it ran on friendship: "I would call up my friend that I've known for years
  and say 'Hey, I need you to get some friends together and go knock on some
  doors on Saturday. If we do that I'll buy pizza and beer.' . . . **Pizza and
  beer and friendship can get a lot of things done in politics.**"
- Total spending around **$5 million**, "one of the more expensive in our study."
- Midland is about **10 percent** of the district.
- The authors are careful: the group here is an activist community, and "we found
  little indication that Mitchell's views were much different." The operative
  reasons were "social solidarity, the interests of the Midland section of the
  district, and known quantities." A source: "These things become as much social
  as political or ideological."

**5. WA-4 — "A Culture of Cooperation."** An all-party (top-two) primary, twelve
candidates, two Republicans dominating.

**Methodological flag, stated in the book:** "(Unlike most source statements in
this book, those in this section have been reconstructed from notes made in
unrecorded phone interviews. Except for short phrases that were transcribed
exactly, statements are therefore not placed in quotation marks.)"

- Clint Didier (Tea Party, former professional footballer) v. Dan Newhouse
  (establishment, veteran legislator and former state director of agriculture).
  Both farmers.
- Didier finished **ahead** in the first stage; Newhouse won the general-election
  runoff **by 2,465 votes out of 153,079 cast**. Stated elsewhere as **30 to 27
  percent** in the primary and **50.8 to 49.2** in the runoff.
- Farm money: **~$500,000 of Newhouse's ~$1.1 million**; **~$75,000 of Didier's
  $360,000** — "a margin of nearly five to one."
- Why farmers would not go to Didier despite liking his style: "If you are a hop
  or tree fruit grower or a dairy farmer, you need immigrant labor. You could
  not be in sympathy with Didier on immigration."
- Decisions are made by "civic leaders" — business, the hospital board, the trade
  fair, Rotary, the Chamber — and "not by formal decision or resolution. It's
  more like people talking all the time."
- **The poker group.** Bruce Smith, a weekly business journal editor, led an
  informal group of fifteen current and former Republican officeholders who
  recruited, financed and managed campaigns — "a soup to nuts political
  operation." Monthly poker night: an email goes out, nine or ten respond, "the
  event would consist of winning and losing $20 worth of chips, with discussion
  of politics running on the side."
- The single best line in the chapter, from Jon DeVaney, asked whether Smith
  counted as one of the civic leaders: **no — "Smith's group was a shadow
  political party, not a general civic group."**
- The authors' own discussion of this case is unusually candid about limits.
  Didier nearly won anyway, and they say so: "That Didier nearly won despite
  Newhouse's support from the district's leading policy demanders points up the
  limits of their influence. If, as we argue in this book, policy demanders
  managed to get their way in most nominations, it may have been less because
  they had an iron grip on politics than because **most nomination contests fail
  to engage the attention of most voters.**" They also concede this is the case
  where voters *did* know the difference: "With two starkly different candidates
  leading the Fourth District contest, the media coverage we observed was clear
  about their differences."

**6. CA-31 — "National Party Intervention in an All-Party Primary."** The case
about the DCCC.

- District: **49.3 percent Hispanic, 9.9 percent Black, 7.4 percent Asian
  American** — demographics that would ordinarily elect a Democrat.
- **2012 disaster:** four Democrats and two Republicans ran in the first top-two
  primary. Pete Aguilar was the top Democrat at **22.6 percent**, behind two
  Republicans at **26.7 and 24.8**. Result: a Republican-v.-Republican general,
  won by Gary Miller.
- The DCCC put CA-31 atop its 2014 target list and recruited Aguilar hard. A
  local activist described an early breakfast: "It was to throw our support to
  him. This was real early in the process . . . before anybody had declared . . .
  All of the presidents of the Democratic clubs stood up and said, 'I'm behind
  Pete and I'm going to do this or that.' And all the labor union
  representatives did the same thing. . . . **But unfortunately, it didn't have
  the effect we wanted.**"
- Two weeks after Aguilar declared, former member **Joe Baca** entered anyway.
  Baca's problems were with the party, not the electorate: he had endorsed the
  Republican Gary Miller in 2012; he had paid only **$12,000 of the $400,000** in
  dues he owed the DCCC over his final four years; Bloomberg's Independence USA
  PAC had spent **$3 million** against him in 2012 over gun control; and he had
  a history of insults aimed at Latina politicians. Activists were "uniformly
  negative."
- **EMILY's List recruited Eloise Gomez Reyes** into the race, against the
  DCCC's wishes. Reyes: "If Emily's List had not spoken with me at the beginning
  of this campaign, I would not be in the race." She ran a **$1.17 million**
  campaign and outraised both Aguilar and Miller in the last quarter of 2013.
- The DCCC's counter-move was to attack the *second-place Republican*, "knocking
  her down a notch and moving Aguilar up to second place in the primary
  balloting." Aguilar then won the general **52 to 48**.
- **The margin: Aguilar's second-place finish was 206 votes ahead of the
  third-place candidate, a Republican.** And a 25-year-old with no experience,
  Ryan Dowling — dismissed by a Republican consultant as doing this "as kind of
  an 'Eat, Pray, Love' thing" — took **1,737 votes**, finishing fifth. Had those
  votes split between the other two Republicans, "it would have been bad news
  for Aguilar."
- The EMILY's List quote that makes the faction-not-ideology point: asked why
  the group risked losing the seat, a knowledgeable source said "[EMILYs List's]
  mission is not for Democrats to control Congress. [Their] mission is to get to
  **50 percent of the Congressional Democratic caucus**."
- The authors note the DCCC's choice was itself *anti*-median-voter: CA-31 was
  only D+5, so a pure electability logic implied backing Baca, whose anti-gun
  control positions would have played well with centrists. "Nobody wants you to
  take their guns away around here, even the Democrats." They chose Aguilar to
  keep local clubs and EMILY's List on side.

### Conclusion, verbatim

> This chapter's evidence suggests that the groups sponsoring candidates in
> House primaries do not typically choose to support them based on their
> ideological positions but on their commitment to the groups' particular
> agendas and their ability to work effectively for those agendas in
> Washington. Even in cases in which groups present themselves as advocates of
> principle rather than interests, a look beneath the surface suggests a good
> deal of interest-based policy demand.

### For the corpus

Three things here are buildable and one is a warning.

**The champion-versus-ally ratio is a real measurable.** Rouzer $288,000 against
Berger $20,000; Newhouse's farm money against Didier's at five to one; Carter's
44 percent from pharmacy. All of these are FEC contribution data classified by
industry, which the corpus already touches in `campaign-finance` and
`finance-source`. A chapter on *concentration of a candidate's money in one
industry* — the share of a candidate's receipts from a single sector — would use
data the corpus already knows how to get, and the book supplies the interpretive
frame and the vocabulary. The Koutoujian figure (37 percent of individual
contributions from Armenian surnames) is a **surname-inference** finding, which
the corpus already teaches in `surnames` and `bisg-check`; note the authors flag
its own bias in the same sentence, which is exactly the corpus's ethic.

**The book's own counts are citable.** 35 Republican contests, 27 with an
insurgent, 12 insurgent wins. 55 contests, 346 interviews. These are small
enough to print and specific enough to check against FEC files.

**The AL-6 timing finding is the sharpest empirical claim in the chapter and it
is a data question.** Club for Growth's ~$300,000 arrived "after polls indicated
that Palmer had pulled into a solid lead." Independent expenditures carry
**filing dates**, and the corpus already has an `independent-expenditures`
chapter built on FEC bulk data. Whether outside money arrives before or after a
candidate is already winning is answerable from the public record, at scale, for
every race — not just this one. That is the single most promising chapter idea
in this reading.

**The warning:** the WA-4 section's quotes are reconstructed from notes, not
recordings, and the book says so in a parenthesis. If any corpus chapter ever
quotes this book, that parenthesis has to travel with the quote.

## Chapter 4. Vetting and Vouching (pp. 82–118)

> **Proposition 2.** *Policy demanders have developed practices of vetting and
> vouching that enable them to base support decisions on observed candidate
> behavior rather than news stories and candidate self-statements.*

This is the book's most original chapter and the one with the most usable data.
It opens with a state party chair asked how much fundraising is "cold calls":

> [SOURCE:] Well, none of it would be cold, ever. . . . There would have to be
> some kind of relationship from somebody, some referral or something. You would
> never just say, oh this guy's an executive at US Bank, call him. You would just
> never do that . . . There's no percentage in cold calls, probably a lower
> percentage than selling siding.

The authors concede "there is exaggeration in this response" but keep the point.

### The three definitions

Recorded verbatim because a corpus chapter using this vocabulary needs them
exact.

- **Vetting** — "the production of reliable candidate information." Methods:
  interviewing candidates; questionnaires; tests of knowledge of the supporter's
  preferences; **hiring professional investigators**; interviewing people with
  personal experience of the candidate; and drawing on one's own experience.
- **Vouching** — "a credible avowal of support, one for which the endorser may
  suffer embarrassment, loss of status, or even loss of income if the candidate
  underperforms." The cost is what makes it credible.
- **Network** — "a group of individuals who share an important interest or value
  and who regularly communicate." May be "as large as the US Chamber of Commerce
  or as small as a friendship circle." Communication "might be a monthly email,
  annual report, or chatting with friends at church or on social media."

Together: **vetting-and-vouching networks, abbreviated VVNs** throughout.

### What a "good type" is: the three C's

A good candidate is **committed** to the demander's interests, **competent** to
pursue them in Congress, and **competitive** enough to win. The first two are
ordinary principal-agent traits. The third "is unique to electoral settings" and
is *contextual* — it depends on who else is running and on the rules.

The worked example is PA-13: three strongly pro-choice candidates from suburban
Montgomery County split their natural base, while Brendan Boyle had Philadelphia
to himself. **"Boyle's advantage would have been neutralized if Pennsylvania
election law required runoffs when first-place candidates fall short of winning
a majority."** Competitiveness is partly a property of the electoral system.

### Why the press cannot do this job

The authors are blunt that newspapers fail at candidate information, and they
promise systematic evidence: "as our examination of newspaper coverage in
winnable open-seat primaries in 2013–2014 will show, most fail at this task."

The illustration: in one Democratic primary, a candidate who had led
conservative efforts to block progressive legislation in the state assembly
"nonetheless called herself a 'true American progressive' in TV ads and went
unchallenged on this point in the **three newspapers we examined**."

### The regulatory reason VVNs matter

- 2014 limits: **$2,600 per individual donor per election, $5,000 per PAC.**
- *Citizens United* (2010) weakened limits, "but in the nominations we examined,
  most candidates raised most of their money in amounts limited to $2,600 or
  $5,000 per election."
- Therefore raising a large sum means assembling many small increments, which is
  "vastly easier for candidates who tap into vetting-and-vouching networks."

### The honest limitation, stated by the authors

> Where vetting-and-vouching networks fall short is in identifying candidates
> committed to the **broader public good**. While they may help weed out
> incompetents, charlatans, thieves, and others who serve no reputable interest
> at all, the value of these networks mostly accrues to their members and to
> candidates committed to their views.

And the connection to the book's central question: primaries "have fallen short
in giving each voter an equal voice. One main reason is VVNs. Members of these
networks not only have more clout because of their contributions of money and
labor, they also possess better information about which candidates will
represent them best."

### LA-6: the fully documented case

Three Republicans, and a figure showing their money over time.

- **Paul Dietzel**, 28, entrepreneur, grandson and namesake of a famous LSU
  football coach. Began fundraising **May 2013, eighteen months before the
  primary**. No electoral experience. Ran on "relentless effort" — worked lists
  of donors to previous campaigns, "calling, visiting, and buttonholing everyone
  he could." Two donors were "unfavorably impressed by Dietzel's lack of
  political accomplishment."
- **Dan Claitor**, three-term state senator, entered January 2014. Described as
  one of the hardest-working legislators, an independent thinker who read every
  bill — and "pushing good ideas that everyone recognizes as good ideas" despite
  being politically infeasible. "This strategy was not one that would be expected
  to attract major patrons, and indeed Claitor's fundraising did not go well."
  Wound up making many cold calls.
- **Garret Graves**, entered **late March 2014**. Fifteen years as legislative
  staff for Louisiana members of Congress, specializing in flood control, energy,
  river transportation. Chaired the Coastal Protection and Restoration Authority
  under Jindal; oversaw the state coastal master plan and the BP spill cleanup.

**Figure 4.1, "Pace of fundraising in LA-6"** — cumulative total fundraising,
June 2013 to December 2014, three lines. Graves enters last and "shot ahead
almost immediately, raising more money in two months than Dietzel had in a
year." By the November primary Graves had **more than twice Dietzel's money**.
He topped the Republican field and won the December runoff.

The maritime money, by the authors' own classification of FEC data:

| | Graves | Dietzel | Claitor |
|---|---|---|---|
| Business PACs in the maritime zone | ~$69,000 | **none** | ~$3,000 |
| Individuals working for such companies | ~$570,000 | ~$120,000 | ~$25,000 |
| **Maritime share of all money raised** | **56%** | **30%** | **6%** |

**Lane Grigsby**, semi-retired CEO of a maritime construction firm, is the
chapter's model network leader. *Times-Picayune* data journalists "found
Grigsby's network of donors to be the third largest in Louisiana, just behind
the state Democratic Party and just ahead of the state Republican Party." He
estimated he could raise **up to $250,000 from family and business associates,
and another $250,000 from other connections**.

His vetting was firsthand and adversarial. He had already rejected Dietzel
("really not worthwhile") and Claitor ("too old and lacking in energy"), and
had nearly backed a fourth candidate "but before committing himself he
**commissioned research that turned up a disqualifying skeleton**." On Graves,
whose executives believed he had blundered in work with the company, Grigsby
arranged a meeting and said beforehand:

> I'm gonna tell you, everybody makes mistakes, everybody can't get things
> perfect. When you own your mistakes is when I like you. It's when you deny 'em,
> and you're absolutely assuming I'm stupid . . . then you and I probably can't
> get along too well.

The meeting ran an hour: a third on Graves's plan for winning the primary, a
third on national issues such as abortion, a third on Grigsby's business.
Graves admitted error and named the subordinates he had fired; the executives
"went back and checked up on his answers . . . They came back later and said,
'You know, he did fire that guy.'" Grigsby then told his executives he was
supporting Graves and that others could leave checks on his secretary's desk.
The secretary collected **about $100,000** — "(Recall that individual
contributions were limited to $2,600 each.)"

Grigsby's ask, verbatim: "Hey, you know, your income stream is a function of how
well we do as a business, my engagement in the community helps our business, I
would ask you to make a personal sacrifice."

The authors then check themselves: Grigsby "was not a dominant contributor."
Koch Industries, the Louisiana River Pilots Association, Ecolab and a Shell
employees' PAC were also there, and "three dozen other maritime donors may have
had their own screening and money-collection procedures." A Republican source
described the looser dynamic: "Nobody comes to 'em . . . and says, 'Alright,
we're all gonna back [this candidate].' It's more or less . . . 'We know who's
helped us,' and so, 'Who you backin'?' 'Well, I'm backin',' 'Well, who're you
backin',' 'Yeah, I guess so.' So it kind of evolves."

### Union vetting

- Union membership **fell from 31 percent of the workforce at its 1954 peak to
  10 percent nationwide in 2014.**
- Unions "played some role in most of the twenty Democratic nominations in our
  study and a **leading role in the campaigns of nine candidates, of whom six won
  nomination**."

**IA-1, the Iowa Labor Council convention** — the most procedurally explicit
vetting anywhere in the book:

> We did a random drawing to determine the order of speaking. And they
> [candidates] went into the room one at a time. None of their staff could go
> into the room to see how the other candidates responded. Everybody got treated
> exactly the same. Everybody got exactly the same questions, exactly the same
> time period. . . . So it was an apples-apples comparison.

**Only three questions**, each asking the candidate to explain how they would
handle a labor problem or scenario. Endorsement required **two-thirds of
delegates**. Pat Murphy — former state House speaker — had failed to clear that
threshold before but did in April 2014. The source's scorecard: Kajtazovic "did
about what people thought she was going to do . . . hasn't floor-managed bills";
"Dandekar, it wasn't good"; "Monica Vernon did a fair job. O'Brien was good, and
**Murphy was lights-out the best candidate**."

The authors' own caveat, which is the honest one: "It's worth noting that this
formal vetting procedure was based on candidates' **words rather than actions**."
And their conclusion: "we observed no instance in which union endorsed
candidates based on words alone — **deeds**, in the form of records of effective
union support, drove union support decisions."

Also: "Endorsements without work behind them are fairly meaningless."

### Evangelical vetting

- "Faith-based actors were an important force in **seven Republican nominations**
  and were on the winning side in **five**."
- **GA-10, Rev. Jody Hice.** "[B]etween the primary and the runoff and the
  general [the Hice campaign] did **289,000 doors and dials** . . . I just can't
  express to you how — *that's a lot of voter contact* . . . [that's] live doors
  and live dials, that's not robocalls, that's people talking to people."
  Volunteers came from Ten Commandments, Inc. and from the church where Hice had
  been senior pastor. No vetting needed: "It was very much like, 'We love Jody,
  so let's go do this.'"
- **IA-3, the "pastors' network."** Robert Cramer quit as president of the Iowa
  Family Leader to run. The group's political director, **Greg Baker**, took
  vacation leave to work for him as an individual. Baker built a network of
  **thirty ministers** "willing to engage their congregations." Churches "did not
  make endorsements, only their ministers."
  - How to build one where the candidate is unknown: get a recommendation from
    "one of Iowa's two or three most respected evangelical leaders," use it to
    get one-on-one meetings with local pastors, then impress them in
    conversation. "You might be able to get a couple of churches without them,
    but if you want a vibrant network you're going to want one of those [top]
    guys."
  - Result: Cramer finished second in a six-candidate primary with **9,032 votes
    out of 42,500 cast**. Baker's own estimate: one in six Iowa voters is
    religiously motivated, and about **30 percent** of those may have been
    influenced by the pastors' network — "about **24 percent of Cramer's overall
    vote total**." The authors: "We are in no position to judge the accuracy of
    Baker's estimate, but it suggests the potential size of a
    vetting-and-vouching network in the eyes of an engaged professional."
- **The counter-case: a network that declined to vouch.** Bill Gustoff, a
  registered lobbyist for Iowa home schoolers and a member of the state assembly,
  was positioned to advise the **two to three thousand parents** he estimated
  voted in Third District primaries. He wrote a section of the group's newsletter
  that could have carried a recommendation and **did not make one**. He had a
  personal preference and "shared it only with friends." The only case in which
  he would recommend: "if one of the candidates was a notably bad choice for his
  group." The authors' reading: preserving a relationship with whoever wins, and
  "sounding alarms when danger lurks may be as much vetting as homeschoolers need
  in a setting as friendly as the Iowa GOP."

### PACs and Super PACs — the numbers

Definitions as the book gives them: a traditional PAC donates directly, limited
to **$5,000 per election**; a Super PAC takes unlimited money but "cannot
contribute directly to candidates; they must instead make *independent
expenditures*."

"The big players were individual Super PACs that spent **$100,000 or more** —
sometimes much more — on **thirty-three candidates** in our study."

Broken down by group interest:

- **US Chamber of Commerce** — six-figure IEs in **two** races; one was $200,000
  for Bradley Byrne in AL-1.
- **Insurgent conservative Super PACs** — six-figure expenditures for **seven**
  candidates, **three** of whom won. Club for Growth spent **>$300,000** in AL-6
  for Gary Palmer.
- **Super PACs aligned with core Democratic groups** — six-figure in **twelve**
  races. EMILY's List supported **five** women with >$100,000 each, **three** won.
  An IBEW-affiliated PAC spent **>$300,000** for Brendan Boyle in PA-13.
- **Professional associations** — six-figure for **three** candidates, **one**
  won. The American Society of Anesthesiologists spent **~$300,000** in PA-13 for
  Valerie Arkoosh, who **finished fourth**.
- **Six additional Super PACs supported only a single candidate each**, of which
  **two** won. Example: "Campaign for Jobs and Opportunity" spent **>$500,000**
  on advertising for John Moolenaar alone in MI-4. Its top donors were the
  **Republican Attorneys General Association ($155,000)** and **Dow Chemical
  ($100,000)**.

**TABLE 4.1. Share of Super PAC spending in fifty-three winnable open-seat
primaries** — transcribed exactly:

| | All candidates | Nominees | Other candidates |
|---|---|---|---|
| Candidate spending | $109,276,078 | $41,692,826 | $67,583,258 |
| Super PAC spending | $12,731,689 | $7,840,128 | $4,891,562 |
| Super PAC share of spending | 10.4% | 15.8% | 6.7% |
| N | 264 | 53 | 211 |

The authors' reading of their own table: "The ability of Super PACs to make
unlimited expenditures makes them seem fearsome. But the above information
suggests that, at least in our set of open House primaries, they were **not
juggernauts but merely important players**."

### How national PACs vet from a distance

- **EMILY's List** "compensates for lack of direct candidate experience by
  maintaining networks of informed sources around the country . . . not just one
  local source but several. EL might also consider **as many as eight or ten
  potential candidates before settling on one to back — or declining to back
  any**."
- EL "was adamant that its candidates support women's choice on abortion but did
  not insist on across-the-board liberalism." It endorsed **Donna Kim in HI-1**,
  who "was not only economically conservative but had voted against same-sex
  marriage in the state legislature."
- EL "may have vetted candidates as much on campaign strategy as on abortion." It
  insisted candidates follow its fundraising program, **"Raising the Dough."**
  Two sources said EL declined a candidate "largely because she insisted on
  adhering to her own, previously successful approach to fundraising."
- **Club for Growth** required a **term limits pledge**; one conservative
  candidate who believed he had passed Club's economics tests was not supported
  because he would not sign it.
- The cautionary tale about not understanding the system: a candidate "flew to
  Washington to solicit contributions from national PACs associated with her
  party, hired a hall for a fundraiser, and sent out invitations for the
  following week." The source "laughed out loud at this. 'You can't just invite
  [these people] and suddenly think they're going to show up . . . [They]
  actually need to know you!'" The standard procedure requires **two personal
  relationships** — candidate to local informant, local informant to PAC.

### "Warm calls"

A terminological proposal worth noting: calls to individuals the candidate has
never met, but who belong to groups whose leaders have endorsed, are not really
cold. "***Warm* calls might be a better term. Future research might fruitfully
investigate what proportion of candidate fundraising calls are of each type and
what their relative yields are."

### Party vetting, personal networks, professional vetting

- **MI-12, Debbie Dingell** — the pure firsthand case. Thirty-three years in the
  political spotlight before running. "She's not someone who comes to a
  Democratic Party meeting and sits in the back of the room and nobody is going
  to notice her. She's going to be front and center." Endorsements arrived so
  fast they demonstrated "to all that she would be all but impossible to defeat."
- **Northern Louisiana, Paul Dickson** — a party fundraiser whose network was
  "about twenty self-interested professional groups, each with its own internal
  pecking order." He cultivated each group's natural leader as a **"bell cow"**
  whose name on an invitation brings the others. Recruits by talking to people at
  cocktail parties. His account of donor motivation: "Everybody is motivated by
  what's going to promote your own well-being. The doctors are gonna be motivated
  by medical issues and health care issues. Oil and gas owners are gonna be
  motivated by both environmental and taxation issues."
- **The dinner party.** A retired self-made millionaire hosts; guests "make the
  commitment that they're gonna write a check before they come to the dinner and
  they've never met the person. They really rely on the host." The host: "You
  don't want to be recommending somebody to your friends who you don't know
  anything about! You check 'em out." Two dinners raised **as much as $40,000**.
  The host described it as "a huge risk because I used all my political capital
  to get everybody I knew to back [my candidate]."
- **Big donors as a leading indicator.** A party actor: "once people understand
  that so-and-so and so-and-so are supporting X, other people will go along with
  that." And the forecasting claim: "**You could make good early guesses about
  who's going to raise the most money by that** [who the big donors support early
  on]."
- **Professional vetters** — consultants, lobbyists, bundlers. A Hawaii lobbyist,
  on the record, asked where candidates get money: "From me, mostly. They all do.
  People like me, lobbyists." On what he gets: "I do get access." On whether
  candidates would vote differently: "Everybody I support is a Democrat, and a
  liberal Democrat, and a union Democrat . . . I wouldn't expect them to go into
  the legislature and suddenly become Blue Dog Democrats." The authors note that
  over **a score of interviews** in that race, "no other source mentioned
  lobbyists as important players."
- **A fundraising consultant** with ~100 donors who give $1,000 or more. Her
  vetting of candidates: "I ask very pointed questions, I ask about their
  marriage, I ask about their children . . . before I agree to work with
  somebody." Sixty to seventy hours a week in peak season.
- **Mimi Walters (CA-45)**, interviewed at length on the record in 2022 after
  leaving office. She raised **about 40 percent** of her 2014 primary funds from
  "pretty much cold calls" to lists supplied by professional fundraisers. She
  hired **four separate fundraisers**, one each for Orange, San Diego, Los
  Angeles and Sacramento. The exchange about "Ann":

  > [WALTERS] . . . if you have a really good fundraiser [she] has developed
  > relationships with these donors over the years . . . So if that donor will
  > say, "Gee, Ann, I don't know. What do you think about Mimi? What is she
  > really like? I don't know if I should give to her." And Ann would say, "You
  > know what, I think she's a great gal, I think you would like her." And
  > they'd say, "OK."
  > [POG] And Ann is the person you hire?
  > [WALTERS] Right. And Ann is my person.

  And on the fundraiser's incentive: "it's their credibility on the line with
  their donors. When they're interviewing me [they're like] 'Do I think she's a
  viable candidate? Do I think she's gonna win? Do I like her or is she a
  bitch?'"
- **Personal networks.** A Republican consultant estimated that personal
  networks generate **60 to 70 percent of local contributions** to candidates in
  House primaries in his southern state. The mechanism: "If I'm in the
  construction business and I've got three electricians that bid on all my stuff
  . . . the guy you buy plywood from . . . those are the people who come to your
  kid's graduation, they come to their birthday parties . . . that's the network
  you build out for the political donations . . . necessarily because of the
  [$2,600 contribution] cap."
  - Crucially, personal networks **do not require vouching for competence**: the
    accountant attending a client's fundraiser "need not care at all about the
    candidate's competence and commitment." Cites Bonica (2017). "Personal
    networks can extend the impact of a vouching network well beyond its core
    policy demand."

### The prevalence estimate, and an inconsistency

The chapter's summary estimate, with an unusually frank admission:

> **Because we were not attuned to the importance of vetting until late in our
> exploratory work**, we can only roughly estimate the relative frequency of the
> two types of vetting in candidate screening and can do so only for the winning
> candidates — that is, the fifty-three nominees who competed in primaries. Our
> estimate is that firsthand vetting may have been the most important form of
> candidate screening by core supporters in about **a quarter** of the fifty-five
> nominations. . . . For another large group of cases, **perhaps half** of all
> nominations, VVNs were the most important vehicle. . . . For the **remaining
> quarter**, firsthand vetting and VVNs both played large roles.

Examples given for each: firsthand — Debbie Dingell (MI-12); VVN — Mimi Walters
(CA-45) and Norma Torres (CA-35); both — Katherine Clark (MA-5).

**Flag:** the sentence says the estimate can be made "only for . . . the
fifty-three nominees" and then states it as a share "of the fifty-five
nominations." The book uses 53 and 55 in adjacent clauses. Table 4.1 is headed
"fifty-three winnable open-seat primaries" with N = 53 nominees, while the
Preface and chapter 3 say fifty-five contests. The likeliest reconciliation is
that two of the 55 nominations had no contested primary, so 53 nominees
"competed in primaries" — but the book does not say so here, and anyone citing
either number should say which.

### George Santos as the negative case

The chapter closes on 2022. Santos sought the nomination in a Democratic-leaning
New York district: "Because no other Republican candidate was interested in the
race, **no one seems to have looked carefully at the false credentials Santos
claimed for himself.**" The *New York Times* investigation ran six weeks after
he won, headlined "Who Is Rep. Elect George Santos? His Résumé May Be Largely
Fiction."

The data point: "**Federal Election Commission records show that Santos received
just one contribution from a business PAC, but more than $100,000 from several
dozen politically oriented PACs**, including the Majority Committee PAC of the
Republican Party in Congress."

The closing claim: "A system of vetting the qualifications of candidates for
office is not an incidental but an **essential feature of the party system**."

### For the corpus

**Table 4.1 is the single most reusable object in the book.** It is a
four-column table with an N, built from FEC data, and every cell is checkable
against public bulk files. $109m candidate spending against $12.7m Super PAC
spending across 264 candidates — and the finding that Super PACs are "not
juggernauts" at 10.4 percent of spending — is a genuinely counterintuitive
number that runs against how outside money is usually described. A chapter that
reproduced this table from FEC data for a *later* cycle would be squarely in the
corpus's idiom: same question, public file, arithmetic the reader can follow.

**The 56/30/6 percent maritime shares are the same measurable I flagged in
chapter 3** — concentration of a candidate's receipts in one sector. Chapter 4
gives it a second worked example and, in Figure 4.1, the *timing* dimension:
Graves entered last and passed everyone in two months. FEC filings are dated, so
"cumulative fundraising by date, by candidate" is directly reproducible. That
figure is a good model for a corpus chart.

**The "warm calls" question is an open research question the authors explicitly
hand off** — "Future research might fruitfully investigate what proportion of
candidate fundraising calls are of each type." That cannot be answered from
public data, and saying so is itself a teachable point about what disclosure
does and does not capture.

**Santos is the corpus's kind of case.** One contribution from a business PAC,
$100,000+ from party-aligned PACs, and a résumé nobody checked. The FEC file
recorded every dollar correctly and recorded nothing at all about whether the
man existed as described. That is the gap between a disclosure record and a
vetting record, and `finance-source` already argues something close to it.

**A caution for any chapter that cites the vetting prevalence estimate:** the
authors say plainly they were not paying attention to vetting until late in the
fieldwork, and that the quarter/half/quarter split is rough and covers winners
only. That admission should travel with the number.

## Chapter 5. Coordination (pp. 119–162)

> **Proposition 3.** *Policy-demanding groups often coordinate across group
> boundaries to increase their clout in the nomination process.*

The longest chapter in the book, and the one that ends in a countable claim:
coordination occurred in **thirty-two of fifty-five** nominations. It opens on
Schattschneider (1960, 35) — "the organized beat the unorganized" — which the
authors say "aptly describes the system of House nominations we observed."

**Note a small internal drift.** The chapter's opening paragraph says
coordination "was present in about **60 percent**" of the nominations; Table 5.2
and the closing text give **32 of 55, or 58 percent**. Use the table.

### The definition, which is unusually strict

Recorded verbatim because the chapter's headline count depends entirely on it:

> *Coordination*, as we will use the term, occurs when party-linked actors with
> different interests or affiliations cooperate in support of the same
> candidate. The mere fact of these actors supporting the same candidate is not,
> in our account, sufficient to qualify as coordination. **The impetus for
> coordination must come from the policy demanders, not the candidate** who
> benefits from the coordination. The policy demanders must agree to support the
> same candidate through some sort of definite, often personal, interaction.

The exclusion is sharp and worth keeping: if an association of electrical
contractors in LA-6 saw Lane Grigsby backing Garret Graves and independently
decided to follow because Grigsby has good judgment, **that does not count**.
"But if (as was the case) the contractors had worked out an agreement with
Grigsby to follow his lead in party nominations and did so in the case of
Graves, it would count as coordination."

- **Strategic coordination** — actors recognize their sincere first preference
  cannot win and band behind a mutually acceptable candidate.
- **Non-strategic coordination** — actors with no strong preferences make a joint
  decision to avoid undercutting one another; or pay too little attention and
  consult better-informed players; or belong to an organization that expects
  members to act with the group.

The methodological discipline is explicit, and it is the reason the count is
described as a floor: "It is easy to find evidence suggesting coordination in
many of our fifty-five nominations, perhaps too easy. . . . **We therefore take
care to distinguish active coordination from cases in which groups happen to
support the same candidate or have been recruited by the same candidate.**"

The worked contrast: Debbie Dingell (MI-12) had "virtually every political club,
union, and party organ in the district" endorse her, but no source attributed it
to a coordinated effort — so it is **not** counted. Rudy Hobbs (MI-14) had
similarly broad support, but "half a dozen sources told us that Hobbs's
widespread support was due to the support of Congressman Sandy Levin" — so it
**is** counted. "We might, despite these comments, still suspect a coordinated
decision from some actors to back Debbie Dingell, but absent any clear evidence,
we do not assume it."

### The four forms

1. **Local Party Organization** — endorsement and tangible support from a county
   committee.
2. **National Party Coordination** — endorsement by the DCCC or NRCC, which
   "function as cues to party supporters" (Hassell 2017, 2023).
3. **Informal Political Coordination** — "informal effort by actors with strong
   links to a party to organize the support of other party-linked actors."
   Broader than but including Masket's (2009) *informal party organization* (IPO).
4. **Group-led Coordination** — informal effort by actors with strong links to a
   *group* to organize other members of their group.

### The opening natural experiment: MI-13, 2018

The best-designed piece of evidence in the chapter, and it comes from **outside
the study frame** — the authors include it "for the light it sheds on a case that
does fall within our study frame, but we do not include it in the book's summary
statistics."

After the incumbent resigned in scandal, Michigan held **two Democratic primaries
on the same Tuesday in August 2018** — one for the partial term, one for the full
two-year term. **Four candidates were on the partial-term ballot; six on the
full-term ballot** — the same four plus two more. Same district, same day, same
voters, different field size. That is as close to a controlled comparison as
field research gets.

**TABLE 5.1. Impact of an uncoordinated field in MI-13** — transcribed exactly:

| | Partial term votes | Percent | Full term votes | Percent |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Brenda Jones | 32,769 | 37.80% | 26,941 | 30.20% |
| Rashida Tlaib | 31,121 | 35.90% | 27,841 | 31.20% |
| Bill Wild | 13,174 | 15.20% | 12,613 | 14.10% |
| Ian Conyers | 9,749 | 11.20% | 5,866 | 6.60% |
| Coleman Young II | — | — | 11,172 | 12.50% |
| Shanelle Jackson | — | — | 4,853 | 5.40% |
| **Totals** | **86,813** | **100%** | **89,286** | **100%** |

Brenda Jones was the establishment candidate (president of the Detroit City
Council, backed by party leaders and the automobile unions); Rashida Tlaib the
insurgent (Justice Democrats). **Jones won the partial term. Tlaib won the full
term** — "by far the greater prize, since it was for two whole years rather than
a few months."

The mechanism, assuming voters prefer co-ethnic candidates and can recognize
co-ethnic names: in the first primary African American voters divided between
**two** co-ethnic candidates (Jones, Conyers); in the second, among **four**
(Jones, Conyers, Young, Jackson). "It thus appears that Tlaib won the second
primary because its candidate field split the African American vote four ways
rather than two."

African American leaders had seen this coming. The district Democratic party's
African American chairman: "That is a very, very, very big concern among a lot of
folks that I talk to" — and he "organized an informal group of 'local policy
demanders' to coordinate support on a single candidate. **The effort came to
nothing.**"

**The companion case, IL-2 (2013).** A largely African American Chicago district
where **one white and sixteen Black candidates** sought the Democratic
nomination. The white candidate led an early poll as the only candidate of her
ethnic group. The district Democratic committee "would normally coordinate
support on a single candidate, but its members were too divided over the large
field to make an endorsement." Then: news stories reported Mayor Rahm Emanuel
working behind the scenes to persuade candidates to drop out; **nine days before
the election the second-place Black candidate dropped out**, sealing the
nomination for Robin Kelly. The *Cook Political Report* cited sources saying the
outcome "bore the fingerprints of Chicago Mayor Rahm Emanuel."

The three points the authors draw: coordination "may be difficult to achieve in
settings in which individual candidates make their own decisions about whether to
run," but "can be effective in managing field composition," and "may thereby
affect election outcomes."

And the generalization, which is the reason this matters beyond race: "there is
nothing special about primaries in which voters from a large group divide their
support among several candidates from their group and thereby make it possible
for a candidate from a smaller group to win. Primaries involving more than one
woman, union member, liberal, conservative, or Tea Partier can work in this way.
So may primaries in which two or more candidates run from the same county, city,
or media market. **Dependence of outcomes on how candidates divvy up votes was a
potential feature of any of the thirty-nine primaries in our study that had more
than two candidates.**"

### Local party organization

Context: "Some form of formal party organization existed in most of the **3,143
counties** in the United States at the time of our study." But most county
parties "exercised little power" — posting monitors, testifying, maintaining
voter lists. "Most party committees did not make endorsements in primaries for
federal offices, and even where they did become involved, their efforts were, as
Gary Jacobson has written, usually feeble." A county chair asked how much
influence he had: **none** — though "by speaking with candidates before they
formally declared their candidacies, he could sometimes shape the field despite
having no carrots or sticks."

The exceptions are the cases:

**PA-6R — winnowing the field to one.** *PoliticsPA* headlined the primary "wide
open" and named five interested candidates; **three days later** it reported that
"the field for the Republican nomination in Pennsylvania's 6th Congressional
District is shrinking rapidly." Ryan Costello was chair of the Board of
Commissioners of Chester County. **When the Chester County Republican Party
Committee met to interview candidates seeking its endorsement, Costello was the
only one who showed up.** He got the endorsement, was unopposed through the
primary, and won the general with 56 percent.

Three foundations of Chester's power, as sources described them:
- **An army of some four hundred campaign workers** who go door-to-door for
  endorsed candidates, "many of whom would be local government employees,
  contractors, and vendors whose livelihoods depend on GOP control of local
  offices."
- Financial support from business, which is "very loyal" to the committee — money
  goes to endorsed candidates and not to non-endorsed ones. Asked whether any
  business organization mattered in choosing nominees, a source said "I guess the
  county chamber is important, but I don't know how important." A second person
  listening in answered better: **"Most people stay out of the endorsement
  process and let us handle it."** The authors flag this division of labor —
  "the first chooses nominees and the second supports them" — as also present in
  Minnesota.
- **Chester covers 42 percent of the district's population.** "Other county chairs
  see that . . . [our] candidate is going to win whether they put up somebody or
  not. Bigger player running, that's what cleared the field."

How the endorsement was actually made: a leading member of the committee "related
it. He and Costello made the decision themselves. Both were interested in running
for Congress, but after a slightly heated discussion — our source initially
described it as a dustup, but then said this was too strong a word — they decided
that Costello would run."

*The Bucks County counter-episode (2010), included for what it reveals about
party boundaries.* Tea Party activists, "incensed at what they saw as the
domineering manner in which their Republican county committee was managing that
year's House primary," built a slate for county and state committee elections and
printed sample ballots to hand out at polling stations. **County constables
stopped them, confiscated their ballots on an injunction claiming violations of
state campaign laws, and asked them to leave.** Chairwoman Pat Poprik: **"This is
a party office. This is not a public office. This is our election. It is highly
unusual for people who have never done a thing for the party to run."** A Tea
Party activist: "I am not the enemy, I am not looking to overthrow them or
anything like that. . . . People like me in the Tea Party, why wouldn't you make
us your allies?" The authors' gloss: "A party is a coalition that needs broad
support in order to win elections, but it doesn't necessarily want to include
actors who might rock the boat."

**New Jersey and the "county line."** The rule matters more than anything else in
the chapter: endorsed candidates "appeared at that time in the first column of
the primary ballot as the 'county line.' Voters nearly always followed the county
line in House primaries, giving party committee leaders great power." And because
districts span multiple counties, **each committee controlled only the line its
own voters saw** — so coordination had to happen both within and across
committees.

*NJ-3R.* Background: in 2008 Ocean and Burlington counties each ran a candidate,
"exhausted themselves on intraparty warfare and lost to the Democrat"; in 2010
they coordinated and took the seat back. "The lesson from these two contests was
still fresh in Republican memories in 2014: Fight with each other and we lose to
the Democrats; work together and we win."

**Fifteen to seventeen would-be candidates** sought the two committees'
endorsement. The obvious choice was **Steve Lonegan** — he had lost the 2013
Senate special to Cory Booker 44–55 but had won that GOP primary 80–20, remained
popular with district Republicans, and was wealthy enough to self-fund. His
disqualifying flaw was principle: "he had opposed federal aid for victims of
Hurricane Sandy, which had damaged **forty thousand buildings in Ocean County**."
The source's reasoning was about county government, not Congress: "Who the
candidate for Congress is matters to us, and Steve Lonegan is too extreme . . .
everything comes down to the courthouse."

The committees endorsed Tom MacArthur. **"All the non-endorsed candidates then
promptly dropped out — except for Lonegan,"** who blasted the Ocean committee for
giving him "a mere three of 126 votes." What the line buys, per a party source:

> . . . instant grassroots volunteer base, structural support, endorsements from
> throughout all these towns. . . . immediately after you win the county line . . .
> the county chairman makes it clear that it's time to get to work . . . on day one
> they have a list of couple hundred people in each county they can start calling
> . . . so [the endorsement] comes with an apparatus, immediately. Whereas, someone
> like Lonegan needs to build his own outside of that.

MacArthur won 60–40. The authors' counterfactual is careful: the margin roughly
matched the spending difference, "but it is still worth asking: What if there had
been no Republican Party committees to support MacArthur? . . . Several of the
fifteen to seventeen candidates who initially expressed interest in the race would
probably have remained in it. If so, Lonegan might have faced multiple
establishment candidates rather than just MacArthur, which would have given him a
substantial chance of emerging as the plurality winner."

*NJ-12D — Bonnie Watson Coleman.* The chapter's best account of how convention
politics actually runs. BWC, 68, African American, had **decided to retire** when
she found "Run Bonnie Run" on her Facebook page one morning in February 2014.
"Within forty-eight hours she had jumped into the race." Two reasons given: a
telephone **"prayer call" of Black women** — "her girlfriends, as BWC described
them to an interviewer — who insisted that she needed to be in the race"; and a
Black campaign operative, **James Gee**, "who quickly assembled data indicating
that a Black woman could win in the majority-white district." Gee: "We started out
with her saying, 'I want to build respect for Black politics, and I don't want to
say I'm not running for this.' And then it moved to people rallying for her to do
it. Then it moved to, 'Hey, you look at the numbers and you could do this.'"

Four counties, four lines:

| County | Outcome |
|---|---|
| Middlesex | Linda Greenstein's home county. She won **81 percent** of roughly two hundred delegates to BWC's **8 percent**. The party's Black caucus leader "feared losing his patronage job, which he held at the discretion of the party chair, if he openly bucked the leadership by supporting Watson Coleman." |
| Somerset | Upendra Chivukula, of the county's significant South Asian community, won the endorsement **41–0**. |
| Mercer | BWC's home county, but Greenstein represented part of it and had union support there. The chair, who favored BWC, "appointed a neutral surrogate to run the convention and ordered the use of voting machines to ensure secret ballots." **A Mercer rule required 60 percent for sole possession of the line**; below that, first and second place would both appear. BWC got **~70 percent to Greenstein's 25**. |
| Union | No candidate was from Union. **Three African American party officials "were designated as the county endorsement convention and met for a restaurant dinner to decide the matter."** They chose BWC. |

Both campaigns "sought to stack the Mercer convention with their supporters
within the party organization. 'We knew where our supporters were,' said a BWC
campaign official. 'For us, it was making sure that they actually went and voted.
So it was almost like a mini-GOTV operation for the [party] committee members.'"
Gee said BWC attempted to contact **all four hundred** eligible attendees.

The result and the number worth keeping: BWC, Greenstein and Chivukula "each won
the primaries in the counties whose lines they had, while the fourth candidate,
with no county lines, carried no county. With two of the four county lines, BWC
won overall. **On average over the four counties, endorsed candidates beat their
closest rival by a margin of 67 percent to 18 percent.**" The authors immediately
hedge it: "This margin cannot be entirely explained as the influence of county
lines, since candidates won lines in counties in which they were strong to begin
with."

Who made up the winning Mercer coalition: African American sections of Trenton and
Ewing (first- and second-largest towns, but "well short of the 60 percent
threshold"), plus **Princeton Township**, "a center of wealthy white
progressives," brought in by the **Progressive Campaign Change Committee (PCCC)**,
plus AFSCME and CWA. The closing observation is the chapter's thesis in miniature:
"Absent the institution of the county line, these groups might have sponsored two
or three different candidates, but participating under the rules of New Jersey
politics they chose to aggregate — which is to say, coordinate — their energies
through their local Democratic Party committees."

*VA-10R — choosing the rules.* Barbara Comstock (establishment, Fairfax) versus
Richard Black (Loudoun, "known for a conservative grass-roots following and a
history of inflammatory remarks about social issues"). **Black dropped out two
days after officially launching**, per the *Washington Post*, "after meeting with
fellow Republican senators in Richmond and hearing concerns that the party could
lose his [state senate] seat if he were elected to Congress." The *Post* added
that his exit "removed Comstock's most formidable foe and the person most likely
to pull her to the right."

Then the rules move. **Under Virginia law parties choose among three nomination
methods: a state-run primary; a congressional district convention; or a "party
canvass," also called a "firehouse primary."** The firehouse primary (1) is held
separately from the state-run primary, (2) has fewer voting locations, and (3)
requires voters to sign a loyalty pledge. All three provisions "limit
participation to party loyalists, which is what Comstock's supporters wanted."
Her Tea Party opponent Bob Marshall wanted a convention, "which might be small
enough to be swamped by his cadre of intense supporters." **The local party chose
the firehouse primary and, "exercising its power to determine voting locations in
a party-run primary, it chose sites of establishment strength (Patterson 2018)."**

Comstock outspent Marshall **$570,000 to $50,000** and won **53 to 28** in a
six-candidate field. Note also: "the National Republican Congressional Committee
stayed out of the race, leaving the job entirely to local officials."

*MN-6R — the Cooper rule.* The most institutionally interesting case. After a
divisive 1998 gubernatorial primary, state chair **Bill Cooper** declared "that
henceforth the party would support its convention-endorsed candidates with as much
money as necessary to defeat any candidate who dared run against its endorsee." A
source: **"You weren't going to be able to beat an endorsed candidate. Nobody put
out a memo or a directive. You didn't have to, people just knew. We did say, 'If
you do run against an endorsed candidate, we'll beat you.'"**

Cooper's logic, as related by Tony Sutton (then executive director, later chair):

> [Cooper] roped in his [corporate] friends and he said, "Look . . . you don't wait
> until after the party has picked the candidates and then kind of support them, or
> worse yet wait until after the election and try and influence them. Try and help
> pick the candidates at the front, get the right people running, get the right
> organization behind them, win the election, and then you don't have to change
> their mind later after they're in office because you've got the right people to
> begin with."

The first challenge "failed spectacularly as the party candidate won by a
two-to-one margin." A winning-side activist: "We just campaigned straight to the
party base. We said, 'This guy is the official candidate' . . . and then on all
the official literature it was, 'endorsed, endorsed, endorsed, Republican-endorsed'
. . . Our guy was on paper the lesser candidate, but he was endorsed." A losing-side
activist agreed the candidates had roughly equal money and volunteers, "but 'it was
the value of that endorsement' that mattered to voters."

"For the next ten years and four party chairs, the Cooper rule prevailed." It
benefited "business conservatives, religious activists, the party's few moderates,
and, beginning in the first decade of the 2000s, libertarians," and supported even
candidates who beat the party's own incumbent officeholders. It "frayed
significantly in the 2010s."

**Tom Emmer's fundraising move is the sharpest single tactic in the book.** When
Bachmann announced her retirement in 2013, Emmer's chief political advisor told him
to act fast to discourage others; **within six hours he was in the race.** Then:

> One of Emmer's first moves as a candidate was to telephone two leaders of a
> business group called the Freedom Club to secure a commitment that they, their
> wives, and nine other couples would contribute the legal maximum of $10,400 per
> couple for the primary and general election, which would total over $200,000.
> **The Federal Election Commission reports contributions on a quarterly schedule,
> and the next reporting period was only weeks away. Emmer believed that if he
> could raise $200,000 and have it reported in the upcoming FEC data release, it
> would be a further deterrent to other candidates entering the race.**

He hit **$225,000** — "but, as it turned out, he got most of it from sources other
than the two network leaders he first contacted. 'They overpromised,' explained our
source." He then won the endorsement easily; one opponent dropped out, the other
was crushed. "The role of voters in this process was essentially zero."

*IA-3R — a mixed case.* **Iowa's rule: a convention of local party officials must
be called if the primary winner receives less than 35 percent.** Six candidates, so
the leader unsurprisingly fell short. The convention "proceeded by a series of
votes in which the candidate with the least support was eliminated until only two
remained" — a libertarian state senator who had finished **first** in the primary,
and a moderate conservative who had finished **fifth** and had never run for office.
**The moderate won 54 to 46.** Four sources gave the same account: "The majority of
delegates sincerely preferred the libertarian candidate but worried he could not win
the general election and so strategically voted to nominate the more moderate
candidate."

**Summary of the twelve local-party districts:** "In all twelve districts,
candidates favored by these twelve local parties won their nominations —
mechanically in the three convention cases, by being unopposed in the primary in two
cases, and by winning contested primary elections in the other seven. **Ten of the
twelve also won their general elections**, the exceptions being two candidates who
were defeated by local party-backed candidates of the other party."

### Informal political coordination

"The soft tissue of American party politics — very important to the system, but
often hard to get a fix on, sometimes even when you are looking right at it."
Eleven primaries.

- **MI-14D, Sandy Levin's group.** District 58.5 percent African American, 4.4
  percent Hispanic. Rudy Hobbs, 38, won the party committee, fourteen local
  political groups, most local officeholders and most unions. Every one of eight
  sources named **Sandy Levin**, a neighboring white congressman, as the key. "Sandy
  Levin picked Rudy Hobbs to run," said one, noting with disapproval that Levin had
  passed over more-senior politicians. Hobbs "hadn't gone through any of the
  traditional chairs . . . no dues paid, no basis for his involvement in the race —
  except the Levin family." Candidates not backed by Levin "hit roadblocks": "The
  Levins sort of put out the word that 'Hey, this [Hobbs] is my guy.'"
  - **And the Levin group lost**, to EMILY's List, which recruited Brenda Lawrence.
    The contrast in ground game is the useful detail: Hobbs's operation ran on
    "union regulars who were older, often retired, and poorly motivated" — "they
    will 'lit drop' . . . meaning [canvassers] are not knocking and waiting for
    someone to talk to and sending the message . . . They're either putting [the
    literature] in the newspaper box or just leaving it in the door." EL's
    canvassers "tended, by contrast, to be idealistic college women excited to be
    doing important work," trained "to do actual real door knocking." Three separate
    sources: "EMILYs List basically took over that campaign"; "Not a chance"
    Lawrence would have won without it; EL "was the biggest difference maker."
  - A careful note on vocabulary: "None of our sources referred to Levin as a boss or
    as running a political machine. These are easy terms to toss around, so we take
    note that they were not used in reference to Levin."
- **FL-13R, competing IPOs.** Bill Young had held the seat forty years; when he died
  in 2013, eight Republicans were mentioned but **none formally declared** — all
  waiting to see whether Mark Baker, the popular former mayor of St. Petersburg,
  would run. Baker declined. **Within hours of his withdrawal, David Jolly — a
  Washington lobbyist with no electoral experience — declared. The next day Mel
  Sembler announced support. Over the next few days every previously interested
  politician stayed out.** The *Tampa Bay Times*: "One would-be candidate said it
  should have been a bloodbath with a half-dozen politicians elbowing their way to
  the front of the line. Instead, the frontrunner is a guy nobody had heard of a
  month ago. . . . What gives? Where are the big names? Why does everyone else
  suddenly have something better to do?"
  - Sembler was a national GOP fundraiser, ambassador to Australia and the Vatican.
    "He's been key in almost every race in town." Asked about him, a county party
    chair said **"He is a saint"**; an officeholder Sembler had supported "choked up
    and was unable to speak."
  - **"The caucus"** — "an informal group of public officials, businesspeople,
    lobbyists, and party activists that met most Friday afternoons at Crabby Bill's,
    a beach district bar that served double drinks for only $3.50." Bill Young had
    been a regular; so was Jolly. Led informally by Tony DiMatteo, a former county
    chair. DiMatteo's pitch:
    > With me, when I raise money . . . I feel very comfortable asking well-off
    > people, that are worth $30 million, to give me ten grand. Because to them, ten
    > grand, they'll piss that away on four days at Monte Carlo or going to the Cannes
    > Film Festival. To them that's not a lot of money. But to me that's a lot of
    > money to help my candidates. So if you want me to . . . give you grief, fine. If
    > not, if you want me to love you, give me the ten grand. You'll get a front table
    > [at the annual Republican dinner], and when Mitt Romney comes, you'll get a
    > one-on-one with the guy for ten minutes . . . People want accessibility.
  - A rival IPO around state Senator **Jack Latvala** ran Kathleen Peters as what
    sources called "a sacrificial lamb." **The authors could not interview it:**
    "Latvala proscribes his politicians from speaking to the news media except at his
    direction, and none, including Latvala, responded to our numerous interview
    requests. . . . Hence our knowledge of the Latvala network is entirely
    secondhand."
  - What the Sembler group wanted was concrete: **federal largesse.** The district
    "is dotted with projects secured by Bill Young — elevated freeways, freeways over
    water, beach preservation, research centers, defense contracts, veteran
    rehabilitation, an air base that Young rescued from closure." Jolly had been
    Young's top staffer. A source: "Who are we going to get to replace him who on Day
    One is going to be able know where the bathroom is, and also can talk to fourteen
    different staff members the way a congressman could? . . . [other candidate] isn't
    going to go to Congress and know what to do. It will take her sixteen years to
    figure that out." And: "It's an incredibly non-sexy message [to voters] to say,
    'David Jolly can pick up the phone and talk to seven different congressional
    staffers like that [snaps fingers].' It's inside baseball. But to the inside
    baseball people, it's really important."
  - Fair-minded footnote by the authors: two of the four finance committee members
    "were mostly retired from business and so could expect little if any personal
    benefit," while the other two "were involved in businesses that might have
    benefited from a friend in Congress."
- **CA-25R, a dinner.** Buck McKeon, 76, chair of House Armed Services, was pressured
  to retire by locals who felt he "could be missing in action when the community
  needed his help." He retired; no local declared; **Tony Strickland "parachuted in"
  from a neighboring district with a $500,000 war chest.** Then: **"five local
  Republican leaders met for dinner to decide what should be done. Two sources, both
  well-placed party insiders, said that none of the five diners was interested in
  running for Congress, but they agreed that one of them must oppose Strickland. They
  also agreed that it should be Steve Knight"** — who "agreed to the race with
  reluctance."
  - Strickland raised and spent **$2.1 million** across the all-party primary and the
    R-v-R runoff; **Knight spent only $400,000** and won. Knight's counter was a
    ground campaign, described in a quoted **UCLA undergraduate research paper by
    Christopher Leach**: the Antelope Valley GOP headquarters ran phone banking,
    canvassing and sign distribution with "12–14 volunteers helping with the campaign
    on a weekly basis and at least 20 volunteers assisting with campaign operations on
    weekends," plus "15 paid volunteers who received $10 an hour who worked as leaders
    to help and train the unpaid volunteers," reportedly reaching **50,000 voters**.
  - District was only R+3 and flipped Democratic in 2018 — so this could read as
    strategic. "But as our sources described the nomination contest, it was, at least
    for Knight's key supporters, more about community interest than ideology."
- **CA-11D, Mark DeSaulnier — 85 percent of the primary vote.** Two strong state
  legislators chose to run for the state senate seat he was vacating instead. Party
  actors — construction unions, retiring incumbent George Miller, and **Nancy
  Pelosi**, "then-minority leader of the House and head of an informal network of Bay
  Area Democrats" — dried up support for others. A potential candidate: "I still
  remember the day when George announced [retirement] and we texted each other and
  then we talked, and I told him I wasn't running, and I said, 'You have my support,'
  and the next day they sent a letter out for me to sign and a press release saying
  [I had]. So they were right on top of it."
  - **Why party leaders "owed something" to DeSaulnier** is the human detail worth
    keeping. In a 2009 House primary they had blessed him; he led the field, but
    "Democratic leaders in the state legislature asked him to take several unpopular
    votes during the campaign period. DeSaulnier did as he was asked, making 'painful
    vote after painful vote to make the budget cuts necessary for the state to stay
    afloat' . . . but then got 'beat up mercilessly' by an electoral rival." Some of
    his own supporters abandoned him and he lost. "Most people who screwed
    [DeSaulnier] once weren't going to screw him a second time, because then that's
    seen as unreliable." As the 2014 filing deadline approached he was still nervous:
    "Well, I'm being told by Miller and others that nobody's going to run, but I don't
    trust it anymore."
  - DeSaulnier declined to discuss party leadership's role but agreed to be quoted:
    **"I always consider support less an anointment than a continuation of a
    relationship. It's a euphemism for you've done the work they support."**
  - And the strategic reason given for not letting voters decide a safe seat: the
    all-party primary made CA-11 "less safely Democratic than it seemed," and
    leadership was "aware of the 2012 primary in CA-31 in which . . . a splintering of
    the Democratic vote had permitted a white Republican to capture a Democratic
    Latino district."
- **MI-8R, a team of two.** Two establishment Republicans (Mike Bishop, Bryan
  Barnett) and one Tea Party candidate (Tom McMillin); "a field with this combination
  of candidates could split the establishment vote." Party chatter followed — a
  "closed door meeting . . . to hash this all out," "expect some decisions to be made
  fast." Three weeks later **Barnett dropped out**, having "secured several major
  endorsements and raised $100,000," saying he didn't want the party to suffer a
  divisive primary.
  - The private terms, from a source privy to the dealings: **"they negotiated a deal
    whereby one would drop out and the other would, as a member of Congress, always
    personally take the phone calls of the former, who would remain in local office.
    The representative-to-be also agreed never to run against the other politician in
    any subsequent election."** Bishop won 60–40 — "comfortable but by no means
    insurmountable if there had been a second establishment candidate in the race."
- **LA-5R, "the Neil Deal" — coordination that backfired.** The day after the
  incumbent's unexpected resignation, State Senator Neil Riser announced with
  endorsements from most major state Republicans. Then **a newspaper revealed he had
  filed campaign papers a week *before* the resignation announcement.** Journalists
  dubbed it the Neil Deal; Governor Jindal "reset the election date to allow
  additional candidates to get into the race, and a good number did" — seven
  Republicans, five Democrats, two Libertarians, a Green, two Independents. Riser led
  the first stage with 32 percent, but with five Democrats splitting that vote the
  second-place finisher was also a Republican, **Vance McAllister, who beat Riser in
  the runoff.** Who wanted what: consultants wanted "the fees from managing an
  off-cycle election"; the governor's supporters wanted Riser; "the retiring incumbent
  to want an administrative position from Jindal that would qualify him for a large
  state pension."

  > **Not checkable against the federal record, and the reason is worth knowing.**
  > Riser's 32 percent is from the **November 2013 special election**, and that
  > election does not appear in the Commission's 2014 compilation at all. The name
  > Riser occurs on no sheet of that workbook, and Louisiana has no
  > `UNEXPIRED TERM` rows in it. The 2014 volume carries only the regular November
  > 2014 LA-5 contest, in which McAllister — by then the sitting member, having won
  > the seat in this very special election — ran fourth with 11.1 percent and lost.
  > So the book's account of the Neil Deal cannot be corroborated or contradicted
  > from this source. Do not go looking for it there.
- **CO-4R, swapping candidates.** Ken Buck, who had cost the GOP a Senate seat in
  2010, announced he would run for Senate again in 2014. DC Republicans tried to
  recruit Cory Gardner, the sitting Fourth District congressman, to run against him;
  Gardner refused. "After weeks of party pressure, the two politicians agreed to swap
  races — Buck would run in the Fourth Congressional District and Gardner would run
  for Senate." Both won. "A skillful piece of field management by GOP leaders."

### National party organizations

- *Open Secrets* estimates the **DCCC spent more than $200 million** in 2013–14 and
  the **NRCC around $150 million**. But both "devoted the bulk of these resources to
  defeating the other party in general elections and seemed . . . to be minor direct
  influences in all but a handful of the open-seat primaries we studied."
- **Four instances** of a formal endorsement in a winnable open-seat primary:
  **CA-31D, IA-3D, NC-7R, NY-4D**. In three of the four (CA-31D, IA-3D, NC-7R),
  involvement "began as efforts to recruit strong challengers before the opposing
  party incumbent decided to retire."
- NY-4D is the odd one: "a strong party organization . . . would normally have
  controlled the House nomination. But when its boss refused to support the leading
  candidate over a long-term squabble, the DCCC stepped in and made an endorsement."
- **"The national committees got the nominees they wanted in all four cases."**

### Group-led coordination

- **AZ-7D, "union hardball."** Two union-backed candidates — Ruben Gallego (United
  Culinary and Food Workers, the district's largest union) and Steve Gallardo
  (AFSCME) — plus Mary Rose Wilcox, a county supervisor and civil rights leader. All
  three had progressive credentials; Gallego was "stronger in one key area — his
  professionalized and modern approach to voter mobilization," which mattered because
  the solidly Democratic district "contributed little to Democratic margins in
  statewide contests due to low turnout among its mostly Latino electorate."
  - The pressure on Gallardo to quit came from three directions and the authors report
    it plainly: senior community leaders met with him to urge withdrawal; **"a person
    associated with the Gallego campaign threatened Gallardo supporters with loss of
    public contracts unless they changed sides"**; and **"Gallardo himself was
    threatened with being outed as gay, according to sources."** The AFSCME
    rank-and-file voted to endorse Gallardo, "but under pressure from Gallego
    supporters, the executive council reversed the decision."
  - The resolution was a trade: **if Gallardo withdrew and endorsed Gallego, the UCFW
    would support him for Wilcox's open seat on the board of supervisors.** He
    accepted, switched races, and won it. **Gallego beat Wilcox by 13 points, "a
    margin that might not have withstood a Gallardo candidacy."**
- **IA-1D, "playing nicer."** The impetus was Swati Dandekar, who "led a bloc of six
  Democratic legislators who obstructed key pro-union and social-welfare measures."
  She was "a prodigious fundraiser and, as the only moderate in a field with four
  progressives, she might have pulled out a win." Union leaders asked the state
  Democratic chair for help and got only advice: **"If you don't want Swati to win
  the nomination, you [union guys] better pick a horse and get behind him."**
  - The difficulty, from a union source: **"In Jimmy Hoffa days, a union president
    would make the decision [on whom to support]. Now membership is more involved"** —
    and more divided. AFSCME favored Pat Murphy; Cedar Rapids construction trades
    favored Monica Vernon; a Waterloo firefighters union backed its local
    assemblywoman Anesa Kajtazovic. "You have two ladies . . . and then you have a
    proven past guy. You now have a dilemma. . . . That sucks. I don't know that there
    is a good answer in how to pick that one. All three of them are a benefit . . . you
    do have to pick one."
  - The procedure: the regional AFL-CIO's tough vetting (chapter 4's convention), then
    **a vote of the membership on whether to release union campaign funds and
    canvassing resources**, requiring two-thirds. Murphy got it, and national AFL-CIO
    support followed. **"By primary day, eighteen of twenty unions in the district that
    made endorsements — endorsements that were, according to our sources, the only
    important form of organized support in the race — were for Murphy."**
  - **The Kajtazovic cautionary tale** is the sharpest thing in the section. She had
    won her first legislative election in the 2010 Republican wave and was "a 'golden
    child.'" Running for Congress, "contacting the same donors to raise money as in the
    past, she got nowhere. Union support that was once strong dried up." An insider:
    those who had supported her "had often received phone calls from leaders suggesting
    that they give. **'Money came to her. She never understood that it came because it
    was partly driven by the party.'**" By opposing Murphy "she was bucking an
    unofficial but quite real pecking order." And: "I think everyone's presumption is,
    we're going to train this woman up, she's going to be in the legislature for like
    ten years . . . and then she stepped out of it . . . The biggest question in that
    campaign that was asked over and over again was, **'You're too young, you're
    stepping out of line.'**" She finished **fifth of five in fundraising and fourth in
    the primary balloting**.

- Local party organization (8) — "we are reasonably confident that we didn't miss any

- National party (4) — "since it leaves a clear imprint in the reports of the Federal

- **Informal (11) and group-led (5) — "cannot be identified with as much confidence.
  Most cases were unreported in news media or blogs and left no discernible trace in
  official records. It took a combination of getting the right interviews and winning
  the confidence of our sources to find out about several of these cases . . . and we
  are keenly aware that we might have missed similar instances in districts in which
  we had fewer good sources."** Hence: **"Our total of sixteen nomination contests
  involving informal coordination by party networks and groups is a lower-bound
  estimate."**

**Thirty-two of fifty-five is 58 percent.** How much it affected outcomes "is a
question that we take up in chapter 6."

### Why not more coordination?

Four reasons offered:

1. Some groups "see a realistic prospect for nominating a true champion, someone who
   is, in their eyes, so much better than any alternative that they will accept a
   lower chance of winning in order to keep some chance of winning big." The
   pharmacists behind Buddy Carter in GA-1 "may have viewed the race in these terms."
2. Some groups represent opposing factional interests "and want to fight rather than
   cooperate" — the religious activists behind Dean Young "would have had little to
   gain by joining in support for a business candidate like Bradley Byrne."
3. **"Some groups may have goals that are inherently difficult to compromise, such as
   the nomination of a woman, a co-ethnic, or an LGBTQ person."**

Closing: "Political coordination is thus a common but not ubiquitous feature of House
nominations. Where present, it introduces potential political advantage to those who
engage in it. But some groups may see greater advantage in going it alone."

### For the corpus

**Table 5.2 is the closest thing to a released dataset in this book.** It is
thirty-two race identifiers in five categories — `NJ-1D`, `CA-31D`, `AZ-7D` and so
on — which is a joinable key. Every one of those races has FEC filings, official
returns, and a district. A chapter could take that table as given and ask what the
public record shows about the races the authors coded as coordinated versus the
twenty-three they did not: field size, spending concentration, winner's vote share.
That is a genuinely new use of the book, and it requires nothing but the table and
data the corpus already knows how to fetch.

**But the table's own confidence gradient is the lesson, not a footnote.** The
authors say plainly that the 8 local-party cases are near-complete, the 4 national
cases leave "a clear imprint in the reports of the Federal Election Commission," and
the 16 informal and group-led cases are **a lower bound** that depended on "getting
the right interviews and winning the confidence of our sources." So the table is
*three different measurements stacked in one column*: an administrative record, a
disclosure record, and a fieldwork record, with completeness declining across them.
Any corpus chapter that treats 32/55 as one number will be wrong in the way the
corpus exists to teach about. The right framing is the one `census-coverage` already
uses — the count grades itself, and mostly returns a blank.

**MI-13 is the single best teaching case in the book.** Same district, same day, same
voters, two ballots, four candidates versus six — and the establishment candidate wins
one and loses the other. Table 5.1 is small enough to print whole and the arithmetic
is subtraction. It is a natural experiment in vote splitting that needs no model, no
regression and no significance test, which makes it usable where Table 7.5 is not.
Note that it sits **outside the study frame** (2018) and the authors exclude it from
their summary statistics — that exclusion should travel with it.

**The rules are the most under-used finding here, and they are all public.** Every
one of these is a documented election-law fact, not an interview:

| Rule | Consequence in the chapter |
|---|---|
| NJ county line — endorsed candidates in the first ballot column | Endorsed candidates beat their closest rival 67% to 18% on average across four counties |
| Virginia's three nomination methods, incl. the "firehouse primary" | The party picked the method *and* the polling locations |
| Mercer County's 60% rule for sole possession of the line | Decided whether BWC shared her home county line |
| Iowa's 35% threshold triggering a convention | The fifth-place primary finisher became the nominee |
| NC's 40% threshold (ch. 7) | Adams's real margin was 4 points, not 20 |
| PA's lack of a runoff (ch. 4) | "Boyle's advantage would have been neutralized" |

That is a chapter: **the same votes, counted under six different state rules, produce
six different nominees.** It is entirely buildable from public sources, it needs no
interviews, and it is the kind of thing `03-elections` is for. Nothing in the corpus
currently covers nomination *rules* as opposed to nomination *outcomes*.

**One methodological detail worth stealing.** The authors count coordination only when
"the impetus . . . come[s] from the policy demanders, not the candidate," and only on
evidence of "definite, often personal, interaction." They then explicitly decline to
count Debbie Dingell, whose support looked coordinated but where no source said it was.
That is a coding rule that costs them a case they probably could have claimed, and they
say so. It is a good model for how the corpus talks about judgment calls in a build
script.

**And a caution on sourcing.** The Latvala network in FL-13 is described entirely
secondhand because its members were forbidden to speak; the AZ-7 threats ("loss of
public contracts," "threatened with being outed as gay") rest on unnamed sources. These
are the book's most serious allegations and its thinnest evidence. If any corpus
chapter ever cites the AZ-7 case, it cites the book's characterization, not the fact.

## Chapter 6. Anchoring (pp. 163–192)

> **Proposition 4.** *Most winning candidates anchor their campaigns in major
> support from a single policy-demander group or association of these groups.*

**This is the most methodologically interesting chapter in the book, and for a
data course it is the most important.** Its subject is not really anchoring — it
is the problem of measuring group interest in FEC records, and the authors are
unusually candid that the records mostly cannot do it. Three tables.

### The concept

> We define an **anchor** as a single source that provides enough support to
> establish its preferred candidate as viable — that is, having a good chance to
> win the nomination. The single source may be an individual group, association
> of groups, or a party.

**Anchoring happens early, and that is the point.** Quoting Danielle Thomsen,
*The Money Signal* (2025, 23–24): "One of the laws of fundraising is that money
begets money. . . . As one candidate said, 'If you can tell people, "Look, we're
starting with half a million dollars; I'm going to win this thing," then you
attract more money.' . . . Early money has a particularly potent feedback effect,
whereby those who post formidable early totals are taken more seriously and seen
as worthy of attention."

Money is not the only anchor — "volunteer labor, coordinated action, and campaign
know-how can be equally effective." And a big early commitment has a second
effect: **the "scare-off effect."** "Any sensible actor would, for example, think
twice after a group like EMILYs List or the Chester County Republican Party
Committee has made its support known. This 'scare-off effect' may help explain
why few primaries have more than one or two anchored candidates — and why anchored
candidates often face weak competition."

The three headline results, stated up front: **(1) 47 of the 55 nominees anchored
their campaigns in a single source; (2) coordinated action was the most common
form; (3) anchored candidates rarely lost primaries to unanchored ones.**

### What counts as anchoring — the thresholds

The authors call these "unavoidably arbitrary" and say so twice.

| Form | Threshold |
|---|---|
| **Coordinated action** | Every case of coordination from ch. 5 counts. |
| **Monetary support** | **$250,000** from a single source in races without a runoff; **$400,000** in races with one. |
| **Volunteer support** | When "a credible source told us that volunteer campaigners had been critical to a campaign." |
| **Campaign know-how** | Major expert assistance on fundraising, voter targeting, phone banking. |

**The justification for the money threshold is itself a nice piece of data
reasoning:** most candidates able to raise $250,000 from one source "could probably
raise at least that much again from others, for a total of $500,000. And from FEC
data we can see that **79 percent of those who spent $500,000 or more finished in
first or second place in their primaries.**" Runoffs cost more, hence the higher
bar.

### Anchoring via coordination — and the field-size evidence

Recap of the 32 coordination cases from ch. 5, then the payoff chapter 5 promised:
**"thirty of the thirty-four candidates having coordinated support did win
nomination."**

Mechanism: coordinated support "tended to discourage some other candidates from
running and the potential supporters of others from wasting resources on them."
Evidence — **"in thirteen of the primaries in which we found some form of
coordination, a news story reported that one or more candidates subsequently
dropped out of the primary."**

**TABLE 6.1. Political coordination and average field size** — transcribed exactly:

| | Coordination (1) | No coordination (2) | Local (3) | Informal (4) | National (5) | Group (6) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Number of candidates | 4.2 | 6.2 | 2.6 | 5.6 | 2.5 | 5.2 |
| Effective number of candidates (ENC) | 2.6 | 3.6 | 1.9 | 2.7 | 2.2 | 3.2 |
| Effective number of financed candidates (ENF) | 2.3 | 3.4 | 1.8 | 2.4 | 1.6 | 3.2 |
| Number of primaries | 30 | 23 | 8 | 11 | 4 | 5 |

*Note in the book: "Second and third rows show average ENC and NEFC across
candidates."* (The note's "NEFC" is a typo for ENF.)

**The ENC measure, explained in the book's own words because it is a genuinely
teachable statistic:** the *effective number of candidates* "downplays the role of
minor candidates who have no chance of winning (Laakso and Taagepera 1979). ENC
is calculated as the reciprocal of the sum of squared vote shares. That is,
**ENC = 1/(Σvᵢ²)**, where vᵢ is candidate *i*'s vote share."

Their three worked examples:

- Three candidates each with .333 → **ENC = 3**. "accurately indicates there are
  three strong candidates."
- Two at .49 and one at .02 → **ENC = 2.08**. "corresponding to the commonsense
  assessment that there are only two effective candidates."
- One at 90 percent and two at 5 percent each → **ENC = 1.2**, "suggesting one
  dominant candidate."

**ENF applies the identical formula to shares of overall spending** rather than
vote shares — "the *number of effectively financed candidates*." The finding:
"the average pattern in primaries in which coordination was observed approximates
that of a primary in which **most of the money has been channeled to just two
candidates.**"

Why two-candidate races matter: "they are free of spoiler effects and perhaps
easier for voters to participate meaningfully in."

### Anchoring via money — the measurement chapter

Two judgment calls are required: **what counts as a group, and how to identify
group members in FEC data.** Both are worked through in unusual detail.

**What counts as a group.** A group is "a collection of individuals animated by a
shared political interest," but "we allow some heterogeneity of interest."

The LA-6 example is the best illustration of a category that does real political
work while having no official existence. Graves's donors with an economic interest
in coastal Louisiana included **Occidental Petroleum, Marathon Petroleum, Phillips
66, Canal Barge Company, Louisiana River Pilots Association, Halliburton, Ship
Builders Council of America, Bollinger Shipyards, National Association of Chemical
Distributors, Koch Industries, Southern Chem Industries, and Offshore Marine
Services Association.** Then a harder set: **Daybrook Fisheries, Taylor Shellfish,
and an ecological restoration company, Resource Environment Solutions.** Should
fishing and shellfish count with oil drilling? The authors include them: "all four
businesses were engaged in economic exploitation of the same fragile environment,
and all were subject to the same federal regulatory regime."

**And then the sentence that should be quoted in any corpus chapter about
classification:**

> That the term *Louisiana maritime sector* has no official standing in commerce
> registries or US census categories complicates measurement, **but this fact does
> nothing to weaken it as a political force in the LA-6 primary.**

Their estimate: members of the Louisiana maritime sector contributed **more than
$755,000** to Graves.

**The three FEC categories, as the book explains them:**

- **Individuals** — up to **$2,600 per election** (primary, runoff and general are
  separate elections). "Most candidates report the bulk of their financial support
  in the form of individual contributions."
- **Traditional PACs** — limited to **$5,000 per election**. A business "cannot
  contribute some fraction of its profits"; unions "cannot contribute the mandatory
  dues of their members."
- **Super PACs** — unlimited, may use corporate funds and mandatory union dues,
  **cannot contribute directly to candidates**; must make independent expenditures.

**The PAC arithmetic is worth keeping** because it inverts the usual assumption:
"imagine a company that has a thousand employees, each of whom voluntarily gives
$5 to the company PAC. These contributions would fund a $5,000 PAC contribution,
the maximum the law allows. But if a mere **twenty** company members made individual
contributions of $1,000 each, the result would be a **$20,000** contribution. **The
typically lesser importance of traditional PAC contributions compared to individual
was a major feature of the House primaries in our study.**"

**The stated limitation, verbatim:**

> FEC data on the three kinds of campaign contributions are the backbone of our
> analysis of group anchoring through financial support, **but the data suffer a
> major limitation: the difficulty of determining the particular group interests
> that individual and PAC donors represent.** In some cases, the group interests of
> donors are obvious, but for many they are not.

**The sampling method used to get around it — GA-1, Bob Johnson.** This is the
methodological core and it should be recorded exactly:

> We say "estimated" because the figure $404,000 is based on **a sample of one
> hundred contributors, not the whole set of contributors**. That is, in a random
> sample of one hundred individuals, **fifty-eight reported medicine-related
> occupations or employers. These fifty-eight contributed a total of $42,825,
> roughly 63 percent of the total $68,251** contributed by all one hundred donors in
> the sample. Meanwhile, the sum of all Johnson's individual contributions (from
> **1,963 donors**) was about **$644,000**, 63 percent of which is about **$404,000**.

Johnson's other figures: Club for Growth **$388,000** independent expenditure,
**$80,000** bundled from individual donors following its recommendation, ~$25,000
in traditional PAC money from Madison Project, Citizens United and SarahPAC, and
~$4,000 of FreedomWorks campaign signs — about **$500,000** from "national
Republican insurgents." Medical PACs added **$87,000** from sixteen organizations
averaging about $5,400 each (above the $5,000 cap because primary and runoff are
separate elections). Medicine-related total ≈ **$490,000**.

Buddy Carter, same method: **$345,000** from pharmacy-connected individuals and
traditional PACs. Plus a candidate-specific Super PAC, *Prescription for America*,
whose donors included some "having no apparent links to pharmacy interests" — **"We
culled these donors,"** bringing it to $84,000 and Carter's pharmacy total to
**$429,000**.

WA-4 Newhouse: ag PACs included **Frozen Food PAC, Dow Chemical Agricultural
Executives PAC, General Mills Inc. PAC** — yet of nearly **$500,000** in ag-related
contributions, **about 75 percent came from individual donors who grew food for a
living.**

**The instructive failure — OK-5, Patrice Douglas.** Her plausible anchor sat at
the *intersection* of banking and energy. "**But FEC data permit us to observe only
the union of these interests. That is, we can identify contributors associated with
banking and those associated with energy but not contributors associated with
both.**" The two sectors together gave **$365,000**, short of the $400,000 runoff
threshold. She also had a **$60,000** independent expenditure from a Super PAC
(*Foundation for Economic Prosperity*) **not required to disclose its donors** — so
the authors assumed banking and energy were represented among them "in the same
proportion as in Douglas's traditional contributions, which was about 35 percent."
Adding 35 percent of $60,000 gives **$380,000**. Still short. **Not counted as
anchored.** "Nonetheless, her case illustrates some of the difficult judgment calls
involved."

**Co-ethnic anchoring.** Armenian PACs and individuals with Armenian names gave
Koutoujian (MA-5) an estimated **$310,000**. Similar co-ethnic anchoring found for
**Japanese Americans backing Mark Takai (HI-1)** and **Chinese Americans backing
Ted Lieu (CA-33)**.

**Where FEC codes run out entirely** — a sentence that could be the epigraph for a
corpus chapter:

> **FEC data has no codes that permit the identification of individuals by their
> political beliefs. Nor does it have codes for the military veterans who backed
> Ryan Zinke in a Montana primary or the "Chamber of Commerce crowd" that backed
> Bradley Byrne in AL-1.**

Three workarounds, in ascending cleverness:

- **Gary Palmer (AL-6)** — Palmer asked donors to his think tank (the Alabama Policy
  Institute) to give **in the first FEC reporting period** so as to demonstrate
  viability. API donors supplied the bulk of the **$241,000** he raised in that
  period and kept giving to **$394,000** overall, plus a **$260,000** Club for Growth
  independent expenditure. Counted as anchored on the assumption that at least 36
  percent of first-quarter donors were ideologically motivated.
- **Alex Mooney (WV-2) — donor-history sampling.** Endorsed by essentially every
  top-tier insurgent PAC, yet direct support was only **$107,000**. But **more than
  95 percent of his individual contributions, about $500,000, came from donors
  living outside West Virginia**, and **73 percent** from outside both West Virginia
  and Maryland. So: **"we took a random sample of twenty-five Mooney donors and
  found that all but three had previously donated to at least one other Republican
  insurgent candidate, and that most had donated to many more. The median number of
  insurgent candidates or groups supported by the sample of individual Mooney donors
  was twenty-four and the average was seventy-seven."**
- **Clint Didier (WA-4) — the same method, negative result.** "Only **10 percent**
  of Didier's individual donors lived outside of Washington, and only **five of
  twenty-five** of his randomly sampled donors had ever given to another candidate
  in a federal election." FreedomWorks made **$110,000** in independent expenditures
  in the runoff, but "money identifiable as national insurgent support did not come
  close to meeting our criterion for anchoring. **In contrast to Mooney, Didier had
  the profile of a non-anchored candidate.**"
- **Ryan Zinke (MT-AL) — cross-referencing a Super PAC's own donor list.** Zinke
  founded *Special Operations for America* (SOFA), which spent $81,000 against Obama
  in 2012. In 2014 SOFA made a **$175,000** independent expenditure for Zinke and
  **$17,000** against an opponent; veterans' PACs gave **$9,000** directly. Then:
  "**Working from the donor list of Special Operations for America, we identified
  individuals who donated both to SOFA and, separately, to the Zinke campaign. Their
  contributions directly to Zinke come to about $50,000**," bringing veteran-linked
  support to **over $250,000** and meeting the criterion.

**And one case where they gave up on quantification.** Bradley Byrne (AL-1): "This
amount still leaves us short of the $200,000 threshold, and there is no usable FEC
code for 'business.' Yet our interview evidence was too strong to be overlooked.
So, **despite our strong wish to ground our analysis in quantitative evidence, we
rely on qualitative evidence in classifying Byrne as anchored by business.**"

The section's closing admission: "**Large group contributions to candidates will
often not leave clear fingerprints in FEC records.** The problem is not, however,
insoluble. Future researchers, with a better understanding of limitations of FEC
data than we had when we began our study, may be able to handle the problem better
than we have by asking sharper questions at the interview stage than we did."

Seven winners anchored via money alone: **AL-6R, AR-2R, CA-33D, GA-1R, HI-1D,
MT-ALR, WV-2R.** Plus Byrne (AL-1R) and Graves (LA-6R), reclassified from
coordination to money because "we judged the money to have had a bigger impact" —
**total nine.**

### Anchoring via expert assistance: EMILY's List

- Endorsed in **eleven of the twenty** Democratic primaries in the study; support
  judged sufficient to constitute anchoring in **seven**. Of those seven, **four
  won (MA-5D, ME-2D, MI-14D, NC-12D)** and **three finished second (CA-31D, AZ-7D,
  CA-33D)**.
- **"EMILYs List is an acronym for 'Early Money Is Like Yeast' — because it 'raises
  the dough.'"**
- "No other group in our study approached EL's combination of resources,
  sophistication, and success."
- EL was "adamant about only one issue: women's right to abortion." Otherwise it
  wanted "female candidates who were willing to mount energetic campaigns and follow
  its advice." The red flag: "'Well look, I want to win, [but] I'm not going to quit
  my job, I can only make fundraising phone calls four hours a day, my husband is
  going to be my campaign manager, and I'm going to do my own direct mail as well.'
  . . . those are huge red flags."
- The hypothetical dialogue EL uses on candidates: "'You'll need to raise $100,000,
  $150,000 even before we send you a check to prove your viability to run for
  Congress . . .' And so they'll say back to me often, 'Well I thought you'd provide
  early money for us.' I'll say, 'We can provide support, we can provide trained
  finance staff, we can help show them a path, but if they're not willing to sit
  down in that chair and make those phone calls, like, the race is over, really.'"
- **The effect size, as spending:** Brenda Lawrence running **without** EL in her
  first House primary in 2012 could spend only **$135,000**; **with** EL in 2014 she
  spent **$460,000**. Eloise Reyes, who had never run for office, spent **$960,000**.
- EL running interference — the Stacey Appel (IA-3) episode: EL had endorsed early,
  then heard the DCCC and a local union were looking for a stronger candidate. EL
  warned both that **"if you put another person in this primary we will crush them
  like a bug . . . You don't want to spend any resources on that."** Both backed down.
- On the DCCC relationship: "Just like a husband and wife, we fight. But we've got
  to keep it together for these races, our children. . . . Sometimes we disagree, and
  it's frustrating, because we're two strategists with different minds."
- What EL actually supplies, beyond money: "[EL is] deeply involved in these
  campaigns. . . . invited to all of their conference calls, [it's] privy to all of
  their decision making . . . In some cases, the consultant will call [EL] and say,
  'Can you convince the candidate that this ad should air?'" And: "part of what
  [they] do at EMILYs List is provide **emotional support** . . . for these candidates
  just having somebody to call at 11:00 p.m. at night when they're in tears because
  they had to miss their son's soccer game is as much about what [they] do as making
  sure that their mail goes to the right audiences."
- **The measurement admission, which matters:** some EL money "was sent by donors
  directly to the candidates, leaving no trace of EL involvement. **We are therefore
  unable to use FEC data to measure overall EL influence — and wouldn't want to
  anyway because financial support leaves out so much of what EL does. We therefore
  assess EL activity holistically**, counting it as substantially influential if EL
  appeared to be making a major effort."

### Grassroots anchoring

The authors say plainly they did not expect this: "When we began this study, we had
little idea how important volunteer phone bankers and door knockers could be in
House primaries. We regarded them as standard campaign kit."

**The prior literature pointed the other way.** Broockman and Kalla (2018) reviewed
studies over nearly half a century and found "the average effect of canvassing on
election outcomes has been very small or null." **But** nearly all were partisan
general elections, most often presidential. The single primary study in that review
— a New Mexico county board of supervisors race — "reported a shift in vote
preference of **18 percentage points** from either a phone or a door-to-door
contact (Arceneaux 2007)."

The case that changed their minds is MI-4: Mitchell led **50–27** a month out, the
race went to a **38–38** tie, and he lost **36–52**. "[Moolenaar] was outspent by
Mitchell, but he had enough to convey the message. But really what they did was they
just out-ground-gamed Mitchell. I don't think Mitchell had any ground game at all."

**Eight primaries, all Republican**, where voter mobilization appeared to anchor. In
five, volunteers knocked and called "at a scale likely to influence outcomes"; in a
sixth they may have.

- **Steve Russell (OK-5)** — an Army Ranger who "led the team that captured dictator
  Saddam Hussein in Iraq." Spent **less than a third** as much as his strongest
  opponent. Twenty to thirty volunteers at any given time, "volunteers numbering in
  the low hundreds overall"; "We could turn out between forty and fifty at a time on
  a weekend." **"Volunteers were really our whole campaign."** He organized around a
  **"battle map"** of precincts where **three hundred or more voters** regularly cast
  primary ballots, contacting "all Republicans who had voted in four of the last four
  primaries and as many as possible of the rest." Finished **first with 32 percent**
  of a five-candidate primary; **won the runoff with 59 percent**, outspent both times.

  > Commission's 2014 compilation shows the OK-5 Republican primary with **six**
  > candidates, not five, and Russell first on **14,604 of 54,961 votes, or 26.6
  > percent** — not 32. The Commission's own `PRIMARY %` column reads `0.2657157`,
  > so this is not an artifact of recomputing the share off a different
  > denominator. No subset of the field reproduces 32 percent either: dropping the
  > smallest candidate gives 28.1 percent, and it takes dropping the two smallest

  >
  > **The runoff figure is exactly right.** Russell took **19,374 of 32,693, or

- **Jody Hice (GA-10)** — first in a seven-person field, then **54 to 46** in the
  runoff, "a margin of about 4,300 actual votes." Three parts: **chairs in each of
  the district's twenty-five counties**; "hundreds and hundreds of events" in
  nineteen-hour days across a district that "took over four hours to drive across";
  and canvassing — **289,000 doors and dials** across primary, runoff and general.
  **The FEC breakdown is the useful part:** primary spending of **$107,000 on

  **$87,000 on mailing** and **$20,000 on television**. In the runoff, **$30,000 on

- **Barry Loudermilk (GA-11)** — "we already have too many laws"; spent his time

  **$184,000** on advertising.
- **Bruce Westerman (AR-4)** — raised **$300,000** against Tommy Moll's **$510,000**.

  volunteers. **Americans for Prosperity** (Koch-supported) had run voter education

  door knocking or phone calls or stuffing envelopes." The **Garland County Tea

  and is counted as Westerman's anchor.
- **Glenn Grothman (WI-6) — the case they refused to classify.** Duey Stroebel spent

### For the corpus

**This chapter is a ready-made argument that a disclosure record is not a record of
interests, and it is better evidence than anything the corpus currently has.** The
FEC files are complete, accurate, and public — and they still cannot tell you that
Halliburton and Taylor Shellfish are in the same political coalition, that a donor
is a veteran, or that the "Chamber of Commerce crowd" exists. The authors wanted to
quantify Bradley Byrne's business anchor and **could not**, and said so. That is the
`finance-source` argument with a concrete, citable failure attached.

**The OK-5 case is the sharpest single teaching example I have found in the book.**
"FEC data permit us to observe only the **union** of these interests . . . not
contributors associated with **both**." A candidate whose real base sat at the
intersection of banking and energy is unmeasurable in a file that records one
employer per donor. That is a set-theory point a first-year can follow, it is
concrete, and it changed the book's own classification.

**ENC and ENF are directly implementable and worth a chapter.** `ENC = 1/(Σvᵢ²)` on
vote shares, the same formula on spending shares. It is one line of arithmetic, it
needs no statistics, the book supplies three worked examples with intuitive answers,
and the corpus already holds both returns and FEC spending. A chapter that computes
"how many candidates were really in this race" for a full cycle — and shows the raw
count saying 6 while ENC says 2 — is squarely `distributions`-adjacent and would
pair with `primary-positions`.

**The sampling methods are a caution, not a model.** The $404,000 medical figure for
Bob Johnson comes from **100 sampled donors out of 1,963**, scaled by a 63 percent
share. The Mooney and Didier network findings rest on **25 sampled donors each**.
These are defensible exploratory moves and the authors flag them — but they are also
the load-bearing numbers behind several anchoring classifications, and a threshold
of $400,000 applied to an estimate built from a 5 percent sample is doing more work
than it can carry. If a corpus chapter cites any anchoring classification, the
sample size travels with it.

**The thresholds are arbitrary and the authors say so twice.** $250,000 / $400,000
decide whether a candidate is "anchored," and Patrice Douglas misses at $380,000
after two judgment calls that each moved her number. Table 6.2's headline — 85
percent — is a function of those cutoffs. That is a good, honest example of a
finding that is real but whose precise value is a choice, which is the kind of thing
`census-coverage` and `turnout-denominator` already teach.

**One number is independently checkable and worth verifying:** "79 percent of those
who spent $500,000 or more finished in first or second place in their primaries."
That is a pure FEC-plus-returns claim about the 2014 cycle, it needs no interviews,
and the corpus could reproduce it for any cycle.

## Chapter 7. Voters (pp. 193–222)

> **Proposition 5.** *Electorates are highly limited in their ability to use
> primary elections to give policy direction to their parties.*

**This is the chapter with the original quantitative evidence.** Two studies the
authors ran themselves — a content analysis of newspaper coverage, and exit
surveys in four contests — plus two case studies and a literature review. It is
the chapter most useful to the corpus and the one with the most transcribable
tables.

The framing question, stated as a series of "what ifs" the earlier chapters have
already shown to be real: what if the candidates closest to most voters "had too
little money to get their views before the public? Or were pressured by party
leaders to get out of the race altogether? Or what if mainstream voters split
their votes among three candidates sharing their views and thereby permit a
fourth candidate with unpopular views to win?"

The authors' own summary of the evidence's weight: "None of this evidence is by
itself dispositive about the role of voters. But it raises substantial doubt
that voter preferences are important drivers of House nomination outcomes."

### Case 1: NC-12, "a close landslide"

A mostly African American Democratic electorate chose Alma Adams, a liberal with
a thirty-year activist record, over Malcolm Graham, a more conservative state
senator who had "served in leadership roles" at Bank of America and Time Warner
and was called "a corporate candidate." Seven candidates ran.

The naive reading is that a progressive electorate picked the progressive. The
authors dismantle it with four mechanisms — **voting rules, field composition,
geography, and hometown voting** — and note that "the local newspapers failed to
play up any ideological differences, and several articles reported that the
candidates 'agreed on most issues.'"

The mechanisms, in order:

1. **The rule.** North Carolina requires **40 percent** to be nominated,
   otherwise a runoff. "Adams's real margin of victory was not her 20-point
   advantage over Graham, but the **4 percentage points — based on only 2,309
   actual votes** — that lifted her above the 40 percent threshold."
2. **Geography and field composition.** Mecklenburg could turn out more than
   twice as many voters as Guilford. But **four of the seven candidates were from
   Mecklenburg and only two from Guilford** — "one had reportedly moved there
   because he knew it produced more votes." So Graham split Mecklenburg three
   ways while Adams had most of Guilford. Guilford candidates won **90 percent**
   of votes cast in Guilford; Mecklenburg candidates won **80 percent** of votes
   cast in Mecklenburg.
3. **Turnout.** Adams was an art history professor at Bennett College, a
   historically Black women's college. Her campus vote drive ran under the slogan
   **"Bennett belles are voting belles"** and reportedly turned out Bennett women
   at a rate of **96 percent**. The *Greensboro News & Record*: "Each fall,
   [Adams] made sure Bennett's students were registered to vote. On Election Day,
   she marched the students, hundreds of them, four blocks south to Reed Memorial
   CME Church to cast their ballots."
   - **The mobilization estimate, worth recording as a method.** In 2012, 132,938
     votes were cast for the House Democratic nominee in Mecklenburg and 72,951
     in Guilford — so Guilford's share was **54.8 percent** of Mecklenburg's.
     Applying that ratio to the 15,804 Mecklenburg votes in the 2014 primary
     predicts **8,673** in Guilford. Actual Guilford turnout was **13,055**. The
     gap of **4,382** votes is attributed to mobilization; if Adams won 72 percent
     of them, that is **at least 3,155 additional votes, 21 percent of her total
     15,235**.
4. **Money.** With EMILY's List and other feminist groups, Adams spent
   **$605,000**; Graham could spend only **$218,000**, "just over a third of
   Adams's total."

The authors' verdict is carefully hedged: absent evidence that Guilford was
"hugely more progressive" than Mecklenburg, "the most important voter input to
the outcome would seem to have been a strong tendency to support home county
candidates."

### Case 2: Cantor–Brat

Included only because "two readers of an early draft of this book raised it as a
potential counterexample."

- Cantor's own pollster had him **34 points ahead**. He spent **nearly $6
  million** against an opponent who spent **$200,000**. He lost by **10 points**.
- **The pollster's error is the interesting part:** afterward he said "he had
  drawn his sample from a pool of regular primary voters rather than from the
  entire eligible electorate." So the poll was not measuring the electorate that
  actually showed up.
- Consistent with that: **primary turnout rose from about 47,000 in 2012 to about
  65,000 in 2014.** A *Campaigns and Elections* writer on the Brat operation:
  "Cantor looked to bring in his typical Republican primary voter while we carved
  out a segment of voters who were not the usual targets for a primary."
- The two readings the authors offer: either voters looked at two candidates and
  chose the one demanding change, or "organized Tea Partiers identified and
  mobilized new voters on behalf of the group's policy demands." Under the
  second, "Cantor's defeat is an example of the power we claim policy demanders
  often wield."

### Prior research — three studies only

The authors say they know of **only three studies** that look closely at the
capacity of House primary voters for informed choice, one of which is their own.

- **Ahler, Citrin and Lenz (2016)**, California's 2012 top-two. They measured
  candidates' ideological locations and focused on **twenty** contests that were
  competitive among ideologically distinct candidates. The showcase case: Abel
  Maldonado, one of the state GOP's most prominent moderates, against Tea Party
  opponent Chris Mitchum. Quoted: "while Santa Barbara's conservative political
  elite observed gaping ideological differences between the two candidates, most
  constituents in the district failed to do so. They placed the two candidates at
  almost the same position on the [7-point] ideological continuum, with
  **Maldonado at 5.21 and Mitchum at 5.26**, despite Maldonado raising more than
  $1.5 million during his primary campaign to advertise his more moderate views."
  Across the twenty most contested 2012 House primaries, "few voters supported
  the candidate closest to their own self-described ideology."
- **Colao, Broockman, Huber and Kalla, "Tracing Polarization's Roots"** — a panel
  of **14,580 pre- and post-primary voters in twenty-two competitive primaries in
  2024**. Findings, recorded because the arithmetic is explicit:
  - Over **108 candidates in twenty-seven primaries**, only **sixteen cases of
    paired-candidate disagreement**. "There were thus no policy disagreements in
    at least eleven of the primaries and an average of less than one per primary
    overall."
  - About **22 percent** of co-partisan voters in competitive primaries were aware
    of the disagreements that occurred, and that subset was **13 percent** more
    likely to support the closer candidate — "which implies an issue effect of
    about **3 percent (.22 × .13)** of voters."
  - Voter learning of **non-stereotypical** positions "was negligible, which means
    its effect was negligible as well."
  - **But:** learning that a candidate was endorsed by a favorably regarded group
    "increased the probability of a voter's supporting that candidate by **20
    percentage points**." The authors: this "strengthens our claim for its
    importance."
- The scholarly poles: **Kinder (2012, 199)** — "Most citizens are ideologically
  innocent: indifferent to standard ideological concepts, lacking a consistent
  outlook on public policy, in possession of genuine opinions on only a few
  issues, and knowing damn little." Against **Sniderman (2017, 3)** — "Citizens,
  at any rate, a large bloc of them, can organize and simplify political choices
  because these choices have been organized and simplified for them by the
  political process itself." The authors' reply is neat: even Sniderman's
  optimism depends on a process that "has 'organized and simplified' politics,"
  and "that organization and simplification are exactly what, for typical voters,
  the primary process lacks. **Party labels are absent, emotionally charged group
  cues are muted, and media coverage is much lighter.**"

Three reasons ideological voting is harder in primaries than in general
elections: voters cannot lean on party differences and must learn each field
afresh; open primaries usually offer multiple candidates rather than two; and
multicandidate races — **thirty-nine of the fifty contested primaries in the
study** — may require strategic voting, which needs horse-race information.

**The counter-example the authors volunteer.** Little Rock, 1958. Incumbent Brooks
Hays took a conciliatory position on Eisenhower's use of federal troops to
enforce school integration; a write-in candidate jumped in and beat him. The
Michigan national election survey happened to be in the field. Nationally "about
20 percent of respondents can typically recall the name of challengers in House
elections, but **in Little Rock in 1958, 100 percent of survey respondents knew
the name of Hays's write-in opponent.**" Voters can do it when they care.

### Study 1: the newspaper content analysis

**Design, recorded in full because it is the most reproducible thing in the
book.**

- There were **fifty-three** winnable open-seat primaries in 2013–14; **three had
  only one candidate**. For the remaining **fifty**, they sought the **three
  newspapers with the highest circulation** in each district. One case was
  limited by the absence of searchable online versions, "hence we could study
  coverage in only **forty-nine** of our primaries." The newspaper list "may be
  found in the online appendix."
- Window: **thirty days up to and including Election Day**.
- Included: editorials, op-eds, letters to the editor. **Excluded:** paid
  advertisements, verbatim duplicates in the same paper, articles about the other
  party's primary, and articles not about the election.
- **Coding: "a minimum of three UCLA undergraduates who were trained to follow a
  detailed codebook. When coders disagreed, we used the majority's code. In cases
  of ties, we used the code indicating the higher information level."**
- Database: **1,962 stories from 156 paper-election combinations.** Of these,
  **340 letters to the editor** were dropped as "too unreliable to be of
  assistance to voters," plus **28 letters and op-eds signed by a candidate**.
  **Final N = 1,594 news articles.** (1,962 − 340 − 28 = 1,594.)

**The coding scheme** — five ways an article could differentiate candidates
politically:

1. *Contrasting candidate positions* — nontrivial differences on national issues.
2. *Distinctive party position* — a position distinct from party consensus, "based
   on neutral sources (or a reporter's assessment)." **Crucially: "We did not
   count allegations of transgressive positions that came only from opposing
   candidates or their supporters unless supported with neutral evidence."** The
   reason given: "potentially bogus claims of policy differentiation do not
   contribute to actual voter influence on House nominations."
3. *Factional alliances* — association with a widely known factional group. Self-
   reported morals do not count; a reporter's assessment does. "A claim that a
   candidate was 'not a career politician' or 'a political outsider' would not
   count as politically differentiating if made by the candidates themselves, but
   an observer's or a reporter's assessment that a candidate was
   anti-establishment would."
4. *Issue champions* — unusual effort or leadership on an issue, "even if the
   issue is itself not controversial within the party." Sponsoring a marriage
   equality bill counts; merely supporting gay marriage as a 2014 Democrat does
   not.
5. *Ideological appraisal* — by a neutral source. "But claims from supporters that
   their candidate is the 'truest conservative' or 'the most committed
   progressive' do not count."

Consensus positions that would *not* differentiate: for Republicans in 2014,
"pro-life, anti-tax, anti-regulation, pro–small government, balance budget, pay
down national debt, and oppose ACA/Obamacare"; for Democrats, "pro-choice,
pro–gun control, pro–access to health care, protect the environment, and support
gay marriage."

The authors note their protocols were "**intentionally generous** in detecting
differentiating information."

**"Duvergerian information"** (after Duverger 1954) — whether an article
identified **the top two candidates**, so a voter could avoid wasting a vote. "An
article that identified only the front-runner did not count." Could be based on
the journalist's judgment, fundraising, or polling — "an article simply listing
the amount of money raised by each candidate would count." They also recorded
*which* candidates were named as top two, "which allows us to differentiate
between accurate and inaccurate Duvergerian information." Coded only for races
with more than two candidates.

sources. Nonetheless, local papers at the time of our study were the best source
for reliable candidate information. . . . We invite readers skeptical of the
value of studying newspaper content in the twenty-first century to consider what
alternative source offers voters the information they need."

### Study 2: exit surveys in four contests

**Design:**

- Run by "a team centered at the University of Arkansas, Fayetteville."
- Four contests: **AR-2** (Little Rock and environs, R), **TX-36** (suburban
  Houston and rural east Texas, R), **IA-1** (Cedar Rapids, D), **PA-13** (split
  between urban Philadelphia and suburban Montgomery County, D). Arkansas, Iowa
  and Pennsylvania were multicandidate; **Texas was a two-person runoff following
  a twelve-candidate primary**.
- Administered as voters left the polls; **five to seven minutes**; **average
  response rate 42 percent**; about **eight polling locations per race**, chosen
  "to be roughly representative of local political divisions and to have histories
  of high voter turnout."
- Sample sizes **102 (PA-13D) to 214 (IA-1D)**; **total 655 respondents**.
- Interviewers were team members and students recruited at local universities,
  **paid $150 for a three-hour training session and a day of interviewing**.
- **Validity check, worth recording:** the surveys "correctly identified the
  winner in all races, and except for one pairing, also identified the correct
  candidate orderings. The **mean absolute difference between actual and survey
  votes was 3 percentage points**; the **correlation between actual and survey
  vote share across fifteen candidates was .98**."
- Issue questions on spending on government services, "Obamacare," and abortion —
  the first two on 7-point scales, abortion on a 4-point scale. Questions were
  "printed onto Show Cards with labeled scale points and given to respondents to
  hold as interviewers read the questions. We mention these details to show the
  effort made to obtain reliable measurement of voter attitudes."

**The one case with policy voting — PA-13 — undercuts itself.** 77 percent of
surveyed voters took the pro-choice side. But three candidates were on the
popular side of the issue and one, Brendan Boyle, on the other. "In the absence
of strategic voting, three candidates on the popular side of an issue against one
on the other side meant that **the minority position prevailed**." Boyle won **41
to 27 percent** over the first runner-up "even though the three suburban
candidates together outspent him by about **$5 million to $1 million**."

Boyle was the sole candidate from Philadelphia. That "was no accident": the IBEW
"led more than a dozen local unions in coordinated support for Boyle, and in
blue-collar, union-dense Philadelphia, this support left little space for other
candidates, two of whom entered but then dropped out when union support for Boyle
became clear (Patterson 2018)."

**And the test for strategic voting comes back negative.** Had pro-choice voters
coordinated, they would have gone to Marjorie Margolies, whom polls showed
strongest. The authors checked whether *better-informed* pro-choice voters did
so — measuring information by knowing which party controls the Senate and being
able to name at least two House primary candidates. "The result was that
better-informed pro-choice voters were **slightly less likely** to vote for
Margolies than for one of the other pro-choice candidates. **There was, thus, no
indication of strategic voting in the PA-13 primary.**"

Home-county effects in the other races: in TX-36, Streusand lived in the Houston
suburbs and Babin in the exurbs, and "the outlying area constituted more than 60
percent of the Texas district," which "was sufficient to throw the race to
Babin." In IA-1, Cedar Rapids is the largest city and **three of the five
candidates came from there**; Dubuque is half its size and **Murphy was its only
candidate**.

### "Uninformed or uninterested?"

The authors name the chicken-and-egg problem and decline to resolve it. Three
observations:

1. Newspapers are businesses. "If, for example, voters cared as much about House
   primaries as many care about high school football, it seems likely that local
   newspapers would provide more information about House primaries than they do."
2. The other way: the *Post-Star* of Glens Falls, population circa fifteen
   thousand, "can run seventy-five distinct stories in thirty days for the NY-21
   primary, then perhaps there is, after all, a market for House primary news."
   They did no interviews in NY-21 and "must leave the question to others."
3. "**Whatever the explanation for the generally poor state of news coverage, it
   leaves most voters unable to give political direction to their parties through
   the House nomination process.**"

And the consequence for candidate behavior: a farm-backed candidate who favors
generous immigration policy "can simply talk about some other issue, perhaps gun
control." A union-backed candidate can talk about health care instead of
right-to-work. "Or, most evasively of all, candidates with unpopular positions
may attack the character of their opponents, as occurred in the contest between
Bradley Byrne and Dean Young."

The comparison to presidential primaries: there, unpopular positions get attacked
and the attacks get coverage, "but this check was largely absent in the winnable
open-seat primaries we observed." Return of the example from chapter 4: "the
Democratic House candidate who had led a blocking coalition against progressive
legislation in the state assembly but went unchallenged for advertising herself
as a true progressive."

### Conclusion, verbatim

> The combination of policy demander involvement in the formation of candidate
> fields and lack of engagement by most voters creates a system in which the
> former are the most important actors in most nominations and the latter are
> typically unable to give political direction to their parties through the
> candidates they nominate. We do not argue that voters are incapable of broader
> influence or that they never effectively assert their preferences over those of
> party actors, only that **under typical open-primary conditions they do not
> usually do so**.

### For the corpus

**This chapter is the one that pairs directly with `primary-positions`.** That
chapter shows that 45 percent of candidates said nothing about immigration on
their own websites, and that the median candidate addressed 6 of 14 issues.
Chapter 7 shows the other half of the same hole: **1.3 percent of newspaper
articles explained an issue difference at more than a single sentence**, and
**47 percent of voters leaving the polls could not name one candidate they had
just chosen among**. Website silence, press silence, and voter recall are three
independent measurements of the same absence. The book supplies two of them and
the corpus already has the third.

**The Duvergerian-information code is a genuinely new idea worth teaching.** It
is not "was the coverage good" but "could a reader have known which two
candidates were live?" — a question with a checkable right answer, since the
result is known afterward. **64.5 percent of papers never once got it right**,
and among articles that tried, "the information failed to correctly identify the
top two candidates about one-third of the time." That is a measurement of press
performance that grades itself against the returns. Nothing in the corpus does
anything like it, and the returns needed to grade it are exactly what
`03-elections` already holds.

**Table 7.1 is a small, complete, checkable table.** Seven candidates, three
counties, votes and percentages, and a finding — 90 percent and 80 percent
home-county loyalty — that a reader can verify by dividing the cells themselves.
It would make a good worked example of *within-group share* against *district
share*, and it is the kind of table `levels-of-aggregation` is about.

**The NC-12 mobilization estimate is a method the corpus should be careful
about.** The authors take a 2012 county ratio, apply it to 2014, and attribute
the residual to Adams's campus drive. That is a plausible counterfactual and it
is also unfalsifiable from the data. The book says "rough estimate" and "might be
attributed to," which is the right hedging. If a chapter ever uses this, the
hedge is the lesson, not the number.

**Table 7.5 must not become a corpus chapter as printed.** It is a table of
logistic coefficients with significance stars, and the corpus does not teach
statistical significance. The *finding* is usable and can be said in plain words
without any of that: **in all four races, where a voter lived predicted their
vote; in three of the four, what the voter believed did not.** That sentence
carries the whole result and needs no p-value.

**A discrepancy to carry:** the text says 18 percent could name all candidates;
Table 7.4's House average says 16. Also note the study frame shifts again here —
"fifty-three winnable open-seat primaries," of which three had one candidate,
fifty studied, forty-nine actually coded. Combined with the 55/53 issue in
chapter 4, the book uses **55, 53, 50 and 49** for versions of its own N. A
corpus chapter citing any of them has to say which and why. **Chapter 1 resolves
this**: 55 nominations, of which 52 were decided by primaries and one more by a
convention after an inconclusive primary, giving 53 primaries; three of those had
a single candidate.

## Chapter 8. Candidate-Centered Politics (pp. 223–246)

No proposition. This is the chapter that takes the rival theory seriously —
"the most widely accepted alternative view to our own" — and tests it on the
cases that do not fit the anchoring model.

It opens with Jacobson's (2013, 23) account of the Daley machine as the last of
the old order. When the congressman for Illinois's 5th died in 1975, state
representative John Fary, 65, "was called into Mayor Richard J. Daley's office
. . . he thought the mayor was going to tell him it was time to retire. Instead,
he was told he was going to Congress." Fary on election night: "I will go to
Washington to help represent Mayor Daley. For twenty-one years I represented the
Mayor in the legislature, and he was always right." **"When, in 1982, he ignored
the party's request that he retire, he was crushed in the primary."**

### The distinction, stated cleanly

> If candidates typically raise the resources necessary for strong campaigns
> based on **purely personal assets** — their own wealth, celebrity, charisma,
> energy, or likability; friends, family, or others having no political interest
> in the candidate's success; personal reputations, followings, or favorable name
> recognition among voters — the process can be described as candidate-centered.
> If instead candidates typically raise campaign resources from **group actors
> requiring evidence that the candidate will behave as a committed and competent
> agent** for their interests or agendas, the process is better understood as
> group-centered.

**"We accept much of the candidate-centered view."** Traditional machines have
"all but disappeared"; candidates mostly run their own campaigns; "intense
ambition is nearly always needed to succeed." The disagreement is that ambition
is "only, or even the most important, force."

Eleven candidates are examined: **the seven unanchored winners from chapter 6,
and four non-winners** who ran nearly successful campaigns on their own assets.

### Three exemplars of candidate-centered politics

**Mimi Walters (CA-45R)** — "a textbook exemplar," and the book's longest
on-the-record interview. Her career began when, having left finance to raise
children, she walked into city hall to volunteer and was assigned **directing
float traffic in the annual parade**. After fourteen years of city and state
races, "Walters estimated that her volunteers numbered around one hundred."

- Finance, her natural sector, gave only about **$150,000** — "well short of our
  criterion for anchoring." The New Majority, a moderate Orange County business
  club, held fundraisers generating **about 10 percent of her campaign funds, or
  about $80,000**.
- Asked directly whether she had an anchor: **"I just sort of appealed to
  everybody who gave to Republicans."**
- **The scare-off in her own words.** Within minutes of learning the seat was
  open she began calling — top donors first, then her consultant, then "a gossipy
  blogger whom she (correctly) believed would spread the word further," and then
  her husband. She gathered **more than sixty endorsements** in a month. On the
  rival she most wanted out: "Raise the money, get the endorsements, scare him
  . . . he's a guy, he's going to run against me, and **did he want to lose to a
  girl?** So that's what I had to do, I had to make him see there was not a path
  for him."
- The FEC-reporting arms race again: hearing Maciariello would report $400,000 in
  the first period, she told her fundraiser "I have to have a better number than
  he does." He reported **$425,000 but had loaned himself $200,000**; she raised
  **$428,000 without loaning herself anything**. **"It was psychologically
  important that I had more money than he had."**
- Club for Growth "put out word that it was looking for a candidate to run against
  Walters" over a vehicle-fee vote. She held her ground, supported the fee again,
  and no Club-backed candidate emerged. Both prospective rivals withdrew "because,
  sources said, they were unable to raise money against Walters."
- Result: first in the top-two primary with **45 percent of the overall vote (65
  percent of the Republican vote)**.
- **The authors' reading is the important part:** Walters "fails to fit the
  dominant pattern in which candidates draw major support from an anchoring group,
  but her case does fit our general argument in an important way: **She attracted
  group support by making herself an effective agent for group needs and got
  enough support to discourage other strong candidates from challenging her.**"
  And "her success in discouraging opposition limited voter choice."

**Paul DeMarco (AL-6R)** — "work hard and avoid controversy." Universally
described as the hardest worker in the race: "a hustler, a grinder, a smart guy
. . . who, if there's more than three people meeting in his district about
anything, Paul's there."

- But he would not take risks for anyone. "Paul was known as somebody who was
  very careful with his votes . . . he didn't want to be caught in something."
  One source described "with indignation" a time DeMarco "tried to kill a bill in
  committee, where his opposition was invisible, but supported the bill in a floor
  vote where the media were watching."
- **"He was never somebody where, if it came down to being in the foxhole, he
  would be there with you."**
- And yet he raised **more than $250,000 from mainly business PACs — "one of the
  highest such totals in our study"** — and was the leading overall fundraiser in
  a seven-candidate race, out-raising even the Club for Growth candidate. Finance
  gave about **$360,000 of the $1.9 million** he raised across primary and runoff,
  short of the $400,000 runoff threshold.
- **Three explanations offered for why business funded a non-champion:** some
  business groups want "small, noncontroversial policy benefits, or help with the
  federal bureaucracy," which a risk-averse hard worker supplies reliably; the
  Republican Civil War made him the acceptable establishment option against Chad
  Mathis and Scott Beason, while the alternative establishment candidate Will
  Brooke was "too wealthy" ("all those rich folks up in Mountain Brook [want] to
  tell the rest of us what to do"); and he had a professional fundraiser who "had
  the lists of who to call and knew who to call and how much to ask them for . . .
  and she sat there with Paul day in and day out and just banged through that
  list."
- **"Walters and DeMarco were the only two candidates in our study whom sources
  reported to be using professional fundraisers of this kind. But it was not a
  matter we systematically probed for, and we may have missed other instances."**

**Brian Babin (TX-36R)** — "reaping what you sow." Fourteen years on the **Lower
Neches River Authority**, a state body regulating water-based economic activity
across six counties. Members of that board "not only contributed to Babin's
campaign but hosted fundraisers as well," as did leaders of the Sabine and Trinity
river authorities.

- Business PACs affected by the three river authorities gave about **$90,000, or
  12 percent** of his total spending. **Dentists** — his profession, and he had
  been president of the Texas State Board of Dental Examiners — gave **$270,000**,
  short of $400,000 "but still a hefty contribution."
- His thank-you visits after winning "went to more than one hundred party
  volunteers."
- The contrast with the outgoing member, Steve Stockman, from a Babin supporter:
  "these people around here think he [Stockman] was the best thing since sliced
  cheese, but as a congressman he was horrible . . . He was voting right, but as
  far as taking care of people, he was awful." And: **"He's not gonna be a
  Stockman."** The authors note voters "would have been unlikely to appreciate
  this difference. But the many public figures who endorsed Babin would have."
- A curiosity they cannot explain: opponent Ben Streusand had been president of
  the Texas Mortgage Bankers Association, "yet received little support from that
  sector. The financial industry gave instead to Babin, supplying about **$70,000,
  10 percent** of his total. . . . it may reflect the hostility between the banking
  and the Tea Party movement."
- Babin joined the Freedom Caucus in Washington "but left it two years later over
  what he viewed as the group's pointless obstruction."

**The analysis of all three is the chapter's most useful concession:**

> The fact that we classified them as non-anchored may be partly due to our
> **admittedly arbitrary threshold** for anchoring. **If New Majority had spent as
> much on Mimi Walters as Club for Growth regularly spent on its candidates, or if
> dentists had contributed as much money for Brian Babin as pharmacists did to
> Buddy Carter, we would have classified these races as anchored.**

### Four well-supported but non-anchored candidates

- **Debbie Dingell (MI-12D)** — the dynastic case. Heir to an auto fortune, rose in
  the industry, "changed course early on when she married Democratic Congressman
  John Dingell." Chaired Al Gore's Michigan campaign in 2000; DNC member. The
  *Detroit Free Press* called her "the most visible and influential spouse in
  Michigan history"; a party official in the same article: "If you've got a
  Michigan political issue, at some point, all roads lead through Debbie Dingle
  [sic]."
  - **After five weeks she had raised more than $500,000** and the endorsement of
    "every Democratic mayor in her district and **two hundred other officials**."
  - The donor pattern is worth noting: "Washington-based PACs that may have had
    business with John Dingell's Energy and Commerce Committee were
    well-represented among the early donors, **whereas Detroit unions were mostly
    missing**." Locals came around later.
  - *Politico*: "the depth of campaign and donor support Dingell is already enjoying
    is nearly unprecedented for a first-time candidate, a reminder of the power of a
    family and its name in Washington politics. **A Dingell has served in the House
    for the last 80 years.**"
  - John Dingell also had **$360,000 in leftover campaign funds** usable for her.
  - Her only opponent was an African American activist who had never held office and
    **could spend $8,300**.
  - "**The actor most important to her political position . . . was not a group but
    a personal asset: her powerful husband.**" But she fits partly: from her many
    board and party roles "she would have been extremely well informed about the
    wants of a wide swath of the Democratic coalition."
- **Donna Kim (HI-1D)** — "transactional politics," in the one district unlike all
  the others. Hawaii's Democratic Party absorbs nearly everyone: "There's a
  business-labor connection in Hawaii that is somewhat unique because **we don't
  have a Republican Party**." "Half the Democrats here would be Republicans
  anywhere else." Labor was split; ideological money was split; "national and local
  progressive money stayed on the sidelines because they just don't see anybody."
  - So **lobbyists and bundlers took center stage** — the one case in fifty-five
    where they did.
  - Development-related donors gave **$220,000**, short of the $250,000 threshold.
    **And the authors say they would hesitate even if it had cleared**, for two
    reasons: "money collected and distributed through independent bundlers isn't
    group money in the same way that money from one group (or an alliance of groups
    formed by the groups themselves) is group money"; and donors cared about her
    power as **state senate president**, not about Congress. A source: "she used her
    connections as Senate president to raise money during the session from people who
    had interests before the Senate. . . . **Her donors are exceedingly
    transactional.**"
  - **"Kim thus appears to have been no one's agent but her own."** She led public
    polls most of the campaign before losing to Mark Takai in the final weeks. "It is
    therefore important to recognize her personalistic style of politics as a viable
    alternative to group-centric anchoring."
- **Don Beyer (VA-8D)** — the case that fits neither model. Owner of nine auto
  dealerships and a former chair of the American International Automobile Dealers
  Association — **yet of the $1.6 million he raised, less than $100,000 came from
  auto dealer PACs or dealers themselves.** No group appears in the interviews or
  the FEC reports, and none of it came from personal loans; 60 percent came from
  his home state.
  - The inference: a career in *party* fundraising. Virginia lieutenant governor;
    national treasurer for Howard Dean 2004; finance chair for Mark Warner's 2008
    presidential campaign; chair of the Mid-Atlantic Finance Council of Obama for
    America, "for which he was rewarded with a posting as US ambassador to
    Switzerland and Lichtenstein"; later several terms as **DCCC finance chair**.
  - **"Beyer's run for office does not seem to fit either the candidate-centered or
    group-centered approach particularly well, but this may be partly due to the
    limits of our evidence."** The authors add that sources in this case "are not
    among our strongest."
- **Paul Dietzel (LA-6R)** — "just keep asking." Paul Dickson on him: "This guy is
  a ball of fire, which as a type often fails to make it. Rarely do you see a
  candidate like him . . . extreme extrovert, extremely focused on winning political
  office, very capable of establishing a broad political network, broadly
  gregarious, but only twenty-eight." Dickson's account of being worn down:

  > I told him, "Paul, you're like a recurring dream . . . that just keeps coming
  > back, over and over and over." . . . Every time I turn a corner, there's Paul
  > Dietzel. . . . By the fourth time, I promised to introduce him to all these people
  > in Shreveport and help him have a fundraiser. Why? **Because the SOB came back to
  > me eighteen times. It works. Persistence works.** And it isn't just that I
  > submitted to his persistence, I was impressed by his persistence.

  - Lane Grigsby went the other way, spurning him and **warning his network against
    him**: "I have questioned his trustworthiness to the public and will continue to
    do so."
  - Dietzel still raised close to **$455,000** by the primary, "about one-third of
    the $1,350,000 raised by eventual winner Garret Graves in that period."
  - **The counterfactual is the sharpest challenge to the book's own model:** "If
    Graves, a late entrant, had stayed on the sidelines, Dietzel would have been the
    Republican runoff candidate in a heavily Republican district and almost certainly
    would have been elected to Congress. If this had happened, **it would have been
    on the basis of his personal energy and charm rather than a record of service to
    a group interest, and hence inconsistent with our group-centric model.**"
  - The companion case: **David Kanuth (CA-33D)**, no political experience and no
    visible group support, raised **$750,000 from individuals** — he "started
    [fundraising] with a stack of blank index cards left over from his law school
    days, writing down names of people to contact for donations and then making call
    after call." It brought him **2 percent of the vote and sixth place**.

### Money and name as candidate assets

**Self-funders.** "Seven candidates in our study, all Republicans, were
self-funders" — defined as (a) spending more than any competitor and (b) much of
it from direct self-contribution or self-loan. **Two of the seven won.**

- **Curt Clawson (FL-19)** spent **nearly $4 million** on high-volume TV and won a
  four-candidate field easily. Best known for a Super Bowl ad "in which he
  challenged President Obama to settle their differences in a one-on-one game of
  basketball," juxtaposing Clawson's college basketball shots with "Obama flubbing
  a layup in a pickup game."
- **Vance McAlister (LA-5)**, an oil field millionaire "whom party insiders scorned
  as unfit for the office," caught a break when **the TV stars of *Duck Dynasty*
  endorsed and made an ad for him**. He finished second behind party-backed Neil
  Riser and picked up enough Democratic votes to win the runoff.
- **"Neither McAlister nor Clawson lasted long. McAlister was primaried out in his
  next election, and Clawson retired for family reasons ahead of the 2016
  election."**

**Mark Sanford (SC-1R)** — "the power of reputation." Four House terms, then
governor 2003–2011. His anti-establishment credentials were not in doubt: "On one
day as governor in 2004, he vetoed **106 items** in the Republican state
legislature's budget and was **overridden on 105** of them." Then the Appalachian
Trail / Argentina scandal, a messy divorce, allegations of state money used for
trysts, and attempted impeachment.

- **Sixteen Republican candidates** ran in the 2013 special. Sanford "received no
  endorsements from important groups, PACs, or other politicians in the primary."
  Club for Growth "served as conduit for some contributions to him but did not
  endorse him." The unusually extensive newspaper coverage mentioned no grassroots
  support for him, though it did for runner-up Curtis Bostic.
- **Three of the sixteen outspent him. He won with 37 percent to 13 percent** for
  second place, and won the runoff easily.
- **"Sanford's famous name was an asset but not one that attracted policy demander
  support."**

**Prior reputation, tested.** The authors identify **seven** candidates likely to
have been familiar to their party's primary voters at the start — those who had
previously run as their party's nominee for governor or US Senate, or served in
Congress in an overlapping district, plus Debbie Dingell.

- **Three won easily: Sanford, Dingell, and Tom Emmer.**
- **Four lost:**
  - **Bob Barr (GA-11)** — four House terms, then the **2008 Libertarian
    presidential nominee**. Finished second, lost the runoff **34 to 66** to Barry
    Loudermilk, "whose principal campaign asset was a large volunteer army of local
    activists."
  - **Steve Lonegan (NJ-3)** — lost the 2013 Senate special by 11 points. "began his
    contest with a large polling lead but lost on Election Day by **40 to 60**" to
    Tom MacArthur, who had the county line in both counties.
  - **Marjorie Margolies (PA-13)** — lost **27 to 41** to Brendan Boyle, "whose
    principal campaign asset was the support of a coalition of union locals."
  - **Joe Baca (CA-31)** — six terms, and "hewed closely to ambition theory's
    playbook for cultivating candidate-specific assets. He brought in federal money
    and made sure constituents associated his name with it." But "his misaligned
    policy positions and past behavior made him unacceptable to key party groups,"
    and unlike Sanford **he faced two group-backed opponents**. He finished fifth
    overall and third among Democrats.
- **The two patterns:** "six of the seven candidates finished first or second, a
  much-better-than-average record that is plausibly attributable to the candidates'
  prior reputations with voters"; and **"four of the seven lost their primaries, a
  pattern suggesting that the benefit of prior reputation is only moderately
  important."**
- The open question they hand off: "whether candidate reputation operates directly
  on voters (as the Sanford case suggests) or operates through groups that provide
  candidates the resources they need to win voter support (as the Dingell case
  suggests)."

### Conclusion

**"There is no bright line between candidate-centered and group-centered
candidacies."** Walters, Babin and DeMarco "were not qualitatively different from
anchored candidates. . . . If any of them had raised enough support from a single
group to meet one of our anchoring criteria, we would have comfortably counted
them as anchored."

The proposed continuum: **Garret Graves** (one anchor, the Louisiana maritime
sector) at one end; **Mark Takai** in the middle (anchoring-level support from
*both* a veterans group and a politically active Japanese American community);
**Walters, DeMarco and Babin** near the other end. "It is possible that if we had
full information . . . most anchored candidates fall somewhere in the middle rather
than at the endpoints — and that other candidates, such as Clawson, Dietzel, and
McAlister, are not on the anchoring continuum at all."

Why single anchors dominate, in two parts: the competence and commitment of the
clearest group champions (Graves, Carter, Torres) "are high indeed," and few
candidates can cultivate that for multiple groups; and anchoring "can be an
expensive proposition for groups," so "while groups may happily add some support
to a candidate who is already anchored, the incentive to do so at anchoring scale
may be muted."

**The closing claim, verbatim:**

> Yet the more important point is that candidate-specific assets are mostly not
> enough. The large majority of winning candidates, including even wealthy ones
> (Tom MacArthur in NJ-3R) and those with high name recognition from prior contests
> (Tom Emmer in MN-7R), anchored their campaign in a single group, association of
> groups, or party.
>
> **Our claim, to reiterate, is not that the candidate-centered approach is wrong
> but that it is radically incomplete. If we focus too exclusively on candidates,
> we miss most of the most important action.**

*(Flag: Emmer's district is given as **MN-5** on p. 243 and **MN-7R** on p. 246;
Table 5.2 lists the convention case as **MN-6R**. Three different district numbers
for the same candidate. Emmer in fact won Minnesota's Sixth.)*

### For the corpus

**This chapter is the book auditing its own threshold, and that is its value.**
The authors state outright that Walters and Babin would have been classified as
anchored had New Majority or the dentists given somewhat more — the same
"admittedly arbitrary" $250,000/$400,000 cutoffs from chapter 6. So the headline
85 percent is not a fact about the world so much as a fact about where a line was
drawn, and the authors keep saying so. That is the most honest treatment of a
cutoff anywhere in the book and a good model for how the corpus talks about
thresholds.

**Three cases here are direct FEC-checkable claims** and would make a good
"concentration of receipts" exercise alongside the ch. 3 and ch. 4 examples: Beyer
raised $1.6m with **less than $100,000** from his own industry; Babin's dentists at
$270,000; DeMarco's finance sector at $360,000 of $1.9m. The pattern worth teaching
is that **the sector a candidate comes from is often not the sector that funds
them** — Beyer the car dealer funded by party networks, Streusand the mortgage
banker whose industry funded his opponent.

**The self-funder finding is small, clean and checkable:** seven self-funders, two
won, and both winners were gone within a cycle or two. Self-funding is visible in
FEC data as candidate loans and contributions, so "how do self-funders do?" is a
question the corpus could answer at scale rather than on seven cases.

**And Dietzel is the honest counterexample the authors volunteer against
themselves** — had Graves not entered late, a 28-year-old with energy, no record
and no group would have gone to Congress, "inconsistent with our group-centric
model." A book that prints its own best counterexample is worth citing on that
alone.

## Chapter 9. Republican Insurgency (pp. 247–268)

**Seven tables and a DW-NOMINATE figure.** The most quantitative chapter in the
book, and the one that overlaps most directly with data the corpus already holds.

### The Republican Civil War, in brief

The fault line dates to **TARP, autumn 2008**: "Nearly half of Republican
Representatives refused to support the Troubled Asset Relief Program, leaving
Democratic Representatives to bail out the president's program." The division is
between "Republicans who were comfortable using government for what they took to
be national purposes" and those who "tended to view government aid to business as
a corrupt form of crony capitalism."

- Tea Party Caucus formed in the House, **July 2010**. **Tim Huelskamp** was
  removed from the House Agriculture Committee for opposing Boehner — which "caused
  problems for Huelskamp with farm interests in his district as well as resentment
  among GOP insurgents who have challenged the House leadership in the past."
- 2013: insurgents fell "a few votes short" of blocking Boehner's re-election, then
  forced a **sixteen-day government shutdown** over defunding the ACA.
- *Bloomberg*, under "Republican Civil War Erupts: Business Groups v. Tea Party,"
  quoted **Scott Reed** of the US Chamber: "We are going to get engaged. The need is
  more than ever to elect people who understand the free market and not silliness."
- *MSNBC*, under "Big Business knocks GOP's 'Taliban minority,'" quoted **Dan
  Danner** of the National Federation of Independent Businesses: "There clearly are
  people in the Republican Party at the moment for whom the business community and
  the interests of the business community . . . don't seem to be their top priority.
  They don't really care what the N.F.I.B. thinks, and don't care what the Chamber
  thinks, and probably don't care what the Business Roundtable thinks."
- **"These events occurred immediately before the 2014 primary season."**

### The classification problem — and a finding that lands on `primary-positions`

Candidates would not differentiate themselves. The two issues on which Washington
establishment and insurgents actually disagreed — reappointing Boehner and raising
the debt ceiling — "were infrequently raised on the campaign trail, perhaps because
candidates believed, as we do, that few voters were attuned to issues of intraparty
difference." Invoking the Tea Party "seemed to mean little"; "outsider" "likewise
failed to reliably convey insurgent orientation."

The AL-6 candidate forum is the illustration: seven Republicans spent ninety
minutes "agreeing with each other about flattening income tax rates, cutting the
deficit, repealing and replacing Obamacare, stopping new federal regulations, and
rolling back old ones, and eliminating the Education Department." Then a moderator
asked whether any supported Common Core: **"None of the candidates raised a hand or
voiced support, and the silence quickly gave way to applause from the audience, the
only such interruption of the event."** Three of those seven "did in fact have
disagreements with the other two. But by this journalistic account, they failed to
go public with the differences."

**And then the sentence that should be quoted in `primary-positions`:**

> To develop usable classifications of the factional orientations of candidates,
> **we examined candidate websites but were disappointed by them. Reports of policy
> positions were sometimes present, sometimes not. Even when present, the
> information was unvetted and sometimes contradictory.**

Having given up on websites, they turned to **group support** as the indicator —
"the groups were better positioned than we were to know their candidates, and they
often had histories that made their own orientations knowable as well."

**Choice of groups.** Following *National Journal* reporter Scott Bland, who
described the Madison Project, FreedomWorks and the Senate Conservatives Fund as
"what you might call the tea-party establishment." The insurgent set used here:
**Madison Project, FreedomWorks, Senate Conservatives Fund, Club for Growth**
(small-government and libertarian insurgency) plus **Family Research Council and
Eagle Forum** (socially conservative insurgency), and **local Tea Party groups**.
Associated with "the Koch brothers, Phyllis Schlafly, James Dobson, Jim DeMint."

For the establishment side, only the **Republican Main Street Partnership**
reliably endorsed that type, and only in a handful of races. So they used
**business PAC support, with a $50,000 threshold**, justified because "(1) it was a
difficult threshold to reach in contributions limited to $5,000 per business in the
many races in which there were no independent expenditures, and (2) it represented
a significant amount of support." **Contributions from individual businesspeople
were not counted.**

### Expanding to non-winners

- **138 losing Republican candidates.** They "attracted fewer high-profile
  endorsements . . . less news coverage, and less attention from sources in our field
  interviews. **A few didn't have websites.**"
- Restricted to non-winners with **at least 10 percent** of the primary vote (or
  treated as top contenders at the Missouri and Utah conventions): **68 of the 138
  failed that cutoff**, leaving **70**.
- Of those 70: **24** classified by the same criteria as winners; **3 more** via
  Liberty Party support = **27 group-based classifications**.
- For the remaining 43 they "gave up on finding any common yardstick and used
  whatever information we could find from any source" — websites, news reports of
  candidate statements, biographies, field interviews, advertisements. This produced

### Conditions of insurgent success

**What did *not* matter.** "We found **no significant associations** with the
partisanship of districts as measured by the Cook Partisan Voting Index (PVI),
socio-economic status of districts as measured by education and income levels,
whether there was provision for a runoff, and whether districts were in the South.
Nor did any of these factors have an association with insurgents winning nomination."

**Two things that did.**

**1. The top-two rule, negatively.** California, Louisiana and Washington had
variations of it "in a total of five districts that leaned Republican. Only one
Republican insurgent ran in any of these contests, Clint Didier in WA-4" — who led
the first stage and lost the runoff. **"So while, as we saw above, insurgents won in
34 percent of all nominations, they went zero for five in top-two contests."** The
authors connect this to reformers' intent: "One reason reformers favored the top-two
rule was the hope that it would make it harder for extreme candidates to win election
even in districts safe for their party. Research evidence has been mixed, but the
results of our five cases are consistent with reformers' hopes."

**2. Coordination, negatively — twice over.**

**"Insurgents lost 92 percent (11/12) of the time when there was coordination and won
73 percent of the time when there was not. Both relationships are too large to be
easily explained as chance variation."**

**The causal problem, stated by the authors themselves:** "One might conclude from
table 9.6 that coordinated actors were responsible for the defeat of insurgents, but
it is also possible that **coordinated actors simply flocked to candidates likely to
win anyway**. . . . The statistical association in table 9.6 offers no clue as to
whether coordination caused insurgent defeats or strong non-insurgent candidates
caused support to coordinate behind them."

Their answer is qualitative. **TABLE 9.7** summarizes the eleven cases where
coordination was present and the non-insurgent won, giving a one-line background for
each (AL-1, FL-13, IA-3, MI-4, MI-8, MO-8, NC-7, NJ-3, NY-4, VA-10, WA-4). **In eight
of the eleven, "the coordinators supported a candidate who was at best uncertain of
winning and more often facing significant danger of defeat."** Two more were
convention delegates who "could choose anyone they wanted." **"In only one case, that
of NY-4R, did the local party choose a seasoned politician likely to defeat his
insurgent opponent under any circumstances."** So: "the evidence in table 9.7 tends to
strengthen the view that coordinated actors support candidates **because they want to
help them win**, rather than supporting them because they are likely to win anyway."

Selected Table 9.7 entries worth keeping: *NC-7* — "NRCC support for establishment
candidate David Rouzer came early and aimed at defeating a Democratic incumbent who
had beaten Rouzer in 2012. When the Democrat retired, Rouzer was left with a large war
chest." *VA-10* — "Fearing that an insurgent would win the primary and lose the general
election, local party leaders pressured a strong insurgent to drop out and arranged a
firehouse primary to stymie a weaker one." *MI-8* — "Two establishment candidates made
a pact for one, Bryan Barnett, to drop out as a means of consolidating the
establishment vote."

**The two caveats the authors flag, and the first is serious:**

> First, **our processes for identifying whether coordination was present and whether
> a candidate was an insurgent were not independent — both emerged from the same
> unstructured research process. We acknowledge the potential for bias in this
> situation.** Second, even if coordination did work against insurgent candidates in
> our period of study, it would not necessarily always do so.

Why insurgents did so well without coordination (11 of 15): **grassroots volunteers**.
"In five of the ten cases in question, insurgent candidates tapped grassroots networks
to power their campaigns."

### Democratic insurgency?

"There was no 'Democratic Civil War' comparable to the self-conscious and nationally
networked insurgency on the Republican side." The nearest equivalent was the
**Progressive Campaign Change Committee (PCCC)**, whose 2014 website "described itself
as having **950,000 members** and as 'fighting for bold progressive change.'"

PCCC endorsed four Democratic winners: **Ruben Gallego (AZ-7), Pat Murphy (IA-1),
Bonnie Watson Coleman (NJ-12), and Carl Sciortino (MA-5)**. But each is qualified —
Gallego "courted business and was distrusted as an opportunist by some progressives";
Murphy "narrowly escaped attack ads from an EMILYs List affiliate for past compromises
on abortion rights"; Watson Coleman "did not consider herself a progressive until she
realized from the PCCC's interest in her candidacy that she was fully progressive in
her views." *(The text then says "All three of these candidates were on the left wing"
after naming four — a slip.)*

### Conclusion: the larger insurgency

The through-line: TARP (2008) → Tea Party (2009) → the 2010 elections → "the rise of
Donald Trump and his Make America Great Again (MAGA) movement after 2015. The twelve
insurgents who won nomination and election to the House in the 2013–2014 cycle are
part of this train of events."

**The chapter's headline claim:**

> The foremost lesson about the larger insurgency is that **it can be stopped**.
> Insurgent candidates lost eleven of twelve nominations in districts in which they
> faced coordinated opposition, with the one insurgent win coming in unusual
> circumstances. But these are victories over insurgents **before they attain office**,
> before most voters know much about them; defeats of insurgent officeholders —
> getting the proverbial genie back into the bottle — may be a different matter.

On Trump: before the McGovern-Fraser reforms, "the American party system was able to
contain populist insurgents like Trump, most notably **Henry Ford in the 1920s and Huey
Long in the 1930s**, neither of whom committed to run for president despite obvious
interest in doing so. **If the 1970s reforms had not turned the power of nomination
over to rank-and-file voters in primaries and caucuses, Trump's 2016 nomination might
have been stopped by party insiders as well.**"

And the counterfactual for the House: **"the Republican House cohort that emerged from
the 2013–2014 cycle would have had a quite different political complexion except for
establishment-leaning coordination acting against both the emergence of potential
insurgent candidates and the success of actual ones."**

### For the corpus

**This chapter connects directly to `04-political-actors/dw-nominate`, and the link is
the second dimension.** The corpus's existing chapter teaches DW-NOMINATE's first
dimension as the left–right summary. Chapter 9 uses the **second** dimension — the one
that "picks up divisions within the parties" — and shows it separating Freedom Caucus
members from business-backed Republicans. Same Voteview file (Lewis et al.), same
scores, a dimension the corpus currently ignores. A chapter on "the axis nobody plots"
is sitting right there, and Figure 9.1's design — truncating dimension one at zero
because no Republican scored liberal — is itself a teachable framing decision.

**Table 9.1 is the most checkable table in the book.** Thirty-three named candidates,
each with a district and a single dollar figure for business PAC support. Every one of
those numbers is recomputable from FEC bulk data. And the finding is stark enough to
survive rounding: **median insurgent $7,250, median establishment $130,800** — an
eighteen-fold gap. A corpus chapter could reproduce the table and then ask what the
$50,000 threshold is doing, since French Hill at $38,250 was reclassified by hand and
Loudermilk at $60,500 was classified the other way.

**Tables 9.5 and 9.6 are 2×2 tables of counts, and the authors read them the right
way.** They state the association, then say plainly that it "offers no clue as to
whether coordination caused insurgent defeats," then go to the case histories to
adjudicate. That is a model of how to talk about a cross-tabulation without teaching
significance, and it is directly usable given the corpus's rule against inference
teaching. The phrase "too large to be easily explained as chance variation" is the only
place they lean on sampling logic, and it can be dropped without losing anything.

**The admission in the caveats is the most important methodological sentence in the
chapter** — coordination and insurgency "were not independent; both emerged from the
same unstructured research process." The book's central quantitative finding rests on
two variables coded by the same people in the same pass, and they say so. If a corpus
chapter ever cites the 92 percent, that sentence goes with it.

**One more line for `primary-positions`:** the authors tried to classify candidates
from their websites and gave up, because the information was "sometimes present,
sometimes not . . . unvetted and sometimes contradictory." That is an independent
research team reaching the same conclusion about the same kind of source in the same
cycle that the Brookings coders were working in — and it is a better citation for that
chapter's argument than anything in ch. 7.

## Chapter 10. Who Gets Represented? (pp. 269–298)

No proposition, and the authors say why: "group representation was not a central
focus of our field research, [so] the analysis we offer in this chapter is more
tentative than elsewhere and includes no new proposition. Our purpose here is only
to introduce an important subject." **It nonetheless contains Table 10.1, which
lists all fifty-five races with an anchor and a motive — the most joinable object
in the book.**

### The one direct observation of representation

The chapter opens with **Buddy Carter, seven and a half years after taking
office**, on the record, asked whether he had been "a representative for a pharmacy
interest."

> **[CARTER].** First thing I did when I got to Congress was to approach the FTC
> and ask them about the vertical integration that exists in our health care system
> with the insurance companies all being pharmacy benefit managers (PBMs) that own
> the pharmacies as well. And that vertical integration needs to be broken up.

He got onto the Health Subcommittee of Energy and Commerce "because I'm a
pharmacist," said fourteen of his bills had passed, and described his method:
**"You keep hollering, as loud as you can. And you keep pushing forth
legislation."** In 2024 the FTC sued three large PBMs "over their tactics for
negotiating prices for drugs," and the House passed two Carter bills — **H.R. 1613,
the Drug Price Transparency in Medicaid Act, and H.R. 2679, the Pharmacy Benefits
Manager Accountability Act.**

**The authors' reading is the theoretically important part:** "It is doubtful that
many of Carter's constituents back home cared much about these bills, and since he
had divested himself of pharmacy interests, he would not have profited financially
even if they had passed. His involvement in them thus makes a good example of what
principals . . . might expect from a good agent — **someone who would act from an
internalized commitment to a group agenda even when he probably would not have been
sanctioned for failing to do so.**"

And the admission immediately after: **"Carter's involvement in representing
pharmacy interests in Washington is our only direct observation of a kind of
behavior that our theoretical orientation would expect to be commonplace."** One
observation, for the mechanism the whole book implies.

### The six motives, and the party-machine cases

The motive taxonomy: **winning the general election; controlling local government;
party interest; group benefit; ideology or values; district interest.**

**NY-4R, Nassau County** is the fullest portrait of a surviving patronage machine.
A longtime observer: "It's not an ideological local party . . . They would say they
care about quality of life in the county, keeping taxes down, keeping streets safe
. . . But it's also a financial way of life . . . **thousands of [government] jobs
and contracts at stake.**"

- **A campaign army exceeding 1,000 workers**; a second source put it at **2,500**.
  "The idea is that [the system] benefits everyone — you get your job, and by us
  winning more seats, we have more jobs available, you have more opportunity to
  rise."
- Contracts to lawyers, engineers, bondsmen, management consultants, construction
  firms, printers. "There's a lot of ways to make money without breaking the laws,
  and the people who are around the longest learn how to do that if money is their
  motivation." Nassau "has gained attention for the proliferation of special
  districts to create opportunities for contracts," including — "decades in the past
  but still pertinent in the mind of our first source" — **"the formation of a
  special tax district to finance and build escalators at a single suburban rail
  station."**
- **The key line:** the party **"cares less about who the Congressman is than for
  any other office . . . because there's no patronage in Congress."**
- Their choice, Bruce Blakeman, self-loaned about **$1 million**. His Tea Party
  opponent had "no chance whatsoever because primaries are organizational efforts
  . . . no way he could ever pull a vote large enough to win a primary if the leader
  of the organization is not only backing your opponent but regards you as a personal
  affront." He was "just one guy screaming."

**NJ-3R** shows the same logic with a twist. "There's thirty thousand more Democrats
in the county, but Republicans control county government here, **so we're very
moderate on the social issues.**" The party volunteered its record on diversity:
"we have a woman sheriff now, we had a Black sheriff before her, we have a Black
freehold director, we have a Hispanic woman in the [state] assembly now, we have two
women Senators in our county." **The authors' observation on that list is sharp:**
"Notably absent from this list were minority or women candidates sponsored for the
county Board of Commissioners, which oversees county government. **'Everything comes
down to the courthouse.'**"

**PA-6R** adds a detail worth keeping: a "very loyal donor base," a committee whose
**420 members** campaign for the slate, and a decision process that was "highly
centralized: The party's choice of House nominee was made by agreement between two
top party officials, both of whom wanted the party endorsement but **one of whom
deferred to the other for, we were told, family reasons.**"

**MO-8R** is the convention case. Missouri law convened an **84-member committee**;
**thirteen candidates** sought the nomination, including the state party chair, the
lieutenant governor and two former statewide officeholders. **Jason Smith won on the
sixth ballot.** Three assets: he "visit[ed] with each of the [84] committee members
at least twice, putting about **10,000 miles** on his car in the process"; he chaired
the Missouri branch of **ALEC**; and at **thirty-four he was the youngest** major
candidate — "two of the sources pointed to Smith's relative youth as the critical
factor. The district had a history of long-serving representatives and wanted to
continue it."

**UT-4R, Mia Love**, is the best convention-mechanics account in the book. The
convention had about **a thousand delegates, and Love spoke to six to eight hundred
of them** one-on-one or in groups. A source: **"We called it her 'turn rate.' When
she met people, they switched."** She entered with a poll showing about **54 percent**
and needed **60 percent** to win the nomination without a primary; **her speech took
her to 70 percent.** On her policy depth: "in one discussion captured on video, it
became apparent that she did not know the meaning of 'insider trading.'" And: "Mia on
the stump speech was fine, but on the sit-down intense discussion about Medicare
reform or something, she just wasn't there . . . that left donors with a bad taste
frequently." **"But the lack of policy chops was no impediment to winning over
delegates."**

> We thus view groups in both these categories as being on the same footing: **The

### Party stakeholders versus selective participators

A second cut: **party stakeholders** (regularly involved) versus **selective
participators** (active only for a particular candidate). "Stakeholders seem likely
to have more friends in Congress — and hence better representation — than selective
participators."

The classification, summarized:

- **Fifteen** local or national party organizations — seven Middle Atlantic
  committees, four local parties operating by convention, the DCCC (three
  nominations), and national Republican officials who oversaw one candidate swap.
- **Elected party officials in five primaries** acting behind the scenes (MI-4, MI-8,
  CA-11, CA-25, IL-2).
- **National groups anchored ten nominations** — unions, EMILYs List, Club for
  Growth, other national conservatives. "That some of these groups back insurgent
  candidates does not undermine their stakeholder status."
- **Faith-based and Tea Party volunteers anchored five**, "each in response to
  mobilization by a particular candidate, marking them as **selective
  participators**."
- **Pharmacists and dentists → selective participators.** The evidence is neat:
  "**Strikingly, pharmacy donors gave only slight support to Ken Reed, a pharmacist
  who finished second in the WV-2 primary.**" So they were backing Carter, not
  pharmacists in general.
- **Ethnic support splits both ways:** the organized Japanese American community in
  HI-1 "has historically helped lead the state's Democratic Party" → stakeholder;
  Asian American individual contributions to Ted Lieu showed no sign of being "more
  than one-off support for a co-ethnic candidate" → selective participator.
- **Agriculture and banking → stakeholders**, each having backed two Republican
  nominations and given substantially in other primaries.
- **Mel Sembler** and his IPO colleagues → stakeholders. **Lane Grigsby** "made a
  point of saying in his interview that he felt no personal commitment to the
  Republican Party as such, but his frequent participation in its primaries mark him
  as a party stakeholder."
- **Ryan Zinke's SOFA** → stakeholder, on an FEC-checkable basis: it made
  **$250,000** in independent expenditures for Zinke in 2014 and **averaged about
  $425,000 in independent expenditures for other Republican candidates in cycles from
  2016 to 2022, according to the FEC.**

### What groups actually want — five categories

1. **Elite constituent service.** A Republican operative on what corporate PACs
   expect: **"The national guys don't give money for the policy . . . They give money
   so if one of their employees in Malaysia on a drilling rig can't get his passport
   together, they can call and get it fixed . . . It's the personal relationships."**
   Asked whether any member would do this: **"Not at the same level. They want curb
   service. They want to be able to call them and get an answer right then. Not the
   staffer, the person [MC] himself."** More simply, on choosing between two equally
   qualified candidates: **"He would take my phone calls."**
2. **Access.** A lobbyist: "I get access . . . I can call people [officeholders] up
   and I can say, 'I've got this person, she'd like to come and see you, would you
   see us' . . . If I'm there for twenty, thirty, forty years helping, and I ask,
   yeah, I should get some access." A liberal pro-union Democrat drew the line
   carefully: "I'd expect them to give me a decent hearing on that, and then tell me,
   'I can't help you' or 'I can' . . . **I don't expect them to give me their vote. I
   expect them to give me a fair hearing on an issue. That's it.**" A Republican
   insider used the same word to mean more: business donors want "my position
   expressed to the elected official with a reasonable chance that it's going to be
   listened to **and followed**."
3. **Championing a cause.** The Massachusetts liberal group leader: "We look for
   champions — folks who will not only vote for us, but will be actively vocal about
   their support . . . and make efforts to encourage their colleagues to support."
4. **Problem-solving agents (the trustee model).** A Republican fundraiser: "I want a
   candidate that is capable of coming up with solutions, **not necessarily with my
   input**. . . . I obviously can't spend enough time to develop a solution to the
   national healthcare problem, but I'm willing to see him get hired to do that."
   EMILYs List was "mainly but not entirely committed to the trustee model" — fealty
   on abortion, but otherwise "sending women to Congress was all it needed to do."
   The example given: Kirsten Gillibrand, "who, as a woman, has a unique voice on
   military sexual assault and will not tolerate it going through the military old
   boys' club. If she were a progressive man, I don't know if he would have stood up.
   In fact, we saw a lot of progressive men not stand up to the military on this."
5. **Grigsby's own account**, given over two interviews and unusually full. He
   insisted he is not a "kingpin": **"We don't have to be in lockstep on *anything* in
   order for me to support you. [Other groups say] 'This is what you're going to vote
   for or I'm not going to give you the money. Promise me before I give it to you.' I
   don't. Go be a good person. Follow your heart, do what's right for people. That's
   the most I can expect from anybody."**
   - The story he told to prove it, about a state legislator on a union bill Grigsby
     opposed: after three conversations, "Friday morning he called me at the house at
     6:30, he said, 'I made my mind up, Lane, I gotta tell you this. You know my dad
     was in the railroad, every Friday he came in, put his paycheck down at the supper
     table' — Tom was very religious — 'and we prayed, and my father always thanked the
     labor union that we had a paycheck that we could afford to feed our family with. I
     can't forget my father's memory, Lane; I'm sorry, but I have to vote the other
     way.' **Quite all right, [I said].**"
   - And a fact that cuts against reading Graves as captured: the **Environmental
     Defense Action Fund**, "a respected pro-environment group, supported him with an
     independent expenditure of about **$110,000**, which was more than 10 percent of
     all spending on Graves's behalf."

### Who doesn't get represented?

**This is the chapter's most important section and the one most useful to the
corpus.**

A Republican mayor on his own advantage: "In [my city], Rotary is a very key group,
meets every Tuesday. **Ninety people come every Tuesday — some of the most
influential bankers, business owners, and retired doctors.** It's a great network,
it's a really good network. You can get a lot of support by going to the Rotary
meeting on Tuesday . . . and walk away with cash and support and lawn sign locations
and promises to host events at their house or their business."

**The extended case is CA-31D and the Latino community** — in a **majority-Latino
district** that nominated a Latino candidate, and where Latino voters still went
largely unrepresented in the nomination process.

- Pete Aguilar, mayor of middle-class Redlands, was seen by some as "insufficiently
  attentive to the large number of working-class Latinos in the district." Even a
  strong supporter: "He didn't have a lot of North Side support. There's a large
  Latino population over there. **[Why?] Because he ignored them.**" His forums,
  "Coffee with the Council," were held on his side of town: "the North Side people
  . . . they've got this culture where they don't venture much outside of their area.
  **You have to go to them. And he should have.**"
- **The DCCC's coordinating breakfast is the structural finding:** "While the meeting
  included representatives from unions and local Democratic clubs, **it had no one
  specifically representing the Latino community.**" And even a Latino activist who
  objected to the DCCC's role "could not recall any other groups that were regularly
  involved in primaries. 'Those are really the key ones . . . the active parties and
  the unions.'"
- **Why the community was absent from electoral politics:** "There were Latino
  organizations in the district, but they focused on **direct community service, such
  as help with immigration problems, not on campaigns and elections.**" A Democratic
  club member: "Our club has tried to recruit, to become more diverse. **We don't know
  who the leaders are on the North Side. If there even are any**, because they are not
  real active in the community. Or maybe we just don't know what they are doing."
- **The barriers are mundane and concrete** — websites inaccurate, emails unanswered,
  "a complicated multistage endorsement system," and unwritten timelines. A club
  insider on a Latina who showed up once: "she got the club vote, but there might have
  been twenty people at the meeting, so that's maybe twenty votes. **You have to be
  willing to work hard.**"
- **And then the entry cost, which is the sharpest fact in the chapter:**

  > **[To run] for City Council, you have to be able to put up thirty to fifty K,
  > maybe sixty.** . . . A friend who ran for City Council, she took out a second
  > mortgage on her house. Now I wouldn't do that.
  >
  > Anyone running for city council has to have a lot of flexibility to show up for a
  > meeting any time. Usually they are self-employed or a lawyer. **If you are a
  > teacher or have a nine-to-five job, it's hard. So we don't have many minorities on
  > our council. Or in our [Democratic] club.**

- **The mechanism named:** "One can see a **vicious circle** whereby potential
  candidates from poorer and less educated communities are discouraged by the
  financial and time demands of running for entry-level office. Without candidates
  from their own ranks, community organizations engage less in campaigns and electoral
  politics than they otherwise would. The disengagement of community groups increases
  the barriers faced by individual Latino candidates."
- **"The advantage enjoyed by the prosperous and politically active is a feature of
  the politics of House nominations in both parties. The less advantaged on the
  Republican side were often less prosperous whites, and on the Democratic side less
  prosperous minorities."**
- Who was addressing it: **groups, not party organizations.** The Kochs' **Americans
  for Prosperity** — the Arkansas chapter had "a professional staff of six" and was
  "one of thirty state AFP chapters." And **EMILYs List**, which recruited **Eloise
  Gomez Reyes** against the DCCC's candidate. A Democratic Club leader on Reyes:
  "Eloise had a lot going for her. But she had not been involved in the community. She
  had not been an activist. **I didn't see her in clubs, at the conventions.**" With
  EL's help she built a credible campaign and "**also brought several Latino
  organizations into her campaign and perhaps into closer contact with the Democratic
  Party.**" She lost, then won a state legislative seat and "rose to the position of
  majority leader of the California Assembly."

### Conclusion

> The vast majority of studies of political representation focus on the relationship
> between voter characteristics and positions taken by their representatives. . . .
> This chapter shows that there is **another huge side to representation — the policy
> demands of the groups that sponsor the candidates among whom voters choose.**

Credited first to **Angela Ocampo (2018)** — a coauthor of this book — on Latino
representation in majority-Latino districts:

> In directly influencing who enters and wins an open-seat in a majority-Latino
> district, parties and interest groups determine the type of advocacy that
> constituents will eventually receive. **Even if districts achieve Latino descriptive
> representation, substantive representation will depend on which candidate was
> vetted, what issues the candidate prioritizes and which group or network the newly
> elected representative has to reciprocate to** (195).

### For the corpus

**Table 10.1 is the join key for everything else in this book.** Fifty-five rows,
each with a district-party code, a named winner, an anchor, and a motive. Combined
with Table 5.2 (32 coordination cases), Table 6.2/6.3 (anchoring), and Table 9.1
(business PAC dollars per candidate), the book effectively publishes a small
candidate-level dataset across four tables that share the same key. Assembling those
four into one tidy table — 55 rows, ~6 columns — would take an afternoon and would
be a genuinely new artifact, since the authors never print it that way. **That is the
single highest-value thing anyone could build from this book.**

**The "who doesn't get represented" section is the strongest candidate for a corpus
chapter that is about people rather than filings.** The entry cost of a city council
race — **"thirty to fifty K, maybe sixty"** — plus the requirement to "show up for a
meeting any time," is a measurable barrier with public data behind it: municipal
campaign finance filings exist in many states, and the occupational composition of
local officeholders is documented. The book supplies the mechanism (a vicious circle
between candidate supply and organizational engagement) and a case where a
**majority-Latino district** produced a nomination process with **no one at the table
representing Latinos**. That is descriptive-versus-substantive representation stated
in a way a first-year can follow, and the Ocampo quote states it exactly.

**A caution on Table 10.2 that the authors themselves supply:** the motive
classifications rest on "sparse evidence," and the group-benefit/ideology split is one
the authors then argue is not really a distinction. So the headline "67 percent" is
two categories the book says belong on "the same footing." Cite the 37, not the split.

**And the one-observation problem is worth teaching on its own.** The book's entire
principal-agent framework predicts that anchored members serve their anchors in
office — and the authors got exactly **one** direct observation of it, Buddy Carter on
PBMs, seven and a half years later and on the record. They say so plainly. A theory
with fifty-five nomination cases and one representation case is a good illustration of
how much easier it is to observe who *gets* nominated than what nomination *does*.

## Chapter 11. Parties on the Ground (pp. 299–317)

The conclusion, and **the most self-critical chapter in the book.** Its middle
section audits each of the five propositions and names the weakest evidence behind
each. Anyone citing this book should read this chapter first.

### Policy demander parties

Following V. O. Key, the party has three parts: the party **in government**, the
party **in the electorate**, and the party **as organization**. Modern scholarship
has focused on the first two, and when it studies the third it has taken "a narrow
view, focused on formal, official organizations with 'Democratic' or 'Republican' in
their titles. . . . **Interest and values groups, even when recognized as stalwarts of
their parties, are conventionally omitted from the third leg.**"

The critique of the two leading textbook treatments:

- **Hershey (2021, 195)** describes groups involving themselves in campaigns "to
  achieve their political goals" — but "because she views groups as distinct from
  parties, Hershey does not explore this competition and cooperation in any depth."
- **Aldrich, *Why Parties?*** — "The party is in service to its candidates and
  officeholders . . . this is a party designed around the ambitions of effectively
  autonomous politicians" (1995, 289). Aldrich does allow a role for groups —
  nominations "are controlled by the strongest and most effective of the candidate's
  personal campaign organizations and by those ambitious benefit seekers who head
  interest groups, PACs and movements" (1995, 295) — but "this statement has the feel
  of an afterthought in a lengthy book that says little about interest groups or
  policy demands."

**The programmatic claim:**

> The proper course of scholarship in this situation is not, we think, to continue to
> study the **half-empty formal structures that once did the work of parties**, but to
> study the actors who are now doing what classical party structures once did.

And a historical corrective worth keeping: **Mayhew's (1986) analysis of Traditional
Party Organizations "suggests that even at their late nineteenth-century peak, they
were active in only a third of the country's counties."** So the golden age of
machine parties was never national.

Key (1965, 329) on the third leg: "The party organization constitutes no disciplined
army. It consists rather of many state and local **points of power**, each with its
own local following and each comparatively independent of external control." The
authors adopt that phrase for "the business, labor, Super PAC (EMILYs List, Club for
Growth), and professional groups that **anchored perhaps 40 percent of our
nominations.**"

**The boundary condition they set for themselves:** "Groups that jump back and forth
between parties with no stable commitment would not be, by our definition, part of a
party. **We saw no such behavior**, however." Veterans split by organization —
"Democratic veterans contributed through VoteVets and Republican veterans through
Special Operations for America." The two non-aligned professional associations (the
American Dental Association for Babin, the American Society of Anesthesiologists for
Arkoosh) backed candidates who "had strong party connections" anyway. **"If we had
observed groups that regularly anchored non-partisan agents of their agenda in party
primaries, we would need to fundamentally reconsider our view of a party as a
coalition of policy demanders. But the system we observed was not like that."**

### The audit of the five propositions

**1. Groups and ideology.** Only **three** of fifty-five contests had leading actors
primarily motivated by ideology — **MN-6R, NJ-12D, UT-4R**.

> **We acknowledge, however, that this argument rests on a foundation of selected
> examples and narratives, and that systematic evidence across our fifty-five cases is
> absent. No shortcoming in our study is more serious than the lack of systematic
> evidence for the paramount importance of group agendas as drivers of House
> nominations.**

And a second admission, about how the central concept was built — **the best
methodological passage in the book:**

> As with other concepts in this study, **we did not begin with a clear definition of
> group in mind. Rather, we developed our concept as we went along** as a means of
> organizing what we were seeing. This approach may be compared to running large
> numbers of regressions to find the particular combination of variables that best
> explains the outcome variable. The danger in the regression context is that
> inferences will give too much weight to chance factors in the data; **the danger in
> fine-tuning a concept as part of the research process is likewise overfitting.** But
> at the same time, endogenous concept-building is what exploratory studies like ours
> must do.

The question they leave open: "How much behavior in other domains of politics is
routinely explained by ideology in the absence of an examination that could reveal
the existence of an effectively represented group demand or, more likely, **a logroll
over many group demands**?"

**2. Vetting and vouching.** The counterfactual: absent VVNs, "**More likely would be
a system in which reliable candidate information was absent, one more likely to
select officials like the disgraced congressman George Santos.**" But: **"the evidence
for our basic claims about vetting and vouching remains limited and anecdotal. We
have been able to plausibly describe how these networks matter in some campaigns, but
we have found nothing systematic."** The open empirical questions they pose — did
every candidate really work donor lists methodically? what is the return on warm
calls versus cold? would medical professionals generally have supported Arkoosh as
readily as fellow anesthesiologists? — are all unanswered.

**3. Coordination.** 32 of 55, 58 percent — "Most of these involved formal party
institutions (sixteen) or informal arrangements among party-linked actors (eleven)."
**"We found only two nominations in which a candidate anchored by coordination
efforts lost to one who was not."**

The self-criticism is direct: they specified four distinct forms of coordination.
**"Is the presence of so many forms an indication of overfitting that we should be
guarding against? Perhaps."** And on the WA-4 "civic leaders" who coordinate by
"talking all the time": "Eyebrows may be raised at counting this behavior as a form
of coordination. . . . our classification is based on the say-so of **only one
source**, albeit a high-quality one. **Yet we would bias our conclusions if we were to
include only readily measurable evidence of our key concepts and overlook other
evidence. We regard it as a strength of this study that our methods enabled us to
recognize kinds of evidence that more targeted methods might miss.**"

**4. Anchoring — and the reverse-causation problem.** "The anchoring proposition is
the capstone of our core theoretical claim." The threat is stated plainly: groups may
back winners rather than make them, and **"the tendency of groups to ask potential
endorsees to describe a credible 'path to victory' makes the possibility of reverse
causation especially worrisome."**

Four qualitative rebuttals:

- **EMILYs List** endorsed in 11 of 20 Democratic primaries, gave anchor-level support
  to 7, won 4. "Some of these candidates were neophytes or had performed weakly in
  previous races." An EL insider on the reaction to one endorsement: **"People were
  calling . . . saying, 'You've got the wrong candidate here, this woman's not good
  enough.' But with EL's help, the woman won. EL was the most active anchor in our
  study, and its profile is generally not that of a bandwagoner."**
- **Faith-based volunteers** anchored two ministers, Hice (GA-10) and Walker (NC-6).
  Hice had run before and lost; Walker had never run. **"No one could have known in
  advance that these men of the cloth would score solid wins."**
- **The US Chamber** made a six-figure contribution to Byrne in AL-1 "when they were
  told that he was in danger of losing to an insurgent. **The chamber wasn't
  bandwagoning to a winner; it was bailing out a friend.**"
- **MI-4 party activists** joined Moolenaar "when he had a double-digit deficit in the
  polls."

**"We acknowledge that these examples have been selected to make our point, but we
aver that it would not be possible to select a comparably clear set of examples for
the opposite pattern."**

Three general reasons anchoring is a cause rather than an effect: contributions were
**large** ("If anchoring groups wanted merely to ingratiate themselves to likely
winners, they could have contributed much less than they did"); where demanders found
a strong candidate unacceptable **they tried to defeat them** — Swati Dandekar
(IA-1), Paul Mitchell (MI-4), Steve Lonegan (NJ-3), all of whom "began their races as
apparently strong candidates but were defeated by groups that rallied against them"
and **"we know of no cases in which they made large political contributions to
opponents"**; and the heavy vetting effort "would have been pointless if groups
intended to bandwagon for the winner."

**5. Voters.** The two most useful props — "party label and the incumbent's record —
are absent in open House primaries." But not zero capacity: voters "can sensibly use
ballot information to recognize and perhaps support co-ethnic and women candidates
and sometimes also candidates from their hometown or county," and PA-13 shows
occasional policy voting. **The scope condition matters:** "we do not maintain that
voter performance in open-seat House primaries is indicative of performance in other
kinds of primaries, **including challenge primaries against sitting House
incumbents**."

### Why no proposition about candidates?

**The interview imbalance, stated as a number: 18 candidate interviews against 110
with policy demanders.** "Is this imbalance a problem? It could be. A more balanced
interview schedule would certainly have been better."

Their defense: "Candidates, speaking mostly anonymously but sometimes on the record,
did not noticeably differ in their accounts of nominations compared to those of other
sources, except for sometimes offering more detail."

A disappointed candidate on how little of it is controllable — a good corrective to
any deterministic reading of the book:

> in races like this how little is within your control. No one controls whether you
> have a [certain ethnic] name, or anyone else has [that ethnic] name, no one controls
> whether there's seven candidates in the race or three candidates in the race. . . .
> the stars have to align in crazy ways. . . . You can work the hardest, you can do X,
> Y, and Z . . . and you won't necessarily be successful . . . **Out of anyone's
> control, actually.**

### Polarization

"Overall, we have had little to say about polarization in this book. Polarization is
often presented as a phenomenon rooted in ideology, and we have argued that ideology
is generally unhelpful in understanding House nominations."

The interest-based alternative: "thinking of parties as coalitions of policy-demanding
groups suggests that **polarization increases as MCs find that the interests they care
about are better served by participating in highly stable partisan logrolls, as
opposed to bill-by-bill ad hoc coalitions.** The stable partisan voting coalitions of
our current polarized era may not be pretty, but real democracy rarely produces ideal
results." Closing with Klein (2020): **"the alternative to polarization often isn't
consensus but suppression."**

### Looking back at "A Theory of Political Parties" (2012)

The authors grade their own prior article and find **two things wrong**:

1. **United fronts.** The 2012 paper claimed policy-demanding groups form "united
   fronts" behind a mutually acceptable candidate. "We observed this phenomenon in
   only a handful of our House contests." Even with coordination broadly defined it
   was 32 of 55 — "a majority, but not an overwhelming one. **And sometimes — as when
   establishments fought insurgents — the coordination was more about crushing the
   other side than achieving party unity.**" Citing McCarty and Schickler (2018) and
   Cohen et al. (2016), "united fronts have become uncommon even in competitive
   presidential contests."
2. **The "electoral blind spot."** The 2012 catchy argument that parties "bamboozle
   voters by taking positions at the edge of the 'electoral blind spot.'" Based on
   Ahler et al. (2016) and their own exit surveys, **"the blind-spot notion assumes
   more ideological structure than voters actually use and therefore does little to
   help us understand House primaries. In this study we make the simpler argument that
   voters are simply too unengaged in House primaries to be effective principals."**

Two things the 2012 article did not anticipate: **selective participators** ("becoming
active only when the right candidate came along"), and **"the importance of volunteer
campaigners, in particular the faith-based actors that were critical in the GA-10,
GA-11, NC-6, and OK-5 nominations."**

### Normative assessment

Opens on the exit-poll number: "only **47 percent** could recall the names of any
candidates even though they had voted for one of them just minutes earlier. . . . we
found no evidence of policy voting in the other three primaries we studied. And even
the best-informed voters facing multicandidate fields exhibited **no discernible
evidence of the strategic thinking necessary to participate effectively**."

**Schlozman and Rosenfeld, *The Hollow Parties* (2024)**, are engaged directly: they
lament "blob parties" — "a term they would likely use to describe the policy demander
coalitions we have studied in this book" — and call for state party organizations with
professional staff, principled programs, regular recruitment, and resources. "We have
no quarrel with calls for stronger party organization, but there is little sign it
will develop anytime soon."

**The pluralist frame.** Dahl (1956, 51): "it is an observable fact that almost no one
regards political equality and popular sovereignty as worth unlimited sacrifice" of
other values. Bruce Cain (2014, 25): "The behavioral law of pluralism is that groups,
political parties, and other intermediaries are 'inevitable. . . . **In a perfect
pluralist world, citizens would not have to be well-informed or actively engaged in
public affairs in order to be well governed. They would take their cues from the
intermediaries they trust.**'" The hope: "make proxy representation of citizens by
interest as fair and efficient as possible." And Dahl (1984, 238) on the limits:
"relatively autonomous associations are necessary to a good life and a decent
sociopolitical order, and within limits which cannot be carefully specified their
existence is as justifiable, morally and practically, as that of the state itself."

**Schattschneider (1960, 34), conceded as true here:** "the flaw in the pluralist
heaven is that **the heavenly chorus sings with a strong upper-class accent**." The
authors: "this criticism rings true for House nominations. The nominations of both
parties were disproportionately influenced by wealthier, better educated, and better
organized actors."

**Three pro-democratic features they nonetheless claim:**

1. **The groups are recognizable to party voters.** Citing Kane et al. (2021), "voters
   are uniquely well-informed about the group composition of both their own party and
   the opposition party and cast ballots that are in line with their likes and dislikes
   of the two sets of groups."
2. **Stability plus openness.** Arrangements ranged from formal (unions in NJ-1) to
   loose (the "free-for-all contests of AL-6R or MA-5D"). And, from chapter 9, "success
   of the new force of insurgent conservatism in the Republican Party was . . . greatest
   where formal party organization was weakest."
3. **Quality control — the argument the book actually ends on.**

> Most successful nominees went through some sort of vetting process. The vetting was,
> of course, done in the interest of the vetters, not the public at large. Yet most of
> the vetters we described were associated with a responsible business, union,
> profession, or social group — that is, an actor that wished to avoid charlatans,
> embezzlers, and fraudsters. . . . **Democracy needs some sort of screening mechanism
> between voters and candidates, and while the vetting-and-vouching system we have
> described is by no means perfect, it is much better than nothing. Reformers should
> think hard before weakening this system through further empowerment of a largely
> unengaged electorate.**

And the immediate hedge: **"These concluding remarks fall intentionally short of a
full-throated defense of the role of groups in House nominations."**

## Epilogue. House Nominations Since 2014 (pp. 318–320)

Three pages. The book was finished **"in the fall of 2025."**

The question: do the five propositions survive Trump, MAGA, and a decade of
polarization? **"Our view, unavoidably speculative, is that changes in House
nominations since 2014 have been and will probably continue to be less important than
the basic features described by our five propositions."** Two reasons.

**First, the localism that made the subject hard to study also makes it stable.** House
nominations "take place at different times under different rules in widely dispersed
locations. The result is that the local factors shaping nomination politics remain in
place." Trump's own interventions "made attempts to influence House primaries through
endorsements and campaign appearances, but his impact has been limited."

**Boatright (2023, 15)** on the 2022 cycle: "**Except for the case of pro-impeachment
Republicans, there is little evidence that anything Trump did during the 2022 election
influenced the election results. However, there is evidence that in many Republican
primaries, voters rewarded candidates who associated themselves with Trump while
general election voters punished such candidates.**"

**The authors' extension of that is the epilogue's sharpest thought:** "Boatright's
finding on general-election voters may be his most significant. **For if Trump-backed
candidates have difficulty in general elections, it may undermine their ability to
generate the group support necessary to win primaries.**"

**Second, the House still does real work, so groups still care.** Against the view that
Congress is "dysfunctional, unproductive, and toothless":

> In the last full session of the House of Representatives at the time of this writing,
> members of the Republican-controlled House formally introduced **10,564 bills**,
> **614** of these measures came to a vote, and **280** were signed into law by
> Democratic President Joe Biden. In the most recent congressional term of unified
> party government from 2017 to 2019, Republican President Donald Trump signed **443**
> bills into law.

Many were minor — "renaming post offices to honor local notables was popular in this
period" — "but many involved matters of genuine importance, with details that greatly
impacted the lives, livelihoods, and values of many groups."

**On Democratic factionalism:** Ocasio-Cortez's 2018 win and the "squad" "could be seen
as a symptom of rising factionalism on the left. That said, House Democrats managed a
smooth leadership transition to Hakeem Jeffries in 2023, and the squad lost two members
in the 2024 elections, both results that would seem to augur that left-wing factionalism
will remain muted."

**The book's last paragraph, and its best sentence:**

> even though we see no particular threat to the forms of House nominations, we must
> recognize that they **lack the constitutional anchor** that the powers of the House of
> Representatives possess. Given this lack, change in the forms of policy demander
> involvement in House nominations is possible. Consider the basic history: nomination
> by self-appointed party committees in the 1790s; by deliberative assemblies of local
> activists in the middle years of the nineteenth century; by boss-dominated conventions
> later in the nineteenth century; by boss-dominated primaries in the early twentieth
> century; and by networked policy demanders in the present. **The diverse forms House
> nominations have taken in the past thus provide precedent for just about anything —
> with the exception of engaged voter participation.**

### For the corpus

**Chapter 11 is the chapter to read before citing any other chapter.** It names, in the
authors' own words, the weakest evidence behind each proposition: no systematic
evidence for group agendas ("no shortcoming in our study is more serious"); vetting
"limited and anecdotal"; coordination possibly overfitted across four forms, one case
resting on a single source; anchoring exposed to reverse causation and defended with
admittedly selected examples; and 18 candidate interviews against 110 with policy
demanders. **A book that audits itself this openly is a good model for the corpus's own
"what this cannot tell you" sections** — and the overfitting passage, comparing
endogenous concept-building to running many regressions until one fits, is a plain-
language statement of a problem the corpus otherwise has no natural place to teach.

**The epilogue's legislative-output numbers are a ready-made corpus chapter and are
entirely public.** 10,564 bills introduced → 614 reaching a vote → 280 signed. That is a
funnel with a 2.7 percent survival rate, from a source (Congress.gov / the Clerk) the
corpus already knows how to reach, and it is structurally identical to the cascade
figure in `primary-defeats`. It also carries its own honest caveat, which the book
supplies: many of the 280 renamed post offices. **A chapter on "what counts as a law"
would be a natural sibling to `house-competition`.**

**Mayhew's one-third figure is a corrective worth keeping** — party machines were active
in only about a third of US counties even at their nineteenth-century peak. The corpus
has a standing interest in claims about the past that turn out to be narrower than the
telling, and this is one.

**And the closing line is the book's most quotable sentence for teaching purposes:**
nomination forms have varied enormously across two centuries, "with the exception of
engaged voter participation." That is the thesis of `primary-positions` stated as
history rather than as data.
