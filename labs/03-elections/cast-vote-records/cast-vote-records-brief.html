Cast Vote Records and Ranked-Choice Counting

A congressional election recounted from the ballots themselves, and what the winner’s majority is a majority of

An election result is a set of totals, because the ballot is secret and a total is what remains once the ballots are put away. Alaska does not put them away. Since adopting ranked-choice voting — a ballot on which voters rank the candidates in order of preference instead of marking one — the state has published its cast vote record:1 one row per scanned ballot, showing every ranking the voter marked. No name and no address, but the actual choices, one ballot at a time. Nothing else in American election data comes this close to the individual act.

The claim you have heard about ranked-choice voting is its promise: rank as many candidates as you like, support your real first choice, and the eventual winner will hold a majority. Every part of that is checkable against the ballots of one congressional election. What does a ballot-level record let you see that no total can — and when the winner’s majority arrives, what is it a majority of?

The test

The contest is the November 2024 race for Alaska’s single seat in the U.S. House: Mary Peltola, the Democratic incumbent, against Nick Begich, with two minor candidates. The data is the State of Alaska, Division of Elections cast vote record for that election, the JSON export the state’s voting equipment writes — a text file in which each ballot’s marks sit nested inside its contests — published at a stable address (https://www.elections.alaska.gov/results/24GENR/). Beside it sits the state’s official round-by-round report, a PDF, typed into tables by hand and used below to grade a recount.

Why this source, of all sources: it is the bottom rung of the ladder of election data, the only public American election file in which a row is one ballot. The levels-of-aggregation chapter walks that ladder rung by rung. Everything this brief measures (how many rankings voters used, which candidates shared a ballot, where eliminated candidates’ votes went) is a property of single pieces of paper. A total has already destroyed it.

Nothing in that record is readable on its own. CandidateId is a number whose meaning lives in a separate file, CandidateManifest.json, listing 209 candidates across every contest:

Rank CandidateId Who that is
1 517 Begich, Nick
2 552 Hafner, Eric
3 520 Howe, John Wayne

Turning 2,028 such files into a table took five judgments, each a rule somebody chose. Keep one contest of the 13 on each ballot. Believe IsVote, not the ink: each mark carries its darkness (MarkDensity) and the scanner’s verdict, and the verdict is what counts here, rather than a darkness threshold of our own. Decide what two marks in one column mean: that is an overvote, recorded as OVERVOTE at that rank, after which the ranks are sorted and single gaps closed. Alaska’s tabulator applies further rules about skipped ranks that this reconstruction does not, and that honest difference is measured below. Translate numbers into names through the manifest.

Then destroy what is left. Ballots with identical rankings are collapsed to one row with a count, and precinct and tabulator identifiers are dropped before anything reaches disk. That last step is for privacy, not tidiness. A record with no name on it is not the same as a record nobody can identify; a distinctive ranking in a small precinct can narrow down who cast it. The collapse cannot be run backwards, which is the point of it.

What ships is three tables. sequences.csv has one row per distinct ranking: a ballots count, then rank1 through rank4, one candidate name per column the voter used. official_rounds.csv holds the state’s votes per candidate per round, and official_transfers.csv records where each eliminated candidate’s ballots were transferred_to — both typed from the PDF report. Before you read on, commit to two answers. Alaska’s ballot offered 4 rankings. First: what share of the voters in this race used exactly one? Second: whose supporters stop after one ranking more often — the front-runner’s, or the smallest candidate’s? Write both down.

Set aside 10,150 ballots that left this contest blank. Of the 330,628 that voted in it, 215,501 ranked exactly one candidate: 65.2%. Given 4 rankings, two-thirds of Alaskans used one. Neither the system’s advocates nor its opponents advertise that number. Hold the obvious explanation, a confusing ballot, because the file can test it, and the test comes out the other way.

First, the count. Instant runoff, the counting rule ranked-choice elections use, fits in three sentences. Count each ballot for its top-ranked candidate still in the race. If nobody has a majority, eliminate the candidate with the fewest and read those ballots’ next choice. Repeat. Each pass is a round.

The percentages match Alaska’s official result to two decimal places. The counts do not: this recount carries 154 more ballots into the final round, because the state’s tabulator applies printed rules about write-ins, skipped ranks and overvotes that the three-sentence version does not. The rules are not in the data. They live in a separate document, and they change the count2 — here by 0.048%, in a closer race by the winner.

When John Wayne Howe was eliminated, his 13,871 ballots split, and the largest single destination was nowhere: 6,295 of them, 45.4%, were exhausted, because those voters had ranked nobody else. Their ballots left the count before the decisive round. Follow one of them through. 5,707 voters ranked Howe and nobody else. In round 1 each of those ballots counts for Howe. Hafner, last with 3,558 votes, is eliminated, and in round 2 the ballot still counts for Howe. Now Howe is last, at 13,871, and is eliminated; the count reads the ballot’s second ranking, finds nothing, and the ballot leaves — it is exhausted. A ballot that had ranked Howe first and Begich second would instead have moved to Begich for round 3. That is the trade at the heart of ranked-choice voting: it lets you express more than one preference, and it does not make you.

Now the prediction. If two-thirds of voters stopping after one ranking is confusion, the rate should be roughly flat across candidates, because confusion has no reason to prefer a front-runner. Split the bullet voters — the ones who ranked one candidate and stopped — by who they ranked first:

First choice Voters % who ranked only that candidate
Begich, Nick 159,703 76.1
Peltola, Mary S. 152,887 56.4
Howe, John Wayne 13,146 43.4
Hafner, Eric 3,537 35.4

76.1% of Begich’s voters ranked nobody else, against 35.4% of Hafner’s, and the rate falls cleanly with candidate size. That is not confusion’s flat signature. It is arithmetic voters appear to have done. If your candidate is the front-runner, a second ranking will almost never be read. If your candidate polls at one percent, the second ranking is the only part of your ballot that will do anything.

A second reading, which this file cannot rule out, is campaign instruction — “rank one and stop” is a documented tactic.3 Either way the conclusion is the same: these ballots show the campaigns’ strategies, not just the voters’ preferences.

Figure 4. Bullet voting is not spread evenly across candidates. One bar per candidate, ordered by first-preference votes (159,703 down to 3,537), because the claim is a gradient and ordered bars make it visible at a glance. The share who ranked nobody else falls from 76.1% to 35.4%.

Which leaves the finding in the subtitle:

Quantity Value
Ballots in the cast vote record 340,778
Continuing in our first round 330,018
Continuing in the final round 322,000
Winner’s final-round total 164,935
As a share of continuing ballots 51.22%
As a share of all ballots cast 48.4%

The winner’s 51.22% is a majority of the 322,000 ballots still in the count, not of the 340,778 people who cast one. Measured against every ballot, the figure is 48.4% — a plurality, the largest share but not a majority. The majority is partly manufactured by shrinking the denominator, the bottom number the winner’s votes are divided by, and Burnett and Kogan (2015) found the same shrinkage across four earlier ranked-choice elections.4 The fair answer is a question: what would the alternative denominator mean? Under a plurality rule, where the most votes win outright, the winner’s share here would also have sat under half, with no second round for anyone’s ballot to survive into, and a voter who declines to rank anyone else has made a choice. Figure 4 says those voters were disproportionately the ones with the least to gain from a second ranking.

What this brief cannot tell you

It is not the certified count. Alaska says so plainly on the page the file is published from: the record “does not include ballots that were only counted by hand,” and write-in names are not resolved,5 so 340,778 is not this contest’s official turnout.

It holds choices, not reasons. Whether bullet voting is a calculation voters made or an instruction they followed cannot be separated from these rows; that would take survey data, and the ballot’s anonymity is deliberate. Where surveys exist, Donovan, Tolbert and Gracey (2016, 2019) find ranked-choice voters mostly say they understand the system, which also cuts against the confusion reading.6 No demographics attach to any ranking.

It is one race, in one state, in one year — a state that adopted ranked-choice voting by initiative, whose voters are not a sample of anything. Whether the system changes who runs and how they campaign is a question about candidates, not ballots.

And the file cannot say who cast what. The published collapse to 926 sequences was chosen so that it cannot be undone, which protects voters and closes some questions permanently.

What you should have learned

  • A cast vote record is the only American election data where a row is a ballot: 340,778 ballots collapse to 926 distinct ranking patterns, and 65.2% of voters used exactly one of 4 offered rankings.
  • The ballots do not count themselves. A three-sentence recount reproduces Alaska’s official percentages to two decimals while differing by 154 ballots, because the binding rules live in a document, not in the data.
  • 7,647 ballots (2.3% of the count) left before the decisive round: 7,595 exhausted plus 52 overvotes.
  • Bullet voting runs from 76.1% among the front-runner’s voters to 35.4% among the smallest candidate’s: a gradient that fits strategy or instruction, not confusion.
  • The winner’s 51.22% majority is a majority of continuing ballots; of all ballots cast, it is 48.4%.

Extensions

None of these is answered above, and every one can be answered from the tables the Sources section hands over.

  • sequences.csv has one row per distinct ranking with a ballots count. Count the ballots whose rank1 and rank2 name different parties’ candidates. How porous was the partisan divide, really?
  • sequences.csv again: find the sequences containing OVERVOTE. How many ballots carried one, and at which rank do overvotes cluster?
  • official_transfers.csv records where each eliminated candidate’s ballots went. Compare the destinations against what the parties involved would predict. Were any transfers surprising?
  • official_rounds.csv shows the count at each stage. Work out who led in round one and who won. Then decide whether the first-round leader has a complaint.

Two stretch ideas point past the spreadsheet. Maine and several cities publish cast vote records for their own ranked-choice races; download one and compute its exhaustion rate — the share of ballots that leave the count before the final round — against Alaska’s 2.3%. And compare both against the four elections in Burnett and Kogan (2015): is exhaustion shrinking as voters gain experience with the system?
