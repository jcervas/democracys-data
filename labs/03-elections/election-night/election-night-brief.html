Two years of campaigning, several billion dollars, tens of thousands of advertisements, an industry of polling and forecasting. Against all of that, set one sentence:

A state elects the senator of the party whose presidential candidate carried it.

There are no candidates in that sentence. No money, no polls, no issues, no campaigns, no incumbents — nothing that happened after the previous presidential election had been counted. Run it one level down, across the 433 House districts of 2024 with the presidential vote counted inside each one. Then ask how many elected a member of the party that had carried them for president. The answer is 418, which is 96.5% of a national election, called by a rule whose single input is two years stale.

Almost nothing else in this course beats that, and the things that try cost millions of dollars. The number is also, taken the way it invites being taken, badly misleading — which is the more useful of the two facts, and the harder one to arrive at.

Everything below is written before the election it is about. That is not a limitation to apologize for. A prior written down afterwards is not a prior, and the 33 Senate seats this chapter ends on have not yet been voted on.

What it means for a contest to be already decided

If a rule using no information about a contest predicts its outcome, then the contest was in some sense already decided. Everything the campaign did was either irrelevant, or the work required to hold a line that was already drawn. Which of those it is matters enormously and cannot be read off the result.

The stakes are practical. Attention is finite, and it concentrates on dramatic races. If the dramatic races are also the ones where the baseline is least reliable, then attention and consequence are pointed at the same small set. That sounds reassuring until you notice how small the set is.

Why the baseline works so well is genuinely contested

There is a real dispute about why a presidential baseline works so well, and it has consequences.

  • Nationalization. Hopkins (2018) argues that American political behavior has become substantially nationalized: voters know more about Washington than about their state capital and vote accordingly, so down-ballot results track presidential ones. Abramowitz and Webster (2016) locate the mechanism in negative partisanship — dislike of the other party producing straight-ticket voting.
  • Incumbency, dwindling. The older tradition held that House and Senate elections were substantially personal. Mayhew (1974) documented the disappearance of marginal House seats; Gelman and King (1990) built the estimator that made incumbency advantage measurable without bias. If that advantage has shrunk, a presidential baseline should have become more accurate over time, which is a testable claim this file cannot test.

Both accounts predict the same high accuracy today. They disagree about whether it is new.

Two snapshots, and a third file that will be somebody’s judgment

Neither file was collected for the question asked here, and the file this chapter is finally about does not exist yet.

The first is a roster. Which seats face the voters in 2026 is a fact about the calendar rather than a fact about politics, so it could be built two years early. The Class 2 senators are pulled out of @unitedstates/congress-legislators, a volunteer-maintained public-domain list of everyone currently serving, and each is joined to the presidential margin in their state.

That margin is borrowed from the electoral-map chapter’s folder. It traces back through a private compilation of the fifty-one state canvasses, for the reason that chapter gives at length: no federal agency publishes American election returns. The country’s most consequential numbers reach the public through people who volunteered to assemble them.

The word to hold on to in that paragraph is currently. A roster of current members describes today and is overwritten tomorrow, which is why senator_last names whoever held the seat in August 2026 and not whoever will appear on a ballot in November. Retirement, primary defeat, appointment and death each break the column, and by election day several will have. That is not damage to be repaired. It is what a current roster is, and catching a name in these 33 rows that is not on any ballot is one of the more instructive things a reader can do with the file.

The second file is the opposite kind of object: an election that is finished. It holds the 2024 presidential vote counted inside each congressional district, beside the party that won the House seat there. It comes from the redistricting chapter’s folder, and it keeps only the 433 districts where both halves are present.

Its left column is less official than it looks. Nobody tallies a presidential vote by congressional district. Somebody has to lay precinct returns over district lines and divide the precincts the lines cut through, and the resulting number is a careful reconstruction rather than a canvass.

It is the right evidence anyway, and for a reason peculiar to this chapter. A rule cannot be graded on the election it was written to predict, because that election has not happened. So it is rehearsed one level down, where 433 districts offer 433 chances to be wrong instead of 33.

The third file is results_senate_2026_TEMPLATE.csv: 33 rows and two empty columns, waiting. What eventually fills them will not be a count. A called race is a judgment. Analysts decide that the ballots still outstanding cannot reorder the ones already in, and states differ enormously in how much is still outstanding on Thursday morning.

The template will most likely be filled by hand from a news site, which takes ten minutes and is entirely defensible. If that is what happened, the chapter should say so out loud rather than let a CSV imply a canvass.

Cleaning barely arises here, and that is a property of the sources rather than a virtue of the script. Both files arrived tidy. The build is one merge on a two-letter state abbreviation, one dropping of rows, and a handful of columns worked out by comparison. Those are: who won the state for president, how far from even that was, and whether the party holding the seat is the party that lost it. Nothing was imputed, meaning no missing value was guessed at and filled in, and nothing was reweighted.

Rebuilding the folder is not a matter of redoing the downloads either, and it is worth being exact about why. Exactly one thing here is fetched from the internet: the roster, a CSV that volunteers keep current at a public address. The margins are not fetched at all. They are read sideways out of what the electoral-map and redistricting chapters already produced. The compilation of fifty-one canvasses and the laying of precincts over district lines both happened in other chapters, and this folder shows no trace of either. Whatever those files got wrong, this one has already inherited.

Then the file the chapter is finally about will be typed in by hand off a news site. A typed file cannot be fetched again or checked against its source. If a digit goes in wrong, nothing here will catch it. One fetch, two borrowings, and a transcription — most of the ways a table can arrive, inside one small folder, and only the first of them leaves a URL behind.

Where the judgment went

Eleven identifiers were available and none of them was used. The roster carries bioguide_id, govtrack_id, icpsr_id, opensecrets_id, fec_ids and half a dozen more. It is, among other things, a crosswalk between everybody else’s numbering systems: a table that translates one set of codes into another. The join here is on the two-letter state abbreviation instead, and that is correct: the thing being joined on is a presidential margin, which is a property of a state and not of a person. A file full of excellent keys is no help when the two sides are different kinds of object.

Three columns were made by comparison, not fetched. pres24_winner, abs_margin and hostile_turf do not exist in either source. They are this chapter’s arithmetic — who carried the state, by how much regardless of direction, and whether the party holding the seat is the party that lost the state. The last one is a definition, not a measurement, and every claim about which seats are exposed rests on it.

The birthday is in the file and the retirement is not. That asymmetry is the point of the section above. A roster records facts that are true of a person now. It has no column for an intention, so a senator who has already announced they will not run looks exactly like one who is running hard. 33 rows describe seats, and some number of them describe an incumbent who will not be on the ballot. The file cannot tell you which, and this is a defect of the unit rather than of the data: a roster of people was never going to be a list of contests.

The rule, stated so it can be wrong

The baseline assigns each seat to the party whose presidential candidate carried the state in 2024. Applied to this map:

Outcome Value
Seats the baseline gives Republicans 21
Seats the baseline gives Democrats 12
Seats where the baseline implies a party change 3

The rule expects 3 of 33 seats to change hands — precisely the 3 on hostile turf, since those are the only places where the incumbent’s party and the presidential winner’s party disagree.

That is a genuine, falsifiable prediction, made without knowing a single thing about who is running.

Testing it on an election that is already counted

We cannot test the rule on 2026 yet. We can test it on an election that has already happened, in a chamber with enough seats to make the test mean something. That is the 433 House districts of 2024, each with the presidential vote counted inside it. This is where the number at the top of the chapter came from, and it is worth watching it arrive.

418 of 433 — 96.5%.

So 15 districts broke the rule: few enough to list by name, and enough to ask a question of.

Before you read on, commit to an answer. Those 15 districts elected a member of the party their own presidential vote did not pick. Where do they sit? Not geographically — where on the distribution of district partisanship. Scattered across it, from the safest Republican seats to the safest Democratic ones, as a rule that is wrong 3.5% of the time everywhere would put them? Or concentrated somewhere in it? If concentrated, write down where, and how tightly, before you look.

The rule is not 96.5% accurate. It is 100% accurate where nothing was at stake and a coin-flip-plus where something was.

Both readings of that 96.5% were available and only one is right. “A rule this simple forecasts a national election” is true and misleading. “A rule this simple forecasts every uncompetitive election, which is most of them” is true and useful.

5 seats of 33 are in the zone where the rule stopped working. Everything else is, on the 2024 evidence, already answered.

Two caveats before anyone treats that as a forecast. A House district is not a Senate state — Senate races have larger, better-funded, better-known candidates and more genuine incumbency, all of which should make a presidential baseline less accurate. And a midterm is not a presidential year: the electorate is smaller and differently composed, and the president’s party has lost House seats in the large majority of midterms since 1858. The rehearsal holds neither of those constant.

What is left for a campaign to do

The claim available now is sharper than the one the headline percentage supports. A rule containing no information about any candidate was right in 96.5% of 2024 House districts, and that figure is an average over two regimes that have nothing to do with each other. Its accuracy depends on competitiveness and on essentially nothing else. So the information a campaign adds is only worth anything in the 90 districts where the baseline fails, which is 20.8% of the House. Applied to the 2026 Senate map, that is 5 seats.

What does not follow is that campaigns do not matter. The baseline holding is consistent with two very different worlds: one in which nothing happened, and one in which enormous effort was spent making the expected thing happen. Nothing in either file distinguishes them, and no amount of additional accuracy would. Neither does anything here say what will happen in November. It says what would have to be explained.

What the argument is really about

The first argument is about whether this is new. Hopkins’s nationalization thesis says the alignment between presidential and congressional results has tightened over decades. Abramowitz and Webster put the tightening down to negative partisanship. If they are right, the same rehearsal run on 1980 would have produced a much lower accuracy, and the 15 crossover districts of 2024 would be a small remnant of a once-large category.

Mayhew (1974) documented the growth of the safe House seat from the other end. Gelman and King (1990) showed that most measures of incumbency advantage in use at the time were biased, which is worth remembering before treating any historical comparison as settled. This file has one election in it and cannot test any of this.

The second argument is about what to do on election night. A high baseline accuracy is an argument for ignoring most of the returns and watching a handful of places. That is exactly what a newsroom does, and exactly what produces the recurring error of reading three dramatic races as a national verdict. Three exceptions out of 33 is 90.9% baseline accuracy, which is worse than 2024 and still very high.

The third argument is about counting. Margins cited on the Thursday after an election move. Mail ballots in several states count for days or weeks. Foley (2013) documented that the shift during the canvass has leaned toward Democratic candidates since about 2000. That is 23 states where the Democratic candidate gained during the canvass in a state they lost, across three cycles, against two for Republicans. A number that is still moving is not a result, and a class held two days after an election is holding provisional numbers.

What 433 districts can testify to

The strength of this evidence is that it is a real out-of-sample test rather than a fit. The rehearsal file has 433 cases in it. That is enough to say something about accuracy at each level of competitiveness, instead of only reporting one overall rate, and the level-by-level statement is the one that turned out to matter. The landscape file’s single predictive column is a fact about 2024, not an estimate, so neither the rule nor its application to 2026 rests on a model.

Its limits constrain different sentences and are worth taking one at a time. The landscape is a snapshot from August 2026: retirements, primary defeats and appointments after that date are simply absent, and an open seat behaves very differently from an incumbent defending one. A House district is not a Senate state, and a presidential year is not a midterm, which are the two caveats above and the reason the rehearsal’s accuracy will not transfer exactly. This is one election, tested across both of those gaps at once. 2 House districts are missing from the rehearsal file altogether, and every accuracy figure above is computed over 433. And there is no turnout, no polling, no fundraising, no candidate quality anywhere in either file — exactly one predictor by construction, which is what makes it a clean baseline and useless for anything else.

Three questions remain genuinely open. Whether baseline accuracy has risen over time is the sharpest of them: the nationalization literature predicts it has, and testing that needs decades of district-level presidential results rather than one year of them. Whether the baseline holding means campaigns are irrelevant or means they worked is undecidable from outcomes alone, because it needs a counterfactual no election produces. And what happens in the 5 close Senate seats is genuinely unknown, which is the honest content of a forecast rather than a defect in one.

None of which disturbs the sentence this chapter opened with, only its meaning. A rule with no candidates in it called 96.5% of a national election. It did so by getting every race that was not a race right. On the 90 districts where anyone was actually competing it fell to 83.3%, which is still better than chance and no longer anything a forecaster would be paid for. Both halves are true at once, and a newsroom that reports the first without the second has said almost nothing about the country and something quite precise about arithmetic.
