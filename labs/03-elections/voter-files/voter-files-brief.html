50 of the 51 states and the District of Columbia will not let you vote unless you registered first. The exception is North Dakota, which abolished voter registration in 1951 and has never brought it back.

Nearly unanimous is not the same as inevitable. Registering is not voting. It is a separate act, done weeks earlier, at a different place, on different paper, and the file this chapter reads is the record of that separate act rather than of anybody voting.

Nobody had to register until somebody decided they did

Keyssar (2009) traces where the requirement came from, and the answer is that it is younger than the country. Before the 1870s most states kept no list of who could vote. You turned up at the polls on the day and settled your eligibility there, with papers or with witnesses. Massachusetts began registering voters in 1801 and a few states followed before 1850, but registration stayed uncommon until after the Civil War, and most proposals for it were voted down as unnecessary and partisan.

Between the 1870s and the First World War most states adopted it, cities first. The reason given was fraud. In a city an official could not know every voter by sight, so registering in advance gave the state time to build a list, check papers and question witnesses before election day.

The principle was simple and the details were not. How many days before the election did you have to register? What hours was the office open? Did you register in the county, the district or the precinct? What documents did you have to bring, and how often did you have to do it all again?

Every one of those answers changed who ended up on the list, and every one was fought over.

New Jersey’s Geran Act of 1911 shows how far that could go, and Keyssar uses it as the limiting case. It required personal registration in every city over 5,000 people and gave prospective voters four days to do it. A man had to name his parents, his spouse and his landlord, and describe the dwelling he lived in. Registration also had to be renewed whenever a voter moved or failed to vote. Hold on to that last clause. A chapter that ends on a 2018 Supreme Court case about being removed for not voting is describing a rule that is more than a century old.

The effects were not subtle. After Pittsburgh’s registration commission went to work in 1907, the number of men registered there fell from 95,580 to 45,819 (Keyssar 2009). Between 1910 and 1920 only 60 to 70 per cent of eligible voters were registered in Philadelphia, Chicago and Boston. His summary is that registration laws did reduce fraudulent voting, and also kept probably millions of eligible voters away from the polls. Scholars he cites attribute a third or more of the national decline in turnout over that period to registration alone.

Congress went the other way in 1993. The National Voter Registration Act, the “motor voter” law, obliged states to offer registration at motor vehicle bureaus and public assistance agencies. It worked, and it did not. Nine million people were added to the rolls in under two years, and Keyssar records that the new registrants were disproportionately young, Black and Democratic. Turnout in 1996 was still lower than in any presidential election since 1924.

Registration closes, and not on one date

No state has one registration deadline. NPR keeps a dated list of them, and for the general election of 3 November 2026 it publishes deadlines for 54 of the 56 states, territories and the District of Columbia it covers. Most of them have three rather than one: a date for registering online, a date for registering by mail, and a date for turning up in person. In 27 of the 53 places that offer more than one way in, those dates do not agree.

In person, the books close anywhere from 60 days before the election to the morning of it. 22 jurisdictions will register you in person on election day itself. The longest lead is 60 days, in the Northern Mariana Islands. Keyssar’s question about how many days a state gives you is still answered differently in every one of them.

The mail deadline is two rules wearing one label. In 38 jurisdictions the date is a postmark: get the envelope stamped by then and you are in time. In 13 it is a delivery date, and the form has to be in the office by close of business. Two places can print the same day and be asking different things of you. Subtracting one date from another will never show it.

An empty cell is not a late deadline. 9 of these 54 jurisdictions offer no online registration at all, and 3 accept no registration by mail. There is no date to record because there is no door. Fill those cells in with the state’s other deadline and you have invented a service the state does not provide.

Count the same-day states two ways and you get two answers. 22 jurisdictions show an in-person deadline of election day. 21 carry a separate note saying voters may register at the polls. Those are not the same list: North Carolina’s note covers only its early-voting period, and Montana and Vermont take registrations on the day without calling it same-day registration. Neither count is wrong, and a table with one column headed “same-day registration” has picked one of them without saying so.

Two rows are blank, for opposite reasons. North Dakota registers nobody, and NPR’s page for it says as much in its own words: it is the only state where registration is not required. Puerto Rico holds no territory-wide election in 2026, so it has nothing to close. A reader who reads both blanks as “no deadline, register whenever you like” has one of them exactly backwards.

Which is why the denominator is a choice

Here is a sentence everyone has read:

“Turnout among registered Black voters was up six points.”

Turnout is a fraction, and that sentence has quietly picked its denominator — the bottom number, the thing you are dividing by. Divide by registered voters and you are measuring something different from what you measure by dividing by everyone old enough and eligible to vote. The registered are the people who already cleared the hurdle this chapter’s first section is about, and after 1911 or 1993 that hurdle sat in a different place.

Neither denominator is wrong. But one of them is a count of people the state has processed, and it moves when the state changes how it processes them. This chapter is about that file, and about the four ways it will mislead anyone who treats it as a list of the electorate.

One row is one registered person: a name, a birth year, a home address, a race, a gender, the date they registered, the date they last voted. Then comes a long run of codes, one for each body that will ever put a name on their ballot. Congressional district, state senate, state house, judicial, county commission, school board, city council, municipal school board, water board, fire district, two kinds of precinct, land lot, land district.

The voter file for Houston County, Georgia has 127,560 people in it. Almost everything below is a count over all of them: the shape of the file rather than anybody in it. There is one deliberate exception. Five real voters are printed further down, names included, exactly as the state publishes them. Georgia’s list is a public record, and a reader who has never seen a row of one does not really know what this chapter is about.

An exhibit, not a dataset

Where the file comes from explains most of how it behaves. Georgia keeps one registration list for the whole state. It is a working system that clerks use every day, not a dataset anybody sets out to publish. Counties pull copies out of it when somebody asks. This is Houston County’s copy, and it arrived by itself. The state’s records of who voted in the 2020, 2022 and 2024 general elections are a separate public record, requested separately and matched to it.

Anyone can ask for a copy. Georgia law requires the state to hand these records over, and that is the only reason outsiders ever see one. This copy arrived as working material in a redistricting case that is still running. That is the ordinary route. Voter files are used as court exhibits and campaign lists far more often than as research data.

This is the county’s own copy rather than a cleaned-up commercial one, and that is the point. Companies sell voter files with the problems already tidied away: the statuses squared up, the missing party affiliations filled in by a model, the people in one household matched to each other. Every one of those repairs is somebody’s judgment call, and this chapter wants to look at them rather than take them on trust. What is here is the file as the county keeps it, still shaped by what that office needed it to do.

It is also one of the very few American records that gives a race for each person, by name, for reasons taken up in a moment. That is what makes it possible to work out turnout by race from government records instead of from a survey.

The hard part is what the file is, not anything wrong inside it. It is a photograph of a system that is still running, and the system keeps no past tense. Nobody saved a copy of the list as it stood on any election day. So a turnout rate for a past election has to be built by matching today’s voters against old records of who turned out. Anyone who has since moved away, died or been taken off the list drops out of the top of the fraction.

23,278 voters drop out this way across the three elections here. Nobody can clean that up. It is what a current list is.

Two more limits are worth naming now. The file holds only people the state has registered, so it says nothing at all about people who could register and have not. And race is a box each person ticked on a form, from a fixed list. One of the choices is UNKNOWN, ticked by 2,175 people here. Another, ALASKAN NATIVE, is ticked by 4, too few for any rate worked out on it to mean anything.

Almost all the processing was throwing things away, plus one repair that matters. The county file and the three voting records are read together. What comes out is the 53-row list of columns, a few small tables, and five whole records. Nothing here is anonymised. The names printed later are the names in the file, because Georgia’s list is a public record and this book does not redact public records.

The repair is the number the two files are matched on. Registration numbers are stored as text, and the two systems do not write them the same way, so the same voter turns up with and without leading zeros and with stray spaces. The script turns each number into a whole number before matching, and drops duplicate rows from the voting records, which otherwise list some voters twice for one election. Get either step wrong and the decay this chapter measures grows, for reasons that have nothing to do with anybody leaving Houston County.

The first five rows are a lie about the file

Everyone opens a new file the same way: read it in, look at the top. On this file that habit produces a confident wrong answer, and the column it gets wrong is one this chapter is built on.

The file arrives sorted newest registrant first. Nothing says so. The consequence is that the first rows are not a sample of Houston County. They are the people who registered most recently. So the columns that fill in only after somebody has voted are empty at the top of the file and filled in further down.

Five rows show you what a record is. They cannot tell you what the file does. Reasoning about the file from five of its rows is how this chapter once came to claim a congressional district that does not exist in this county.

Congressional District is stored as a number and is not one. This county holds exactly two of them, 002 and 008, and district 8 is not four times district 2. No average of that column means anything, but R will compute one without complaint, and it will drop the leading zeros on the way.

Birth Year is a number you may subtract and must not add up. Registration Date is text, and text sorts as text: it happens to sort correctly here only because the state wrote it year-first. That is luck, not design, and the same column in a file written 03/01/2024 would sort every March before every April of every year.

None of that is visible from the values. It is visible only by asking what each column is for. That is the question the rest of this chapter is about, and the next figure asks it of all 53 columns at once.

What the list decides

Every state maintains a list of everyone registered to vote. It is a public record. Every campaign works from it to decide who to knock on. Every turnout model is built on it. Most published claims about who votes come out of it.

Nothing in American elections works without a list, and no other document decides so much with so little scrutiny. Whether you are handed a ballot at all depends on whether a clerk finds you in it. Which ballot you are handed depends on the stack of district codes attached to your row. Whether a campaign ever contacts you depends on how your row scores in somebody’s model.

The list is also how people are removed. List maintenance is the official name for taking people off it, and it is where the right to vote is quietly rationed. Nobody is told they have been made inactive, and the trigger is usually a piece of mail rather than anything a voter did.

And this file is one of the very few places where American government records race on an individual basis. Georgia does, because Section 5 of the Voting Rights Act required covered jurisdictions to show that changes did not harm minority voters, and that cannot be shown without knowing who the voters are. The coverage formula fell in Shelby County v. Holder (2013). The data collection remained. That is why turnout by race can be worked out here from state records rather than from a survey.

So the question worth asking of the file is narrow and answerable: what is actually in it, and what was it built to do?

Those 21 columns are congressional, state senate, state house, judicial, county commission, school board, city council, municipal school board, water board and fire district. Then two kinds of precinct, a “Combo” field that bundles the whole stack into a single code, and land lot and land district.

That is not a description of an electorate. It is a routing table. Its purpose is to let a poll worker hand one specific person one specific piece of paper. Everything researchers want from it is a by-product of that clerical need: who registered when, who turned out, who lives where.

The consequence runs through everything below: nobody designed this file to answer anybody’s research question, and no part of it is obliged to.

The secret ballot is doing real work here. A campaign can know that you voted; it can never know whom you chose. Everything later in this book exists to bridge that gap: turnout models, racially polarized voting analysis, the whole guessing industry. It exists because the gap is permanent.

None of those people did anything. Every reason in that column is an event in a mail system, not an act by a voter.

Whether that is hygiene or disenfranchisement is a live legal fight, and both sides are describing this column. The National Voter Registration Act requires states to keep lists current and forbids removing people for not voting. Ohio’s practice was upheld in Husted v. A. Philip Randolph Institute (2018), five to four. That practice is to treat a missed election as evidence of having moved, send a confirmation notice, and remove the voter if it goes unanswered. The majority’s answer to the prohibition was that the removal is for failing to answer the notice rather than for failing to vote, so the clause is not triggered. Put as a data question it is this: is a returned envelope evidence about a person, or evidence about the postal system?

That is a publishable finding. It is the kind of sentence that opens news stories and appears in expert reports. It is also the sentence this chapter started from.

Look again at what went into it. Every rate in that table has a 2024 numerator and a 2026 denominator — a top number from one year and a bottom number from another. For that to be harmless, the people in the file now would have to be the people who were in it then.

Before you read on, put a number to it. Of the people who actually cast a ballot in this county in November 2020, what share are missing from the 2026 file altogether — dead, moved away, or struck off? Commit to a percentage before you turn the page. Then commit to a second answer: would you expect the people who vanished to have voted at about the same rate as the people who stayed? Match the current file against the state’s own record of who actually voted and this is what comes back. 13,803 people voted in the 2020 general election and are not in the 2026 file at all — 18.6% of everyone who voted in this county that November. Most readers guess something in the low single digits, reasoning that a list loses people at about the rate people die. It is roughly one voter in 5, in six years.

They died, moved away, or were removed. A voter file is a photograph of a system that is still running, not an archive. It describes now. The further back you reach with it, the more people have quietly left your denominator. They left for reasons that are tied to almost everything anyone wants to measure: moving, age, how long they have lived somewhere, income.

4.6% of 2024’s voters have already gone in under two years. Nothing tells you whether the people who left were more or less likely to vote than the people who stayed, or whether they left at the same rate from every group. The loss is not a random sample. Leaving is what movers do, and movers are younger, poorer, and likelier to rent.

The list also moves in both directions, and not at a steady rate. New registrations arrive all year, in a rush before each deadline. Removals do not work like that. A voter is marked inactive first, waits out the period the law requires, and is struck only when the state next runs a maintenance pass. So the distance between this copy and the November 2024 list is not simply age. Some of it is the timing of the last purge, which is a decision of the office rather than a fact about voters.

The right denominator is the file as it stood in November 2024, and this is not that file. Most states overwrite: the list is a live system, and last year’s version is not kept anywhere in it. The repair has to be arranged before the fact. Ask a state for a copy in the weeks around an election and you have the denominator for that election forever; ask afterwards and no request can produce it. That is why serious work on past elections uses archival snapshots, bought from vendors who took the copy at the time.

What a copy costs depends entirely on which state you ask. Two published price lists cover all fifty-one jurisdictions, and both run from nothing to five figures. They also disagree about 18 of the 43 jurisdictions where each gives a single plain amount, so neither is a quotable fact on its own. Georgia is $250 on the older list and $485 on the newer one. The two tables are laid side by side in price.csv.

Three arguments, one of which has no data answer

Everyone agrees lists must be maintained; people die and move. The dispute is over what counts as evidence that a particular person has gone, and the 9,386 inactive records above are that dispute sitting in a column. The reasons recorded there are postal events, and postal events track residential mobility, which tracks income and tenure. Whoever is hardest to reach by mail is the person the process removes. Husted upheld the practice over a dissent arguing it punishes non-voting, which the National Voter Registration Act forbids, and neither opinion turned on a number that this file could supply.

The second argument is about how good government records are in the first place, and here the news is better than expected. Ansolabehere and Hersh (2012) matched survey respondents to commercial voter files. They found that self-reported turnout is badly inflated, while the file’s own vote-history record is comparatively reliable. That is a strong result for records over surveys.

But the same authors are clear that the demographic and modeled fields vary enormously in quality between states. The lesson is not “trust the file”; it is trust the columns the file needs in order to work, and be careful with the ones it does not. Vote history is a column the file needs. A modeled ethnicity score is not.

The third argument is about values, and no amount of this data settles it. Your name is on a list like this, the list is public, and vendors buy it, add hundreds of modeled columns, and sell the result. Eitan Hersh’s Hacking the Electorate (2015) is the book-length version of the finding that campaigns see you as whatever your state happens to record. What a state records differs from state to state, for reasons that have nothing to do with campaigns.

The transparency case is that a public electorate can be audited by anyone. The privacy case is that nobody agreed to a permanent commercial file about them, built around whether they vote. That argument is not settled, and this chapter does not settle it. Note only that it is an argument about what others may do with a public list, not about whether the list is public. It is.

What the routing table will bear

The strengths are genuine and unusual. This is a count of everyone rather than a sample: every registered person, not an estimate. Whether somebody voted is recorded by the state rather than reported by the voter, which avoids the exaggeration that ruins survey turnout estimates. And in Georgia the file carries race, so turnout by race can be worked out from records instead of from a survey. Very few states allow that.

The weaknesses are equally specific. 70.0% of the file carries no party signal, because the column that looks like party identification records only which primary ballot somebody last requested, and Georgia collects nothing else. How many people count as registered is unclear by 7.4%, depending on whether inactive registrants are included, and publications rarely say which they used. The file forgets, non-randomly: 18.6% of the 2020 electorate is already gone, and the people who leave are movers, who are younger, poorer and likelier to rent than the people who stay. Race is one box, self-reported at registration, possibly decades ago.

This is one county in one state. The shape carries over to other states and the columns do not, since most states record no race at all and about half record a party registration Georgia does not. And this is the public file; the commercial versions campaigns actually use carry hundreds of modeled columns that no one outside the vendor can inspect.

What can be said, then, is that a voter file is a routing table. 21 of 53 columns exist so that a clerk can hand one person one piece of paper, and every research use of the file is scavenging. The column that looks like party is not party.

And the file forgets. 13,803 people voted in November 2020 and are simply not there now, erased from every backward-looking calculation anyone will ever run on it.

What cannot be said is how anyone voted; that is what the secret ballot is for. Nor a correct turnout rate for any past election computed from a current file. Nor whether the missing 18.6% voted at the same rate as the survivors. That is the whole ballgame for backward-looking turnout work, and it cannot be answered from a file that has already lost them. Nor whether list maintenance is neutral, since the reasons are postal and postal reliability is not evenly distributed. Nor whether any of this should be public at all, or how many people a registration deadline turned away: a deadline is a rule, and the people it stopped are recorded nowhere.

The finding that travels furthest is not about voters. A live operational system optimizes for its own job, overwrites what it no longer needs, and owes history nothing. Everything anyone knows about who votes in America is scraped off the side of a clerical process that was built to hand out ballots and has no reason to remember last time.

Your turn

None of these is answered above, and every one can be answered from the tables you just opened.

  • status.csv breaks registrations into statuses with reasons. Work out what share of the file is not active. Who is in a voter file but cannot vote from it?
  • lost.csv counts voters who took part in an election but are no longer in the file. Work out what that does to anyone computing turnout from a current file for a past election.
  • columns.csv says how each column is stored and how many distinct values it has. Find the column stored as a number that is not a quantity. What breaks if software treats it as one?
  • turnout.csv gives participation by race across three elections. Compute the rate for each group and year. Then check race_party.csv and ask what the primary ballot column adds.
  • deadlines.csv gives three deadlines per jurisdiction: online_days, mail_days and in_person_days, each counted back from election day. Subtract the smallest from the largest in each row. Which jurisdiction asks the most of somebody who picks the wrong door, and does the answer change if you leave out the rows where a door is missing?
  • deadlines.csv also has mail_rule, which says whether the mail deadline counts the postmark or the day the form arrives. Sort by mail_days and compare jurisdictions with the same number of days but different rules. Which of the two is the later real deadline for a voter, and by roughly how long?

Why this data cannot be fetched again 
The main file behind this chapter has no address, and no assistant
can fetch it for you. One of its side tables does, and is named below.

WHY NOT. The core file is a voter registration extract for Houston
County, Georgia: 127,560 registrations, 53 columns. It was obtained
through a public-records process during a redistricting matter, not
from any URL. The state's vote-history files for 2020, 2022 and 2024
are a second public record, requested separately and matched to the
extract on the registration number; they did not arrive with it.
Georgia publishes voter history at
https://mvp.sos.ga.gov/s/voter-history-files, so that half has an
address even though the registration extract does not.
Neither is redistributed here, because a voter file is full of names,
addresses and birth dates, and this book quotes from it rather than
reposting it. There is nothing to download, so there is nothing to
rebuild.

WHAT EXISTS INSTEAD, PART ONE. The registration deadlines this
chapter opens with are fetchable, and are the one thing here an
assistant can rebuild. NPR keeps a page per state and territory at
https://apps.npr.org/voter-registration-2026-mail/ with the deadlines
marked up in the HTML: each is tagged online, mail, in-person or
same-day, and the mail one says whether the date is a postmark or a
delivery. Take the general election section from each page, keep the
rule as well as the date, and leave a jurisdiction's cell empty when
it does not offer that way of registering. The tracker is dated and
gets revised during a cycle, so record the day you fetched it.

WHAT EXISTS INSTEAD, PART TWO. The registration records themselves
are public and purchasable. Georgia sells its statewide voter list:
any member of the public can order it from the Secretary of State's
elections division, which charges a flat fee for the full file. Other states have their own
terms — a companion scan in this book measures all 51 of them. Know
before buying that what arrives is a different object: a voter file
is a snapshot of a live administrative system, and a file bought
today has already forgotten some of the people the 2026 extract still
holds.

WHAT YOU CAN STILL CHECK. On the deadlines, which you can fetch: 56
jurisdiction pages, of which 54 list a deadline for the 3 November
2026 general election. North Dakota lists none and says why, because
it requires no registration; Puerto Rico lists none because it holds
no territory-wide election that year. Of the 51 jurisdictions taking
registrations by mail, 38 count the postmark and 13 count the day the
form arrives. Get a different split and you have read the rule off
the wrong element, or the tracker has been revised since.
