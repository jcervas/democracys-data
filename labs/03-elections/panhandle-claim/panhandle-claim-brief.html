Testing a Viral Claim Against Florida’s Returns

What the Panhandle did: a claim about race and a Republican primary, tested four ways

On 18 August 2026 Florida Republicans chose a nominee for governor. Byron Donalds won with 47.8% of a 11-candidate field, and 1,697,262 people voted. Donalds is Black. He is the first Black person either party in Florida has nominated for governor.

Then look at where his votes came from. In Collier County he took 65.8%. In Lafayette County he took 18.0%.

Figure 1. Donalds against his own statewide share, county by county. A map fits this question because the claim is about a place: each county is shaded by how far Donalds ran from his statewide 47.8%, on a diverging scale, because the sign is the subject. The deficit is not scattered. It is the north.

A claim follows that map around Florida politics, and it is the reason this brief exists. Panhandle voters would not vote for the Black candidate. This brief takes the claim seriously enough to test it, which means being willing to find it true. Four tests follow. The first two make it look stronger than you might expect; the third and fourth do not.

The test

Every test runs on official returns published by the Florida Department of State’s Division of Elections: county-level results for every statewide contest in the 2026 primary. The 2018 and 2014 primaries sit beside them for comparison. The 2026 file is the one the state published after the counties finished canvassing, not the wire-service snapshot from election night. Alongside the returns sit the Division’s book-closing reports for the same primary: registered voters by county, party and race. That report is what lets the electorate be counted rather than guessed at.

Returns are the right instrument for the first three tests because they are complete and official: every accepted ballot is in them. And the same voters appear in four contests on one afternoon’s ballot, which is the natural experiment the first test runs on. The returns-source chapter is this data’s biography: what a return is, who certifies it, and the one thing it can never contain, which is any record of an individual voter. That missing record is exactly where the fourth test will end.

The data

The build reduces the state’s contest-level files to a handful of county tables. county_results.csv holds every candidate in every county: county, candidate, votes, county_total and share. deviations.csv holds each county’s share for each of the four statewide winners, the winner’s statewide share, and the deviation between them. That deviation is what makes a five-way race and a two-way race readable on one scale. benchmark.csv pairs each county’s donalds_dev with desantis_dev from 2018 and the residual, meaning what is left over after you subtract what 2018 predicts. electorate.csv carries each county’s registered Republicans by race from the book-closing report, and regions.csv and williams.csv aggregate the same numbers by region and candidate.

One constructed column is a decision worth knowing about. The regions are this brief’s own: sixteen Panhandle counties, thirteen in the Big Bend and north central belt, and the rest of Florida, assigned by geography before any result was read.

Before you read on, commit to a number. The last contested Republican primary for Florida governor was 2018: Ron DeSantis against Adam Putnam. DeSantis is white, and was endorsed by Trump, as Donalds was. Write down how far below his statewide share you think DeSantis ran in these same Panhandle counties. Then write down whether you expect his county-by-county spread to be wider or narrower than Donalds’. Both numbers, before you read on.

What it says about democracy

Test one: the same voters, four times

The claim has an obvious rival. Maybe north Florida just votes against whoever is winning.

That is checkable, because four statewide Republican primaries ran on the same ballot that day. Governor, US Senator, Chief Financial Officer, Commissioner of Agriculture. Same voters, same ballot, same afternoon. If a region is simply hostile to frontrunners, all four winners take the hit.

Region Donalds (Gov) Moody (Sen) Ingoglia (CFO) Simpson (Agr)
Panhandle −7.5 +0.6 −5.1 +1.1
Big Bend / north central −18.7 +1.4 −6.5 −1.7
rest of Florida +1.7 −0.1 +0.9 −0.1

They do not all take the hit. In the Panhandle, Moody ran +0.6 and Simpson +1.1 against their own statewide shares, while Donalds ran −7.5. In the Big Bend the gap is wider still: Donalds −18.7.

So it is not turnout, and it is not a regional habit of rejecting frontrunners. Something about the governor’s race specifically. That is a real finding and it survives the rest of this brief.

Test two: the same map, eight years earlier

DeSantis’ spread across the 67 counties was 51.9 points. Donalds’ was 47.8. The white candidate’s map was the more uneven of the two.

The two maps correlate at 0.72. Counties that ran below DeSantis in 2018 ran below Donalds in 2026, in nearly the same order, at nearly the same size. In the Panhandle, DeSantis ran −5.2 against his statewide share and Donalds ran −7.5 — a difference of 2.2 points. Most of what the first map shows you was already there before Donalds ran.

Not all of it, and the exceptions are where the claim has its best evidence. Okaloosa and Santa Rosa are large, white, heavily Republican Panhandle counties holding about fifty thousand primary votes between them, and both sat above DeSantis’ statewide share in 2018. In 2026 both sat well below Donalds’.

Test three: where the missing votes went

A deficit is also a surplus somewhere. Donalds’ missing points in north Florida went to somebody, and who received them narrows the question a great deal.

Candidate Statewide Panhandle Big Bend / north central Rest of Florida
Donalds 47.8 40.3 29.1 49.5
Collins 25.2 29.0 32.1 24.4
Fishback 10.5 11.0 12.1 10.3
Renner 8.5 11.4 10.4 8.1
Williams 4.1 4.2 13.7 3.6

In the Panhandle they went mostly to Collins and Renner, and Renner is a former House speaker from Palm Coast — a north Florida figure. In the Big Bend something else happened. Bobby Williams took 4.1% of the state and 13.7% of the Big Bend. A candidate with four per cent of the state absorbed roughly half the region’s shortfall. So where is his vote?

Not in one region. His best county is Lafayette at 28.5%. The rest of his top ten includes Glades, Hardee and Okeechobee, which are cattle and citrus counties in the middle of the peninsula, three hundred miles from the Panhandle. Hardee is 42.9% Hispanic and gave Donalds 27.6%.

That footprint is agricultural. It is not northern, and it is not obviously racial. Whatever Williams was to those voters, he was the same thing to them in Okeechobee as in Lafayette.

Test four: the county’s people are not the primary’s voters

The natural next move is to put race in the picture directly: take each county’s share of Black residents and see whether Donalds ran worse where it is higher.

Do not make that move. It is the ecological fallacy: reasoning from a group’s share of an area to how members of that group behaved. Here it fails in an unusually clean way, because Florida registers voters by party and reports them by race, so the electorate can be counted. In the Panhandle, 7,544 of 525,542 registered Republicans are Black. That is 1.4%. A county’s population is not its primary electorate: Gadsden County is more than half Black and cast about two thousand Republican ballots.

County characteristic With his geography With what 2018 leaves Range across the 67 counties
Black % of registered Republicans 0.23 0.26 4.3 points
Hispanic % of registered Republicans 0.44 0.44 75.1 points
white % of registered Republicans -0.50 -0.47 79.7 points
Black % of county population -0.16 0.01 49.5 points

The county-population measure gives 0.01 against what 2018 leaves unexplained — nothing at all. Switching to the registration measure moves it to 0.26, which is weak and positive, meaning Donalds did slightly better where more Black Republicans live. Neither number is worth much, and the last column says why.

What the standard method would add, and where it stops

There is a standard method for questions like this. Racially polarized voting analysis, used in Voting Rights Act litigation, estimates how each racial group voted from precinct returns and precinct composition. The levels-of-aggregation chapter grades that family of methods against a counted truth and watches it return an impossible number without complaint.

The pieces are more available than they look. 17 of the 29 north Florida counties published precinct results, and they are in this brief’s raw/. Florida’s voter file is public by law and carries race, party and precinct on every record, so precinct composition can be counted rather than modelled. What stands between you and the file is a written request rather than a download link — a gap the voter-file-access brief measures across all fifty states. So the honest position is not that the method is impossible here. It is that it would tell you something you already know, and still not tell you the thing in dispute.

Region White share of registered Republicans Donalds So white Republicans gave him Window
Panhandle 91.3% 40.3% 34.6% to 44.2% 9.5 points
Big Bend / north central 91.5% 29.1% 22.5% to 31.8% 9.3 points
rest of Florida 76.3% 49.5% 33.8% to 64.9% 31.1 points
STATEWIDE 78.1% 47.8% 33.2% to 61.2% 28.0 points

Here is why. Where an electorate is nearly all white, the overall result very nearly is the white result, and no model is required to see it. 91.3% of the Panhandle’s registered Republicans are white, and Donalds took 40.3% there. Even if every non-white Republican in the region had voted for him, and even if none had, white Republicans gave him between 34.6% and 44.2%. That is a 9.5-point window, it needs no assumptions at all, and it is the method of bounds Duncan and Davis published in 1953.

Notice which way the homogeneity cuts. Statewide, the same calculation gives a 28.0-point window, which is useless, because Florida’s Republican electorate is 21.9% non-white and there is real room for the groups to differ. The uniformity that makes an estimate for Black Republicans hopeless is exactly what makes the white estimate nearly exact. In Holmes County, 36 of the registered Republicans are Black and 97.2% are white. Whatever happened in Holmes, white Republicans did it.

The wall is not measurement. It is attribution. We can say with confidence that somewhere around four in ten white Panhandle Republicans voted for Donalds. No voter file says why the other six did not. Was it because he is Black, because he is from Naples, because the governor’s office backed Jay Collins, or because Bobby Williams was the one who came to the county fair? The file has no column for it.

What this brief cannot tell you

It cannot tell you the claim is false. Nothing here rules out that some Panhandle Republicans declined to vote for a Black candidate. Okaloosa and Santa Rosa moved several points against the frontrunner relative to 2018, and no benign explanation offered here fully accounts for them.

It cannot tell you the claim is true. The region’s deficit is mostly older than the candidate, and a white candidate drew it in 2018. An agricultural candidate whose base runs to the bottom of the peninsula absorbs much of the rest. What is left does not track the racial composition of the electorate in either direction with any strength.

It cannot separate four things that move together. Donalds is Black, is from Naples, was Trump’s endorsee against the sitting governor’s ally, and is a metropolitan south Florida congressman. North Florida is distant from Naples, rural, agricultural, and was DeSantis’ base. Every one of those alignments predicts the same map, and 67 counties cannot pull them apart.

And it cannot be fixed with better data of this kind. More precincts, finer geography and newer census figures all leave the attribution wall exactly where it is. What would settle the claim is a different instrument: a survey of Republican primary voters with a candidate-choice question and a race question, or an experiment varying a candidate’s race. The returns record 1,697,262 decisions and not one reason. Whichever way you already lean on this claim, the map at the top of this brief is consistent with your view, which is the surest sign that it is not evidence.

What you should have learned

  • A viral claim can be turned into a question and tested: the returns confirm a real, governor’s-race-specific deficit — Donalds ran −7.5 in the Panhandle while the same voters gave Moody +0.6 on the same ballot.
  • Most of the pattern predates the candidate: the 2026 map correlates at 0.72 with DeSantis’ 2018 map, and the white candidate’s spread, 51.9 points, was wider than Donalds’ 47.8.
  • Reasoning from a county’s population to its voters is the ecological fallacy; counting the actual electorate changes the answer, because only 1.4% of the Panhandle’s registered Republicans are Black.
  • Where an electorate is 91.3% white, arithmetic alone bounds the white vote inside a 9.5-point window — and no data of this kind can say why anyone voted.

Extensions

  • benchmark.csv has each county’s residual — what 2018 does not explain. Sort it and look at the top and bottom fifteen. Do they share anything the brief did not name, and would you have called that a racial pattern if you saw it first?
  • williams.csv gives Bobby Williams’ share in every county. Pick his fifteen best and find them in electorate.csv. Are they alike in the composition of their Republican electorate, or only in being rural?
  • deviations.csv has all four statewide winners in all 67 counties. Ingoglia is the one whose deviation pattern most resembles Donalds’. Work out where the two agree and where they part. What does the agreement rule out?
  • electorate.csv has registered Republicans by race beside regions.csv. Work out how many Black Republicans live in the counties where Donalds ran furthest behind. Could their votes have moved his share in those counties at all?
  • county_results.csv has every candidate in every county. Collins and Renner both gained where Donalds lost. Which of the two better predicts the counties Donalds lost ground in, and what would each answer imply?
  • Stretch. The precinct returns for 17 north Florida counties are in this brief’s raw/. Compute the method of bounds for one county’s precincts and see how much narrower the windows get.
  • Stretch. Write the records request for Florida’s voter file and count one county’s Republican electorate by precinct and race. Does the answer land inside the window the brief already knew?

precinct on every record, is public and free but needs a written
request to the Division of Elections; the chapter explains why having
it would not change the answer.
