The widest gap since Reconstruction, and a chamber nobody redistricts

Congress is more divided than it has ever been. You have read that sentence in a newspaper and heard it from a member of Congress. This brief asks how anybody could know. Nobody can watch a legislator’s opinions, and nobody can put nine hundred million dollars of campaign rhetoric on a ruler. What the country has instead is a complete record of how every member voted, and the question is whether a number worth arguing about can be built from that alone.

The answer matters beyond bookkeeping. The American system was built on the assumption that the parties would overlap. Separated powers, two chambers, the filibuster and the committee system all need cross-party coalitions to produce anything. When the parties are internally unified and far apart, those features stop working as checks and start working as blockages.

So the question splits in two. Can polarization be measured from votes and nothing else, and if it can, is the country’s favourite explanation for it right? The answer to the first is yes. The answer to the second is no, and the file says so plainly.

The test

The measure is DW-NOMINATE, computed for every member of the House and Senate from 1879 to 2025. It is published by Voteview, a project run by Jeffrey Lewis, Keith Poole, Howard Rosenthal and their colleagues. It arrives as one file, HSall_members.csv, with no key and no account: one row per member per Congress, carrying a coordinate on a line running from about −1 to +1.

The coordinate is manufactured, and the roll-call chapter says what it is manufactured from. Congress records how every member voted on every recorded vote, and for most of two centuries records nothing at all about what the vote was about. A scaling method reads that grid of agreement and disagreement, then arranges members on a line so that members who vote together sit near each other. Nobody reads a bill, and nobody labels a policy as liberal or conservative. That the line falling out of the arithmetic looks like left and right is a finding rather than an assumption, and it is the most remarkable thing about the method.

This file is the right evidence here for a reason that is easy to state and hard to satisfy. The question is about change across 146 years, and comparability over that span cannot be added afterwards. Every Congress is scaled in one estimation, so a coordinate from 1879 and a coordinate from 2025 sit on the same ruler. Nothing else in American politics does that. The cost is that no government agency produces the file, and if the project ever stopped there is no other file in the world that would do its job.

The data

One row is one member in one Congress. bioname is the name, state and district_code the seat, and party the party. congress is the numbered Congress and year the calendar year it convened. dim1 is the left–right coordinate: negative is liberal, positive is conservative. dim2 is a second dimension, which for most of the twentieth century carried the split over race and region and now barely moves.

That table is the download reduced by five decisions, and each one is a decision rather than a cleanup. Presidents are dropped. They carry coordinates too, but theirs are estimated from announced positions rather than from roll calls, so folding them into a chamber median would average two different measurements together. Only the two major parties are kept, which means every independent in American history leaves, including the ones who caucus with a party and vote with it. Members with no coordinate are dropped rather than carried as blanks, and those are people who served too briefly for the model to place them.

Two more decisions set the frame of every figure below. year is computed from the Congress number rather than read, because the download carries no year at all: the first Congress met in 1789 and a new one begins every two years. The series then starts at the 46th Congress, in 1879, on the judgment that earlier party labels do not mean the same thing and rest on far fewer recorded votes.

Note what a row is not. It is not a survey answer, a statement of belief, or anything the member said out loud. It is a position estimated from votes cast, and it exists only because other members voted too.

Before you read on, write down a number. The most common explanation for congressional polarization is gerrymandering: safe districts produce members who fear only a primary challenge and therefore drift to the extremes. Now consider the Senate. Senators represent whole states, and nobody draws a state line. If districting is the engine, the Senate should have polarized markedly less than the House. How much less? Half as much, a quarter, not at all — commit to a fraction before you look.

What it says about democracy

Take the median member of each party in each Congress and subtract one from the other. That distance is the entire measurement, and it is arithmetic anybody can check.

Congress Year Median Democrat Median Republican Distance
90 1967 -0.343 0.241 0.584
100 1987 -0.320 0.346 0.666
110 2007 -0.379 0.426 0.805
119 2025 -0.396 0.530 0.925

The gap between the House parties was 0.584 in 1967. It is 0.925 now, the widest in a series reaching back to 1879. The sentence everybody repeats is true on this measure, and it is not close.

Two exceptions matter more than the headline. The narrowest Congress on record is 0.498, in 1947, in the middle of the century that produced almost all of our intuitions about how Congress is supposed to work. The 1890s then reached 0.852, in 1895, not far below today. The shape of the series is a U, and the bipartisan era sits at the bottom of it. Nobody can want the mid-century comity without the party system that produced it, which included conservative Southern Democrats and ended with the civil-rights realignment.

Two lines rather than one, because the comparison between the chambers is the test: the House is redistricted every ten years and the Senate has never been redistricted at all. If partisan districting were the engine, the Senate line should be visibly flatter than the House one. Hover for both values at any year.

Chamber Gap in that year Gap now Change
House 0.584 0.925 +0.342
Senate 0.587 0.917 +0.331

They are nearly identical. The House went from 0.584 to 0.925, a change of 0.342. The Senate went from 0.587 to 0.917, a change of 0.331. Two chambers, one drawn by partisan legislatures every ten years and one never drawn at all, polarized in near-lockstep over sixty years.

That is real evidence against the districting explanation, and it is worth sitting with, because gerrymandering is the story most people arrive holding. Do not overclaim in the other direction either. The result shows that districting is not sufficient to explain polarization. It does not show that districting has no effect. Both chambers face primary electorates, national donors and the same media environment, and shared causes like those would produce exactly this pattern.

The middle emptied in both chambers as well. The 107th Congress, in 2001, was the last in which a single Republican in the House sat to the left of a single Democrat. There were 2 of them, and since then the count has been zero every time. The Senate followed one Congress later.

Zero overlap is not itself new, and it happened repeatedly between 1879 and 1919. What is unprecedented is zero overlap alongside a gap this wide. Two measures tell two different stories, and only their combination is new.

Figure 2. The House of 2025, one dot per member. Position along the axis is the member’s first-dimension coordinate; vertical position is random and carries no information, so that overlapping members can be told apart. Two clouds, and nothing between them. The separation is not something anybody put into the method, which never saw a party label. It is what the voting produced.

The two parties both moved, and they did not move equally. Since 1967 the Democratic median in the House has moved 0.053 to the left, and the Republican median 0.289 to the right, which is 5.5 times as far. This is the finding called asymmetric polarization, and it is among the most contested claims in the study of Congress. The argument is not about the arithmetic. It is about what the scale is anchored to, which the next section takes up.

What this brief cannot tell you

The scale is relative to the votes that were actually held, and that is the limit built into the file. If Congress stops voting on some issues and starts voting on others, a member who never changed their mind can move on the scale. Bateman, Clinton and Lapinski (2017) rebuilt the estimates holding the policy content of votes fixed, and found today’s parties less divided than the standard series suggests. “Republicans moved right” and “the agenda moved and Republicans stood still” produce the same picture in this data, and nothing inside it can tell them apart.

Nothing here is a member changing their mind. In this file every member carries one pair of coordinates for a whole career. Of the 6,936 House members here, only 8 have more than one value of dim1, and those are people who left Congress and later came back. Every movement in a party median is somebody arriving or leaving.

The file attaches no uncertainty to a coordinate, so two members a few hundredths apart are not actually distinguishable. It also cannot separate a sincere vote from a strategic one, because a recorded vote is an act rather than a preference. And all of it is 535 people. Whether the public polarized alongside its legislature is a different question, and the roll calls cannot speak to it.

What you should have learned

  • The distance between the House party medians has risen from 0.584 in 1967 to 0.925 today, the widest in a series that reaches back to 1879.
  • The series is a U rather than a slope: its narrowest Congress is 0.498, in 1947, and the 1890s reached 0.852.
  • The Senate went from 0.587 to 0.917 over the same span, in a chamber whose boundaries nobody draws, so partisan districting cannot be the main engine of polarization.
  • A DW-NOMINATE coordinate is manufactured from the pattern of who voted with whom. It says who a member voted with, never what they voted for.
  • The scale is relative to the agenda, and no party median here moves because somebody changed their mind: only 8 of 6,936 House members in the file have more than one coordinate.

Extensions

None of these is answered above, and every one can be answered from the table the Sources section hands over.

  • nominate_members.csv has dim1, party and year for every member of every Congress. Average dim1 inside each party in each year and plot both lines. Which party moved, and when?
  • The same file has chamber. Do the party-median calculation separately for House and Senate rows in the same congress. Does the chamber that draws no districts look different from the one that does?
  • dim2 is the second dimension. Compute its spread across House members in each year. When did it stop mattering, and what issue left American politics with it?
  • state and bioname name the seat and the person. Filter to one state and follow its delegation’s dim1 across Congresses. Is the state moving, or are its members being replaced?

Two stretch ideas point past the spreadsheet. Voteview also publishes coordinates re-estimated inside each Congress rather than fixed across a career; fetch those columns and redo the party medians, then ask whether the widening changes shape once members are allowed to move. And Boris Shor and Nolan McCarty publish comparable scores for all fifty state legislatures — pick your own and ask whether it polarized on the same timetable as Congress.
