“The most conservative Court since the 1930s.”

Sentences like that are written constantly, and every one of them quietly requires a number. Nine people who never stand for election, answer no surveys and give no interviews about their views are nonetheless described in print by adjectives that nobody defines and everybody uses: moderate, conservative, the swing vote. Those adjectives decide how nominations are fought and how confirmation votes are cast.

The Supreme Court is the easiest place in American politics to put a number behind them. Nine people, every vote recorded, every case public, and a database that has counted all of it since 1946. So the question this chapter asks is not where the justices sit, which everybody thinks they know. It is whether a number that places them can be built without anybody supplying an opinion about ideology — and, if one can, what exactly that number is made of.

It matters because the Court is the only branch that never has to explain itself in electoral terms, which makes the informal vocabulary unusually load-bearing. Putting a number behind it changes the argument. Segal and Cover (1989) derived justices’ values from pre-confirmation newspaper editorials, that is, from perceptions recorded before any vote was cast, and found they predicted votes well. Martin and Quinn (2002) built the standard dynamic score directly from votes, allowing positions to move over time. Both turned a rhetorical claim into a measurable one, and both are now routine inputs to research on judicial behavior (Epstein, Landes and Posner, 2013).

The votes, not the scores

Everything here comes from the Supreme Court Database, held at Washington University in St. Louis: every justice’s vote in every case the Court has decided since 1946. It is taken from the justice-centered file organized by citation, 2024 release 01.

It is unlike most of the sources in this book, because no statute required it and no organization produced it while doing something else. Harold Spaeth started it as a research project, the National Science Foundation paid for it, and academics have maintained it ever since. It exists because somebody decided the Court’s behavior was worth writing down in a form that could be counted. The releases are numbered and archived, so naming one identifies exactly what was read, which is more than a versioned news graphic can offer. It is also, in 2026, served over http and not https, a small fact about maintenance that will eventually become a large one.

Choosing this file rather than an easier one is most of the chapter’s argument. Martin-Quinn scores are published, updated every term, and would answer where does each justice sit in a single download. They are not used, for two reasons.

The practical one is that the site sits behind a bot check and will not answer a script. The real one is that a published score is the answer, and this chapter is about how the answer gets made. The database supplies the votes those scores are computed from, so the ordering recovered below can be checked, broken on purpose and argued with rather than accepted on authority. The data best suited to a question is not always the data closest to it.

The difficulty is that the most important column in the file is not an observation. direction records whether a decision came out in the conservative or the liberal direction. That is a coder’s reading of a written opinion against a codebook, a rule specifying issue area by issue area which outcome counts as which. Where the codebook supplies no rule the field is left blank.

That is not a defect anybody could repair. Nothing measures the ideological direction of a legal holding. Every number ever published about the Court’s ideology rests on somebody having read the cases and decided.

What can be asked is whether the rule was applied the same way across 78 terms of American legal history. That is a question about a codebook rather than about a Court.

direction is not the exception in this file. It is the rule, made visible. Issue area, the constitutional provision at stake, which party prevailed, the reason the Court took the case. Each of those is likewise a person’s reading of a document, entered against a rule somebody wrote. The Supreme Court publishes opinions. It publishes no data.

Everything countable about the Court was made countable by someone outside it, decades after some of the cases were decided. The resulting file arrives looking like a record of what happened. It is a record of what a coding team concluded had happened.

That is the price of having a dataset at all here, and it is not avoidable. It is also entirely invisible from inside the CSV, where a judgment and an observation are both just a number in a column.

The cleaning is short and consequential. The release is downloaded whole, and the terms from 2020 to 2023 are kept. Every case whose direction is blank is dropped from the agreement counts, because “voted the same way” has no meaning when there is no direction to compare.

The build then writes the four small tables this chapter reads. One choice in that build does more work than the rest: for every pair of justices it records the number of cases as well as the number of agreements, rather than the percentage alone. That is why the pair who never sat together is visible in what follows instead of silently absent. Nothing was filled in at build time. The one fill in this chapter is made in the open, on the page, and undone again a few pages later.

61 columns. Read down them and sort each into two piles. Things that could be checked against the U.S. Reports by anyone with a library card, and things that exist because a person read an opinion and reached a conclusion. The first pile is small — usCite, dateDecision, docket, majVotes, justiceName. Nearly everything else is in the second: certReason (why the Court took the case), issueArea, partyWinning, precedentAlteration, lcDispositionDirection, declarationUncon, and the column this chapter turns on, direction.

Every value in that row except the name and the date is an integer standing for a word. issueArea 8 is economic activity. direction 1 is conservative and 2 is liberal, and which is which depends on the issue area, by a rule the codebook sets out case type by case type. In a criminal case a vote for the defendant is liberal; in an economic case a vote for the government usually is. vote 2 is not “two votes”; it is a code meaning the justice dissented from the opinion of the Court. majority 1 means the same thing again, in a different vocabulary.

That last redundancy is the point. The file says the same fact three times in three encodings, and a reader who summed vote across a term, or averaged issueArea, would get a number with no referent whatever. None of these columns is a quantity, and every one of them will do arithmetic if you ask.

Where the codebook has no rule, direction is blank rather than guessed. That is the honest choice, and it has a cost. Those cases cannot enter a pairwise agreement count, because “voted the same way” means nothing when there is no way to compare. The build drops them, and that is the only filtering it does.

Two names, a count of cases they both voted in, and a count of times they agreed. That is all this chapter’s central figure is built from, and the unit of analysis has moved twice to get here: from a justice-vote, to a case, to a pair of justices. Nothing in the source file is a pair. The pairs were built by taking every justice against every other, finding the cases in which both cast a directional vote, and counting matches. That is why the cases column had to be kept beside agree, rather than reduced to a percentage on the spot. A pair who never sat together would otherwise have arrived as a percentage of nothing, and disappeared.

The score falls out

Now the move that makes DW-NOMINATE, Martin-Quinn and every other ideal-point score work. Treat agreement as closeness and disagreement as distance. Then ask: if these 10 people had to be arranged along a single line so that people who agree sit near each other, what line would that be?

Gorsuch -0.132

Kagan 0.206

Sotomayor 0.246

Read the order. Thomas, Alito, Gorsuch, Barrett, Roberts, Kavanaugh, then a gap, then Jackson, Kagan, Breyer, Sotomayor.

That is the ideological ordering of the Roberts Court as any court reporter in America would give it, and it came out of 45 agreement rates and nothing else.

One warning about that column of numbers, and it governs every figure below. The method recovers distances, not coordinates. Multiply every position by -1 and every pairwise distance is unchanged, so the fit is unchanged. A scaling solution can be flipped, or in more than one dimension turned, at no cost whatever.

Which end comes out negative is a convention of the arithmetic, not a finding: the order and the spacing mean something; the direction does not. The same is true of Martin-Quinn, of NOMINATE, and of every ideal-point estimate anybody will ever quote at you. The orientation is always supplied by the analyst after the fact.

The scaling was handed pairwise agreement rates and nothing else — no case subjects, no vote directions, no party of appointing president. Set its output beside each justice’s share of conservative votes, a column it never touched, and the two line up almost exactly. The correlation is -0.975. Its size, 0.975, is the finding; its sign, by the paragraph above, is not.

That is what an ideal-point score is. A summary of who agrees with whom, which turns out to line up with something the culture already had a word for.

Quantity Value
Span of the 6 on one side 0.145
Span of the 4 on the other 0.095
Distance across the gap between them 0.224

And it is not a spectrum. The 6 justices on one side span 0.145; the single step across the middle is 0.224, larger than that entire bloc. This is not a line with people spread evenly along it. It is two clusters with a chasm between them.

At this point the score looks like a measurement of nine people. There are 45 agreement rates behind it, a recovered ordering that matches conventional wisdom without having been told it, and a correlation of 0.975 in magnitude with a variable the model never saw. It is worth pausing over how much of that would survive if one of the 45 numbers turned out not to exist.

That fill is a modeling decision, made here, in one line of code. It is about the most conservative guess available, since it assumes nothing whatever about whether the two would have agreed. It is still a guess, sitting in the middle of the matrix, carrying exactly the same weight in the arithmetic as the 44 real observations around it.

Refusing to guess

So refuse. Drop both justices entirely and re-run the scaling on the 8 who overlap completely. No fill, no guess, no cell without data in it.

Before you look at the table, say what it will show. That fill is an invented number sitting in the middle of the matrix the entire ordering came out of. Take it away and how much of the ordering survives? Does it stand exactly as it did? Does it come apart? Or something between — and if between, how many of the 8 justices end up somewhere other than where the filled version put them? Commit to a count, and to one more answer: whichever way the individual ranks fall, do the two blocs survive intact?

Most readers commit to one of the two extremes — that removing an invented cell changes nothing, or that it brings the whole ordering down. Both are wrong, and being wrong in either direction is what makes the actual answer worth having: 4 positions move out of 8, and every one of the moves is between neighbors.

This is the turn, and it cuts two ways at once.

The recovered order is not a fact about the justices at fine resolution. Adjacent positions moved because of a decision about a cell that contains no data. Anybody reporting which of Thomas and Alito sits further out is reporting a fill choice as much as the Court’s behavior.

And the blocs do not move. Six on one side, the rest on the other, in both versions, with the chasm in the same place. The correlation with conservative voting comes out at 0.990 in size, on 8 justices with no guess in the matrix at all. That is slightly stronger than the 0.975 obtained with one. The coarse structure is in the data. The fine structure is in the assumptions.

Published scores make dozens of choices of this kind. Martin-Quinn makes distributional assumptions and lets positions drift over time; NOMINATE fixes a scale and a functional form. The difference is not that this one has assumptions and those do not. It is that this one is visible, and reversible in a single line.

Figure 4. Share of decisions coded conservative, every term from 1946 to 2023. The gray line is the term-by-term figure and the red line a five-term moving average; the dotted rule is half. The series runs from a low of 21.8% in 1963 to a high of 65.1% in 2021, and every point in it is a coder’s judgment about the direction of a decision.

Before believing any story in that line, notice exactly what is plotted. It is the share of decisions that the database’s coders labeled conservative: a human judgment, applied case by case, for 78 years, by people following a codebook. It is a good codebook, and a case coded conservative in 1962 may have almost nothing in common with one coded conservative in 2022.

That is not a reason to discard the series. It is a reason to say what it is. Every long-run measure in social science is somebody’s coding decision repeated for decades, and the ones worth using say so.

The argument about this

On circularity. The strongest defense of what was done above is that the ordering was not put in. The scaling was given 44 numbers and no labels, and the dimension it found happens to be the one the culture already names. That is a genuine finding about the structure of judicial voting. The strongest objection is that agreement and ideology may simply be the same phenomenon observed twice, in which case a correlation of 0.975 is a tautology with extra steps. Both positions are defensible, and neither is refutable from this table.

On what a score can be used for. Martin and Quinn (2002) built the standard version precisely because a static score cannot show drift, which is the question people most want answered about long-serving justices. Segal and Cover (1989) went the other way and measured ideology before any votes existed, from newspaper editorials, so that votes could be predicted rather than summarized. The three designs answer three different questions, and treating them as competing estimates of one quantity is a category error.

On the majority rates. The arch in Figure 3 looks like a finding about power, and the orange points show it is substantially a finding about which cases exist. Whether “the middle wins” is a claim about the Court or about its docket is not settled here — and the docket is chosen by the same nine people.

On whether “conservative” is constant. The database codes each decision’s direction case by case, following a codebook, for 78 years. It is a human judgment repeated across an enormous span of American legal history, and Figure 4 is only as comparable across its two ends as that codebook is.

On agreement itself. Two justices can reach the same result from incompatible doctrines. Agreement is not agreement about reasons, and the reasons are most of what law professors argue about.

What this evidence will bear

The Supreme Court Database records every vote in every case since 1946, coded consistently by a documented codebook and maintained as a versioned academic resource. The window scaled here is a complete census of the Court’s decisions rather than a sample. And the method is transparent enough to be broken on purpose, which is what the second half of this chapter does to it.

The limits bound different claims, and each has already appeared above in its proper place. One cell has no data in it, and the fill chosen for it moves 4 positions in the recovered order, so no fine ranking here is safe. Two justices sat for part of the window, so their statistics rest on different denominators from everybody else’s, which is why they are hollow in Figure 3 and absent from the close-case table. One dimension was chosen and found to fit well, which is not the same as discovering that judicial disagreement is one-dimensional. “Conservative” is a coder’s judgment applied for 78 years, and agreement is not agreement about reasons.

And the scaling is a snapshot, in which nobody can move. It cannot show a justice drifting, which is exactly what Martin-Quinn was designed to capture.

Three questions stay genuinely open. The recovered dimension may be ideology, or it may be something else that moves with it: deference to precedent, institutional caution, the identity of the litigants. Agreement rates cannot settle that. Whether coding direction is stable across seventy-eight years is a question about the codebook rather than about the Court. And whether the Court’s high agreement rates reflect consensus or docket selection is unanswerable here, because the justices choose which cases to hear, which makes the denominator endogenous to the thing being measured.

What can be said is worth the trouble it took. A left–right ordering of the Supreme Court can be recovered from 44 agreement rates that carry no information about ideology, cases or appointing presidents. It lines up with a column the method never saw, at a size of 0.975. The blocs in it are in the data, and survive refusing to guess about the one pair who never met. The fine ordering is in the assumptions, and does not.

Every score has a fill. Somewhere inside every published ideal-point estimate is a cell with no data in it and a decision about what to put there. The question to ask of any such number is not whether it made assumptions, because all of them do. It is which ones, and what happens when you change them.

Your turn
