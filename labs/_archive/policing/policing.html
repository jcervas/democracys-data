Where did that number come from?

“Black drivers are pulled over more often.”

Easy to say, hard to prove — and the reason is worth being precise about. To know whether drivers of one race are stopped more than they should be, you would need to know how many were driving, and how many were doing something stop-worthy. Nobody records that. The denominator does not exist.

So the honest version of the question has to be built differently. There is a way, and it does not need the denominator at all. Ten steps.

Step 1 — What one record is

The source file has one row per stop. We work from a collapsed version, but the fields it was collapsed from decide everything below:

data.frame(
  field = c("date, time", "location", "subject_race", "reason_for_stop",
            "search_conducted", "contraband_found", "outcome"),
  holds = c("when", "district or intersection",
            "the OFFICER'S PERCEPTION, entered on a form",
            "the violation cited", "TRUE / FALSE",
            "TRUE / FALSE — recorded only if a search happened",
            "warning, citation, arrest"))
#>              field                                             holds
#> 1       date, time                                              when
#> 2         location                          district or intersection
#> 3     subject_race       the OFFICER'S PERCEPTION, entered on a form
#> 4  reason_for_stop                               the violation cited
#> 5 search_conducted                                      TRUE / FALSE
#> 6 contraband_found TRUE / FALSE — recorded only if a search happened
#> 7          outcome                         warning, citation, arrest

Two of those matter enormously.

subject_race is what the officer believed, not self-report. For a question about the officer’s behaviour that is arguably the right variable — but it is not the census’s variable, and the two must never be mixed.

contraband_found exists only when a search happened. You never learn what an unsearched driver was carrying. That absence is the whole problem.

905,070 stops over ten years in one city, and searches happen in about 1.6% of them. Everything interesting is inside that small slice.

Black drivers were 16.8% of stops. San Francisco’s Black population over this period was roughly 5–6% of residents.

It is very tempting to put those two numbers next to each other.

Step 4 — Why that comparison does not work

Do not put them next to each other. The residential population is not the population at risk of being stopped.

San Francisco is a commuter city — a large share of weekday cars belong to people who live elsewhere. Driving is not distributed like residence: it varies with car ownership, work schedules, and which roads run through which neighbourhoods.

And even a perfect count of drivers would not be enough. You would also need to know how many drivers of each group were doing something that warranted a stop. Nobody records that. Nobody could.

data.frame(
  you_would_need = c("Who was driving", "Where and when",
                     "Who committed a violation", "Who was carrying contraband"),
  does_it_exist  = c("No", "No", "No", "Only for those searched"))
#>                you_would_need           does_it_exist
#> 1             Who was driving                      No
#> 2              Where and when                      No
#> 3   Who committed a violation                      No
#> 4 Who was carrying contraband Only for those searched

This is not a funding problem. There is no census of who was on the road. The disparity in Step 3 — real as the counts are — proves nothing on its own, and most public arguments about policing data stop right here, with both sides asserting a denominator neither can produce.

Step 5 — Condition on the stop

Change the question. Take the stop as given, and ask what happened next.

d$search_rate <- round(100 * d$searched / d$stops, 2)
d[order(-d$search_rate), c("race", "stops", "searched", "search_rate")]
#>                     race  stops searched search_rate
#> 3                  black 152196    23622       15.52
#> 4               hispanic 116014    11445        9.87
#> 5                  other 106858     3746        3.51
#> 1                  white 372318    11707        3.14
#> 2 asian/pacific islander 157684     2861        1.81

Black drivers were searched in 15.52% of stops. White drivers, 3.14% — nearly five times the rate.

Better than Step 3, because the comparison is now within a population the officer had already selected. Still not enough, and the objection is the standard one: perhaps the stopped groups genuinely differed. If officers were encountering more grounds for suspicion in one group, a higher search rate would follow from correct policing rather than a lower bar.

You cannot answer that with search rates. You would have to know how suspicious each driver actually was — the unobservable thing.

Step 6 — The move that gets around it

In 2001 three economists — Knowles, Persico and Todd — proposed a way out that needs no denominator at all. Condition one step further. Take the search as given, and ask what the officer found.

An officer searches when suspicion crosses some internal threshold. If the same threshold applies to everyone, the searches it authorises should succeed at roughly equal rates. A lower threshold means more searches on weaker evidence, and weaker evidence means a lower success rate. So unequal hit rates imply unequal thresholds.

The comparison is now between searches, not populations.

Before running the next chunk, predict. Given a search rate five times higher for Black drivers, should the share of searches finding contraband be higher, lower, or about the same?

Step 7 — What the searches found

d$hit_rate <- round(100 * d$contraband_found / d$searched, 1)
d[order(-d$search_rate), c("race", "searched", "contraband_found", "hit_rate")]
#>                     race searched contraband_found hit_rate
#> 3                  black    23622             2183      9.2
#> 4               hispanic    11445             1165     10.2
#> 5                  other     3746              761     20.3
#> 1                  white    11707             2834     24.2
#> 2 asian/pacific islander     2861             1033     36.1

Contraband was found in 9.2% of searches of Black drivers and 24.2% of searches of white drivers.

Searched 4.9 times as often, and found with something 2.6 times less often. Under the outcome test, that is evidence of a lower evidentiary bar.

Step 8 — Both numbers at once

One group could be a coincidence. Put every group on the same picture.

plot(d$search_rate, d$hit_rate, pch = 19, col = "#C41230", cex = 1.6,
     xlim = c(0, 17), ylim = c(0, 40),
     xlab = "% of stops that led to a search",
     ylab = "% of searches that found contraband")
text(d$search_rate, d$hit_rate, d$race, pos = 4, cex = 0.75)
abline(h = weighted.mean(d$hit_rate, d$searched), lty = 3, col = "grey50")

The relationship is monotone across all five groups: the more often a group was searched, the less often those searches found anything. Asian and Pacific Islander drivers were searched least — 1.81% — and had the highest hit rate at 36.1%.

That ordering is what makes an innocent explanation hard. It would have to cover the Black and Hispanic results and the reverse pattern for Asian drivers, simultaneously, across 905,070 stops.

Notice what this argument never needed: who was driving.

Step 9 — Is it just one bad year?

Ten years are stacked into every number above. A pattern that comes from one unusual year is a different finding from one that repeats.

y$hit <- round(100 * y$contraband_found / pmax(y$searched, 1), 1)
w <- y[y$race == "white", c("year", "hit")]
b <- y[y$race == "black", c("year", "hit")]
m <- merge(w, b, by = "year", suffixes = c("_white", "_black"))
m$white_higher <- m$hit_white > m$hit_black
m
#>    year hit_white hit_black white_higher
#> 1  2007      26.2      11.1         TRUE
#> 2  2008      24.2       9.9         TRUE
#> 3  2009      19.3       8.7         TRUE
#> 4  2010      27.6       8.9         TRUE
#> 5  2011      21.6       6.0         TRUE
#> 6  2012      20.7       5.9         TRUE
#> 7  2013      21.9       7.6         TRUE
#> 8  2014      30.7       9.4         TRUE
#> 9  2015      29.8      13.1         TRUE
#> 10 2016      28.2      16.0         TRUE
ks <- c("white", "black", "hispanic", "asian/pacific islander")
cl <- c("#2c7fb8", "#C41230", "#e08214", "#4d9221")
plot(NA, xlim = range(y$year), ylim = c(0, 50), las = 1, xlab = "",

In all ten years, searches of white drivers found contraband more often than searches of Black drivers. The levels move around a great deal — everyone’s hit rate falls through 2012 and rises after — but the ordering never flips.

Step 10 — What you can now say

You can say: within this department, over these ten years, searches of Black and Hispanic drivers were authorised on weaker evidence than searches of white drivers. That is a claim about the bar an officer applied, reached without ever knowing who was driving.

You cannot say how much of the gap is individual animus, how much is departmental policy, and how much is where officers were deployed. The data cannot separate them, and the distinction matters enormously for what anyone would do about it.

The objection that survives

The outcome test has one real weakness and you should be able to state it.

It compares average hit rates, and the theory is about the marginal search — the last, weakest one an officer decides to make. If the distribution of actual suspiciousness differs between groups, average hit rates can differ even when the bar is identical. Economists call this infra-marginality, and it is why the test is strong evidence rather than proof.

d$arrest_rate <- round(100 * d$arrests / d$stops, 2)
d[order(-d$search_rate), c("race", "search_rate", "hit_rate", "arrest_rate")]
#>                     race search_rate hit_rate arrest_rate
#> 3                  black       15.52      9.2        2.33
#> 4               hispanic        9.87     10.2        1.97
#> 5                  other        3.51     20.3        0.85
#> 1                  white        3.14     24.2        1.07
#> 2 asian/pacific islander        1.81     36.1        0.77

Take the objection seriously, then notice how much it has to do. To explain this table innocently you need a story in which Black drivers are searched five times as often for good reasons, and those reasons turn out wrong far more often, and the same holds for Hispanic drivers, and the reverse holds for Asian drivers. Each piece is possible. Together they are a lot to carry.

What this lab cannot tell you

  • Who was driving. The denominator problem never went away; we routed around it. Nothing here supports a claim about stop rates per driver.
  • Whether any individual officer discriminated. This is an aggregate pattern — it says something about a department and nothing about a person, the same gap between group and individual that runs through the RPV lab.
  • One city, and a decade old. San Francisco, through 2016. Do not generalise the numbers. The method generalises; Stanford has comparable files for dozens of jurisdictions.
  • Race as recorded — the officer’s perception, not self-report.
  • Whether “contraband found” is itself neutral. What counts, and whether a find gets recorded, are decisions by the same officer whose behaviour is being measured.
