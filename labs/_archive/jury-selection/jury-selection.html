Read jury-selection-brief.Rmd first. This lab runs the same numbered steps with the code exposed. The brief tells you what the question is and why it is hard; this shows you where each number came from.

Where did that number come from?

“The prosecutor struck Black jurors because they were Black.”

That is a constitutional violation. Batson v. Kentucky (1986) forbids striking a juror on account of race, and gives defendants a way to object.

It is also almost impossible to prove one strike at a time. A peremptory strike is the one a lawyer may use without giving a reason — that is what “peremptory” means. Challenged under Batson, a prosecutor need only offer some race-neutral explanation, and almost any explanation will do: the juror seemed inattentive, had a relative with a conviction, lived near the defendant.

In the thirty years after Batson, the North Carolina Supreme Court never once found a strike to be racially discriminatory.

So how would you ever show it? Not from one strike. From thousands.

Journalists at APM Reports spent a year reading court records from 225 trials over 26 years in Mississippi’s Fifth Circuit — every trial prosecuted by District Attorney Doug Evans’s office. 14,873 jurors, each with a race and a record of what happened to them.

They did it because of one defendant: Curtis Flowers, tried six times for the same crime by the same prosecutor. In Flowers v. Mississippi (2019) the Supreme Court threw out his conviction, and the majority relied heavily on the pattern of who Evans struck.

One prosecutor’s office, 26 years. That narrowness is the point: a national average would tell you about American prosecution, this tells you about a decision-maker.

Step 3 — The disparity

st <- s[s$side == "state", ]
st
#>    side  race eligible struck  pct
#> 1 state Black     1811    902 49.8
#> 2 state White     3318    372 11.2
round(st$pct[st$race == "Black"] / st$pct[st$race == "White"], 1)
#> [1] 4.4

The state struck 49.8% of the Black jurors it could strike, and 11.2% of the white ones — 4.4 times the rate.

Note the denominator, because it matters. A juror already removed for cause, or never reached, could not be struck by anybody. These rates count only jurors the state was actually able to strike — which is a stricter and fairer comparison than dividing by everyone in the room.

Step 4 — Why that is not yet an answer

A disparity is not proof. The reply is ready, and it is not frivolous: perhaps the Black jurors in these pools differed in some legitimate way — more of them had relatives with convictions, or expressed doubts about the death penalty. If those characteristics are unevenly distributed in a county with this history, a race-neutral striking rule produces a racial disparity on its own.

You cannot refute that with the table above, because it has no control group. What you need is a group of people who saw exactly the same jurors, in the same trials, under the same rules, and wanted the opposite outcome.

Step 5 — The control group is in the file

The defense strikes too. Same trials, same jury pools, same Batson rules, opposite incentives.

If Black jurors really were disproportionately strike-worthy for reasons both sides would recognise, the defense should strike them too — or at least not strike them dramatically less.

Before running the next chunk, write down what you expect the defense’s two numbers to look like under each explanation. The chunk is two lines and it will not wait for you.

Step 6 — The mirror

s
#>      side  race eligible struck  pct
#> 1   state Black     1811    902 49.8
#> 2   state White     3318    372 11.2
#> 3 defense Black      846    127 15.0
#> 4 defense White     2812   1330 47.3
m <- matrix(c(s$pct[s$side == "state"   & s$race == "Black"],
              s$pct[s$side == "state"   & s$race == "White"],
              s$pct[s$side == "defense" & s$race == "Black"],
              s$pct[s$side == "defense" & s$race == "White"]), nrow = 2)
barplot(m, beside = TRUE, names.arg = c("struck by the STATE", "struck by the DEFENSE"),
        col = c("#C41230", "#2c7fb8"), ylab = "% of eligible jurors struck",
        legend.text = c("Black jurors", "white jurors"), ylim = c(0, 60))

The state struck 49.8% of Black jurors and 11.2% of white. The defense struck 47.3% of white jurors and 15.0% of Black.

It is a mirror. Whatever made Black jurors worth striking to the prosecution made white jurors worth striking to the defense — at almost exactly the same rate, in the same courtrooms, from the same pools.

This is what makes the inference strong. The “Black jurors were different” explanation now has to explain why white jurors were equally different, in the opposite direction, to the opposite side. There is one variable that does that easily, and both sides were clearly using it.

Only one side’s use of it is unconstitutional in the way Batson was written to catch.

Step 7 — Now try to prove it in a single trial

Here is the difficulty at the centre of the law. Batson is litigated one trial at a time, in front of a judge who sees one jury pool. Aggregate patterns are not before the court.

So take the first trial in the file on its own terms and ask: if the state had chosen its strikes at random, how often would the split have come out this lopsided or worse? That is an urn problem, and R has it built in.

o <- t[1, ]
p1 <- phyper(o$black_struck - 1, o$black_eligible, o$white_eligible,
             o$black_struck + o$white_struck, lower.tail = FALSE)
c(black_eligible = o$black_eligible, black_struck = o$black_struck,
  white_eligible = o$white_eligible, white_struck = o$white_struck,
  probability = round(p1, 3))
#> black_eligible   black_struck white_eligible   white_struck    probability 
#>          3.000          3.000         12.000          3.000          0.044

About one chance in twenty-three. Suggestive, and nowhere near enough. A judge would be right to say that unlikely things happen in single trials.

A single trial almost never carries the evidence. That is not a flaw in this data — it is a description of how the rule works.

Step 8 — Twenty-six years of single trials

Compute that same probability for every trial and count.

t$p <- phyper(t$black_struck - 1, t$black_eligible, t$white_eligible,
              t$black_struck + t$white_struck, lower.tail = FALSE)
# The shortcut 0.05 * 211 assumes p-values spread evenly over 0 to 1. These are
# hypergeometric tests on small counts, so their p-values are discrete and the
# tests are conservative -- they fire LESS often than 5% of the time. Simulate
# the real null instead: redraw each trial's strikes from its own margins.
set.seed(20260810)
sim <- replicate(2000, {
  bs <- rhyper(nrow(t), t$black_eligible, t$white_eligible,
               t$black_struck + t$white_struck)
  sum(phyper(bs - 1, t$black_eligible, t$white_eligible,
             t$black_struck + t$white_struck, lower.tail = FALSE) < 0.05)
})
c(trials = nrow(t),
  significant_05 = sum(t$p < 0.05),
  expected_shortcut = round(0.05 * nrow(t), 1),
  expected_simulated = round(mean(sim), 1),
  significant_01 = sum(t$p < 0.01))
#>             trials     significant_05  expected_shortcut expected_simulated 
#>              211.0               99.0               10.6                3.5 
#>     significant_01 
#>               51.0

Ninety-nine of 211 trials clear the 0.05 threshold, where chance alone would produce about 3.5. That is about twenty-eight times as many. Note that the textbook shortcut, 0.05 x 211 = 10.6, is wrong here and wrong in the direction that understates the finding: discrete tests on small counts are conservative, so the real chance rate is lower than the nominal one.

Step 9 — Trial by trial, and the one at the far corner

t$black_rate <- ifelse(t$black_eligible > 0, 100 * t$black_struck / t$black_eligible, NA)
t$white_rate <- ifelse(t$white_eligible > 0, 100 * t$white_struck / t$white_eligible, NA)
k <- t$black_eligible >= 3 & t$white_eligible >= 3

c(trials = sum(k),
  struck_every_black_juror = sum(t$black_struck[k] == t$black_eligible[k]),
  struck_no_white_juror    = sum(t$white_struck[k] == 0))
#>                   trials struck_every_black_juror    struck_no_white_juror 
#>                      202                       11                       59
plot(jitter(t$white_rate[k], 2), jitter(t$black_rate[k], 2), pch = 19,
     col = adjustcolor("#C41230", 0.5), xlim = c(0, 100), ylim = c(0, 100),
     xlab = "% of eligible white jurors struck by the state",
     ylab = "% of eligible Black jurors struck by the state")
abline(0, 1, lty = 2)

Every point above the dashed line is a trial where the state struck Black jurors at a higher rate than white ones. 188 of 202 trials — 93% — are above it. In 11 trials the state struck every Black juror it could; in 59 it struck no white juror at all.

This is also the level at which the law operates. A Batson challenge happens inside one trial, where the numbers are small and a prosecutor can explain each strike individually. The pattern is only visible from above — and no individual defendant has 225 trials to point at.

Curtis Flowers did, eventually, because journalists built this file.

The most extreme trial in the file is the third trial of Curtis Flowers: fifteen Black jurors eligible and fifteen struck, twenty-one white jurors eligible and none. Roughly one chance in five billion.

Step 10 — What you can now say

You can say that in this district, over 26 years, the state’s strikes were not race-neutral — and that the conclusion does not rest on assuming which jurors should have been struck, because the defense’s mirror-image behaviour supplies the comparison.

You can also say something about the law. The pattern is overwhelming in aggregate and nearly invisible one trial at a time. Step 7 was not a detour: a 4.4% probability is what a typical Batson motion actually has to work with, and it loses. The rule is enforced at the only level where the evidence is weakest.

You cannot say why any individual juror was struck. No reasons are recorded, which is the whole design of a peremptory challenge.

What this lab cannot tell you

  • Why any individual juror was struck. Peremptory strikes require no reason, and the reasons given when challenged are not in this file. The pattern is aggregate; the law is individual, and that mismatch is the whole problem.
  • Whether Doug Evans is unusual. We have one district and one prosecutor. Studies in North Carolina and Philadelphia found similar patterns, but this file cannot establish that.
  • What race the jurors would have claimed. Race here was coded by journalists from court records and other sources — not self-report. For a third of jurors they could not determine it at all.
  • Whether the juries were wrong. Nothing here says anything about verdicts. It is about who got to sit.
