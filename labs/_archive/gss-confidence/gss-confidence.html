Read gss-confidence-brief.Rmd first. This lab runs the same numbered steps with the code exposed. The brief tells you what the question is and why it is hard; this shows you where each number came from.

Where did that number come from?

“Trust in institutions has collapsed.”

You have met surveys three times now: one instrument up close, one question checked against a public record, and many polls at once. This is the fourth thing a survey can be, and the rarest.

Since 1973, the General Social Survey has read the same sentence to Americans:

“I am going to name some institutions in this country. As far as the people running these institutions are concerned, would you say you have a great deal of confidence, only some confidence, or hardly any confidence at all in them?”

Same words. Same three options. Thirteen institutions. Fifty-one years, in 33 of which the question was asked.

That is worth pausing on. Every other trend in this course has an asterisk — the question changed, the mode changed, the sampling frame changed. Here almost nothing changed, which means when the number moves, something in the country moved.

In 1973, 24.1% of Americans had a great deal of confidence in the people running Congress. In 2024, 5.6%.

Roughly one in four became roughly one in eighteen. Meanwhile the share saying “hardly any” went the other way. This is the sentence everybody quotes, and here it is with the receipts.

Steps 6-7 — All thirteen, and the exception

Stop and commit to an answer first. You have just seen Congress fall from 24.1% to 5.6%. The obvious summary is Americans have lost confidence in their institutions. Thirteen institutions were asked in the same interview. How many of the thirteen do you expect to have fallen?

The interesting part is what did not happen, and you only see it because the same question was asked about thirteen things at once.

  • Organized religion −20.2, Congress −18.5, the press and education close behind.
  • The military ROSE 10.6 points, the only substantial gain.
  • Organized labor barely moved (+1.3) — and it started near the bottom.
  • The scientific community is almost flat (−4.8) across half a century.

Look at when the military moved. 32.7% in 1973, at the end of Vietnam — and 63.1% in 1991, the year of the Gulf War. It has drifted down since without ever returning to where it started.

“Trust in institutions collapsed” is not what this data says. Confidence in most institutions fell, one rose sharply, and two hardly moved. A single sentence about “institutions” averages over a pattern with structure in it.

Steps 8-9 — Where everything stands now, and fifty-one years at once

n <- aggregate(n ~ year, data = cf[cf$institution == "Congress", ], sum)
plot(n$year, n$n, type = "h", lwd = 3, col = "#2c7fb8",
     xlab = "", ylab = "respondents asked about Congress")

Two things to notice before trusting any of the above.

The survey is not annual. GSS ran yearly, then biennially, and the gaps are not evenly spaced. A line drawn through these points implies data between them that does not exist.

And 2021 was not a normal year. GSS could not interview face to face during the pandemic and moved online — the one break in an otherwise unbroken method. The answers from that year come from a different instrument wearing the same words, which is exactly the problem the rest of this lab is celebrating the absence of.

Step 10 — What you can now say

You can say that confidence in the people running most American institutions fell substantially between 1973 and 2024, that the decline is not confined to government or to politics, and that Medicine — not Congress — lost the most.

You can say it is neither universal nor irreversible. The military rose, and its series responds visibly to events: it peaks in 1991, the year of the Gulf War. Whatever is driving the other eleven is not an irreversible drift in national character.

You cannot say whether institutions became less trustworthy, whether people became less trusting, or whether the meaning of “a great deal of confidence” drifted over fifty years. The question measures a report, not a relationship.

Your turn

Change one thing, re-run, and write about what changed. Pick one.

Option A — A different institution. Pick one that interests you and plot its whole series. Find the year it moved most, and go and find out what happened that year. Does the event explain the move, or did you just find a story to fit?

Option B — The bottom, not the top. Everything above uses “a great deal.” Redo Step 6 with hardly_any. Do the institutions rank the same way? An institution can lose enthusiasts without gaining enemies, and the two columns tell different stories.

Option C — Ceiling and floor. Organized labor started at 16.1% and ended at 17.5% — but it may have moved a great deal in between. Plot it. What does a flat first-to-last comparison hide, and what does that imply about the table in Step 6?

Option D — Your own question. 13 institutions, 35 survey years, one unchanged sentence.

What this lab cannot tell you

  • Why anyone answered as they did. No reasons here, only the distribution.
  • Whether the institutions changed or the standards did. A falling number can mean worse performance or higher expectations, and the question cannot separate them. This is the deepest problem with any long attitude series.
  • What “the people running these institutions” means to a respondent. The wording is about people, not the institution — and whether respondents honour that distinction is unknown.
  • Anything about non-respondents. GSS response rates have fallen sharply over fifty years, like every survey’s. The weights adjust for what is known; the people who stopped answering may differ in ways nobody measured.

Where the data came from

General Social Survey, NORC at the University of Chicago — cumulative file 1972–2024, release 3a. 75,699 respondents. https://gss.norc.org/us/en/gss/get-the-data.html

The confidence battery has been asked with identical wording since 1973. Estimates are weighted with wtssps, the only weight covering every year in the file.

The build script uses one package, haven, and it is the only place in this course that does: the cumulative file is a Stata 13+ .dta, which base R cannot read. The script converts it once to the CSVs this lab reads.
