Where did that number came from?

“Turnout among registered Black voters was up six points.”

You have read a sentence like that. Today you meet the file it came from.

Every state maintains a list of everyone registered to vote. It is a public record, it has your name on it if you are registered, and it is the single most consequential dataset in American campaigning — every targeting operation in Session 5 was aimed at rows in a file like this one.

But it was not built for research. It was built so that a poll worker can find you in a book and hand you the right ballot. Almost everything odd about it follows from that.

We are using Houston County, Georgia: 127,560 registered voters. Georgia because it is one of a handful of states that records race on the registration form — collected for Voting Rights Act compliance, not for anybody’s convenience. That makes this file unusually revealing, and it is why Sessions 7 and 14 both come back to it.

Twenty-one of the fifty-three columns exist to place you in a district. Congressional, state senate, state house, judicial, county commission, school board, city council, municipal school board, water board, fire — plus two kinds of precinct, and a “Combo” field that bundles the whole stack into one code.

That is the file telling you what it is for. It is not a description of the electorate. It is a routing table. Everything a researcher wants from it — who registered when, who turned out, who lives where — is a by-product of the county’s need to hand you the correct piece of paper.

The column is called “Last Party Voted” and it is the one people misread most.

Georgia has no party registration. You cannot register as a Democrat there. What this column records is the last party’s primary ballot you happened to request — which is why 40,663 voters have nothing in it at all, and another 48,603 are marked non-partisan.

tot   <- rowSums(rp[, -1])

So seventy percent of this file carries no usable party signal, and it is worst for the groups with fewest voters. Anybody who tells you how many registered Democrats there are in Georgia is either using a different state’s data or making it up.

Part 3 — Who counts as registered

st <- read.csv("data/status.csv", stringsAsFactors = FALSE)
st
#>     status        reason voters
#> 1   ACTIVE               118174
#> 2 INACTIVE    NO CONTACT   3029
#> 3 INACTIVE          NCOA   2654
#> 4 INACTIVE   CROSS STATE   2322
#> 5 INACTIVE RETURNED MAIL   1381

9,386 people on this list are marked INACTIVE, and the file records why. “NCOA” is the Postal Service’s National Change of Address database. “CROSS STATE” means another state reported registering them. “RETURNED MAIL” means a letter came back.

None of those people did anything. They are being removed from a list because a piece of mail failed, and the reasons are a record of an administrative process rather than of anybody’s intentions.

This is the column that decides whether “registered voters” means 127,560 or 118,174 — a difference of 7.4% — and every turnout rate you will ever read depends on which one somebody used, usually without saying.

Part 4 — The file has no memory

Here is the thing that makes voter files genuinely dangerous to use casually.

lost <- read.csv("data/lost.csv", stringsAsFactors = FALSE)
lost
#>       election voters_no_longer_in_file
#> 1 2020 general                    13803
#> 2 2022 general                     5677
#> 3 2024 general                     3798
barplot(lost$voters_no_longer_in_file, names.arg = lost$election,
        col = "#C41230", ylab = "voters who voted, and are not in the 2026 file")

We took the current file and matched it against the state’s record of who actually voted. 13,803 people voted in the 2020 general election and are not in the 2026 file at all. For 2022 it is 5,677; for 2024, 3,798.

They died, moved away, or were purged. A voter file is a snapshot of a live system, not an archive. It tells you about now, and the further back you reach with it, the more people have quietly vanished from your denominator — and they are not a random sample of anybody.

Part 5 — Turnout by race, and why it is not quite right

to <- read.csv("data/turnout.csv", stringsAsFactors = FALSE, check.names = FALSE)
to$rate_2024 <- round(100 * to$`2024 general` / to$in_file_2026, 1)
to[, c("race", "in_file_2026", "2024 general", "rate_2024")]
#>                     race in_file_2026 2024 general rate_2024
#> 1                  WHITE        65116        45030      69.2
#> 2                  BLACK        42603        23467      55.1
#> 3        HISPANIC/LATINO         4518         2154      47.7
#> 4 ASIAN/PACIFIC ISLANDER         3547         1959      55.2
#> 5        AMERICAN INDIAN         1849          706      38.2
#> 6         ALASKAN NATIVE            4            4     100.0
#> 7                  OTHER         7748         4369      56.4
#> 8                UNKNOWN         2175          645      29.7
k <- to$race %in% c("WHITE","BLACK","HISPANIC/LATINO","ASIAN/PACIFIC ISLANDER")
barplot(to$rate_2024[k], names.arg = c("White","Black","Hispanic","Asian/PI"),
        col = "#2c7fb8", ylim = c(0, 80), ylab = "2024 turnout (%)")

White 69.2%, Black 55.1%, Asian 55.2%, Hispanic 47.7% — a twenty-one-point gap between the top and the bottom.

Every one of those numbers is slightly wrong, and you now know why. The numerator is 2024 votes; the denominator is who is on the list in 2026. Anyone who voted in 2024 and has since moved away is in neither. Anyone who registered in 2025 is in the denominator and could not possibly have voted.

The right denominator is the file as it stood in November 2024, which nobody kept. This is the ordinary situation. Published turnout-by-race figures from voter files almost always have this problem, and almost none of them say so.

Compare it against Session 8, where the Census Bureau will ask people whether they voted and get a different answer again.

Part 6 — Your turn

Change one thing, re-run, and write about what changed. Pick one.

Option A — Use active voters only. Recompute Part 5’s rates against 118,174 active registrants instead of all 127,560 (status.csv has the counts). Which group’s turnout rate moves most, and what does that tell you about who ends up marked inactive?

Option B — The midterm drop. Compare 2022 to 2024 by race. Turnout falls for everybody, but not equally. Which group falls furthest, and what would you need to know before calling that a fact about those voters rather than about the file?

Option C — The categories. 7,748 people are “OTHER” and 2,175 are “UNKNOWN” — nearly 10,000 who fit no box, more than the Hispanic and Asian counts combined. Their recorded turnout is the lowest of any group. Give two explanations, one about voters and one about the form, and say how you would tell them apart.

Option D — Your own question. 53 columns, 127,560 people, three elections.

What this lab cannot tell you

  • How anybody voted. The ballot is secret. This file knows that you voted and which primary ballot you asked for; it will never know who you chose. Session 14 is entirely about what people do with that gap.
  • One county in one state. Most states record no race, and many record party registration that Georgia does not. The shape of the file generalises; the columns do not.
  • Race here is self-reported on a form, one box, at the moment of registration, possibly decades ago — with all the flattening Session 2 showed in the census.
  • We used the public file. Commercial vendors — L2, Catalist, TargetSmart — sell versions with hundreds of appended columns: modeled party, modeled turnout, consumer data, estimated income. Campaigns use those. The modeling in Session 7 is the cheapest of them.

Where the data came from

Houston County, Georgia registration extract 71754, and the state’s voter-history files for the 2020, 2022 and 2024 general elections. Both are public records in Georgia. Working data for the 2026 Houston County redistricting matter.

Nothing individual-level is in this folder. 127,560 records were collapsed to a schema listing and four small tables before anything was saved — the column names and the margins are all this lab needs. The build is in data/build-data.R.
