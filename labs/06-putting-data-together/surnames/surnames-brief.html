The United States Census Bureau publishes a table about surnames. For any surname held by at least a hundred Americans, it gives the proportion of the people carrying it who are white, Black, Asian, American Indian, Hispanic, or of two or more races. It is free. It requires no login. It has 156,622 rows, and the first of them looks like this.

Surname Rank People holding it White Black Asian/PI Am. Indian Two or more Hispanic
SMITH 1 2,369,644 67.9949 22.618 0.681073 0.833205 4.37243 3.5004
NA NA NA NA NA NA NA NA NA

68.0, NA% of the 2,369,644, NA Americans named Smith are white and 22.6, NA% are Black. The six shares are mutually exclusive and sum to 100, and between them the rows of this file account for 298,898,231 people.

Now multiply that table by a second public table: the racial make-up of the county somebody lives in. Out comes a probability that a named person belongs to a racial category. The procedure is called Bayesian Improved Surname Geocoding. It was developed for health research by Elliott and colleagues (2009), and brought into political science by Imai and Khanna (2016). It is now routinely accepted as evidence in federal voting-rights and lending-discrimination cases. Courts use it to decide how many Black voters a district contains.

Nothing in that description is a metaphor or a euphemism. Somebody’s race is being inferred, without their knowledge, from their name and their address, by multiplying two spreadsheets published by their own government.

Nobody is guessing for fun

Voter files do not record race. In most states nobody is asked, and where they are asked, many decline.

But the Voting Rights Act is about race. To argue in court that a districting plan weakens Black or Latino voting strength, somebody has to establish how many Black or Latino voters there are and how they voted. The records do not say.

The alternative to this method is therefore not a better measurement. The alternative is no measurement, and with it no ability to detect the discrimination the law prohibits. Without an estimate of who the voters are, no §2 vote-dilution claim can be brought. And the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau, which set out its own version of the method in 2014, cannot find the lending patterns it is charged with policing.

That is a trade, and it deserves to be stated as a trade rather than as a solution. The objection that this is racial profiling is a real question rather than a slogan. The serious answer is that the guess is about populations, and that it exists because the records which would answer honestly do not. Whether that answer holds depends on how well the method works, on whom, and on where its confidence actually comes from. That is a question about the table above, and it can be settled by looking at it.

Two tables, fourteen years apart, with a floor under one of them

Neither table was made for this. The surname file is the Census Bureau’s Frequently Occurring Surnames from the 2010 Census, and the Bureau publishes it under genealogy. Its intended reader is somebody tracing a family, not a court handing out a remedy.

The county file is a different product of the same agency, and a different kind of number altogether. It is the Population Estimates Program’s county release, 3,144 counties, vintage 2024. One is a count taken on a single day. The other is a model carried forward a year at a time from the last count. The method multiplies them together as though they were the same sort of object, and the multiplication is where every result in this chapter comes from.

Acquiring them cost nothing worth reporting. Both sit on the Bureau’s own servers as public archives. There is no key to obtain, no request to file, no page to scrape and nobody to ask. The whole job is two downloads and an unzip.

That is worth one sentence precisely because it is not the usual shape of the problem in this book. Elsewhere the hard part comes before the file exists: the letters, the parsing, the rebuilding of a table no agency keeps. Here the file is free, and the trouble is entirely on the far side of it. It is in what happens when two tables built for unrelated purposes get multiplied together by somebody neither of them had in mind.

They are the appropriate files because the method has exactly two ingredients and these are the published versions of both. There is no third public table that would do better. And the thing that would genuinely be better is not a table at all. The honest input is a record of what people say about themselves. No such record exists in a voter file, and the reason is not cost. It is that American states decided not to ask.

There are real improvements available inside the method, and they are declined here on purpose. The standard software adds first names, age, sex and party registration. It also pushes the geography down from the county to the census block, which is a far sharper starting guess than a county average. That version is better in degree. It is the same thing in kind. And the arithmetic is easier to argue with when it is on the page rather than inside a function call.

The difficulties start with the calendar and end somewhere worse. The surname table accounts for 298,898,231 people as they described themselves in 2010. The county table describes 340,110,988 people in 2024. Anyone born or arrived in between sits in the denominator of one file and is absent from the other. The denominator is the bottom number, the thing you divide by. And no column in either file tells you which mismatch is doing more work: the growth, or the floor on names.

The floor is the sharper limit. The Bureau publishes no surname held by fewer than 92 people. So Americans with the rarest names are not classified badly by this method. They are outside it entirely.

Among the names that do appear, the Bureau withholds any single cell small enough to identify somebody. What that withholding does to the arithmetic is the subject of a later section. It is not a rounding matter.

Underneath all of it sits the problem no version of this file can repair. A surname is not evidence about a person. The table reports what the people carrying a name told the census about themselves. That is a statement about a population. The method runs it backwards onto one individual, which is a direction the table was never built to travel.

Both files needed cleaning, and one of the decisions is the chapter. In the raw surname file, a withheld cell is written (S). Read carelessly that is a piece of text. Read conveniently it is a zero.

The build turns it into a missing value instead. That keeps the difference between nobody and too few to say, right up until the arithmetic collapses it again.

The county file arrives as separate male and female counts by age group, for several vintages. The build takes the July 2024 estimate for all ages and adds the sexes together. Then it collapses the Bureau’s categories down to the six the surname file uses, folding Asian together with Native Hawaiian and Pacific Islander because the surname file folds them.

Before writing anything, it checks that the six shares sum to one in every county. A reader rebuilding this would have to make the same category choices. They would find the surname file has already made most of them.

Two hundred thousand cells that say (S)

Everything in this section is about the 2010 tabulation. It is the one that suppressed, and it has to be understood before the next section shows what replaced it — the numbers BISG runs on today come from 2020, and that file suppresses nothing.

The 2010 file is the one source in this book that cannot be trusted to arrive. In August 2026 its address answered a request on the 12th, and again on the 14th, with a 247-byte page reading “Request Rejected”. Then on the 16th, with nothing visibly changed, it answered with the real thirteen-megabyte archive. Everything else in the same directory, and the whole 2020 release, downloaded without trouble throughout. Any single attempt at this address measures a moment, not the file.

What follows is therefore a reconstruction of the format, not a capture of the file. Every feature of it is taken from the code that reads the archive. Nothing is invented beyond what that proves:

Two things in that shape do all the damage. The header is upper case, and everything after it refers to these columns in lower case. So the build lowercases the names before doing anything else. It is a one-line repair, and without it every column lookup in this chapter fails at once.

And the suppressed cells are the string (S). Not a blank, not a zero, not NA: two characters and a pair of parentheses, sitting in a column of numbers. Read the file without saying so and every one of those columns arrives as text, every percentage becomes a character string, and nothing will average. Read it carelessly, forcing the column to numbers and letting the warning scroll past, and (S) becomes NA anyway. That is the right answer reached by the wrong route. The build names the marker explicitly, so the conversion is a decision rather than an accident.

Here is what those two rows look like once the file is clean:

(S) has become NA, and that is not a missing value in the ordinary sense. The Bureau withheld those cells on purpose, because publishing them could identify people. If only a handful of the 56,261, NA people named VANG are recorded as American Indian, the exact number would give them away, so the percentage is withheld. The absence is a privacy decision, and it is itself information. It tells you the count was small.

The scale of it is the thing to hold on to. Across 156,622 surnames and six columns, 0 cells are withheld. 0 surnames are missing at least one, which is most of the file. A method that dropped incomplete rows would throw away most American surnames and keep the common ones. Those are exactly the names that say least about anybody. The chapter therefore treats NA as a value rather than as a defect, and a later section is about what those quiet cells are hiding.

The 2020 Census was released through a disclosure-avoidance system that adds noise to every count before anybody sees it. Noise added to a small count can push it below zero. Rather than quietly trim those and say nothing, the Bureau ships the trimmed table and, beside it, the same table with the negatives left in. In 54,147 of the 156,621 surnames, at least one cell records a negative number of people. That is 34.6% of the file.

Read the third and fourth columns together. The published file says that no American Indian or Alaska Native person is named CHEUNG. The other file, from the same office and the same release, says minus two.

Neither is a measurement. Minus two people is not a mistake. It is what the protection costs, made visible. The count of AIAN people named CHEUNG is so small that publishing it truthfully could identify them. So the published number is the true number plus noise, and for a cell this small the noise is bigger than the thing being measured.

The last thing the file does is the elegant part, and it is checked here rather than taken on faith. The negative is never simply deleted. Trim it to zero and the row would gain two people who do not exist, so the neighbouring cells give them up. Across 156,620 surnames, meaning every row where both files can be compared, the six categories still add to exactly the same number of people.

The 2010 file and the 2020 file are two answers to one question, and neither answers it. 2010 declines to tell you how many AIAN people are named CHEUNG, and marks the refusal (S) — 315,341 times. 2020 tells you, and the answer is fiction. What no version of this table can give you is a small cell you can trust, which is exactly the cell a rare surname depends on.

That matters downstream. BISG is confident about a name in proportion to how lopsided its racial composition is, and the most lopsided names are the rarest ones — the ones whose cells are smallest and therefore noisiest. The method is most sure of itself exactly where the file is least able to support it.

Four or five to one is the reasonable expectation, and it is close to what most readers write down. It was not merely too low. It was off by more than an order of magnitude, and in a direction that matters: the group the Voting Rights Act was written to protect is the group whose surnames carry the least information.

This is not a quirk of the data and it is not a flaw in the Census Bureau’s processing. It is history sitting in a spreadsheet. Enslaved people were given, or took, the surnames of enslavers, and after emancipation those names stayed. Black and white Americans have shared a common stock of surnames for a hundred and sixty years, so a surname carries almost no signal about which of the two a person belongs to.

The second ingredient: every county, in the same six categories.

Bayes’ rule combines them. We want P(race∣surname,county)P(\text{race} \mid \text{surname}, \text{county}); we have P(race∣surname)P(\text{race} \mid \text{surname}) from the surname file and P(race∣county)P(\text{race} \mid \text{county}) from the county file:

P(r∣s,g)∝P(r∣s)P(r∣g)P(r)P(r \mid s, g) \;\propto\; \frac{P(r \mid s)\,P(r \mid g)}{P(r)}

Dividing by the national share P(r)P(r) prevents double-counting the fact that some groups are large everywhere. That is the entire model — one line of arithmetic.

Answer this before you run the arithmetic.

Smith is the commonest surname in the United States and it is 68.0, NA% white by name alone — not a coin flip, a clear answer. Now put a Smith in the Bronx. Does the model still call that person white, yes or no? If not, which group does it pick instead, and with what share?

SMITH barely moves. WILLIAMS moves +26.3 points. Williams gains +26.3 points and Nguyen gains -0.4. That is the same fact from both ends. The geography contributes almost everything where the name contributes almost nothing, and almost nothing where the name is already sharp.

That is Bayes’ rule behaving correctly — a sharp prior is hard to move, a flat one is easy. But follow the consequence. The names that need help are the common ones: Smith, Johnson and Williams are three of the five most frequent surnames in the country. Nguyen and Garcia are precise and describe a much smaller share of Americans.

So for most Americans, BISG is substantially a method for inferring race from address. Told only the name, you would learn almost nothing. Told only the county, you would learn most of what the model produces.

And that has an uncomfortable consequence, which is worth saying rather than implying. The method is accurate because American neighbourhoods are segregated. It works well in the Bronx precisely to the extent that the Bronx is not integrated. The estimate borrows its precision from the condition the Voting Rights Act exists to address.

Suppression peaks at 53.3% among names held by 500-1,000 people and collapses to 1.7% among names held by more than five thousand. For surnames held by fewer than two hundred people the Black share is withheld 42.1% of the time, and 83.5% of all names in the file are held by fewer than a thousand people.

Suppression is applied by a neutral rule — small cells are hidden. It does not land neutrally. Recent immigrants, small national-origin groups and Native American families are disproportionately the holders of rare surnames, so a rule that is neutral in form is unequal in effect. And because a suppressed cell enters the arithmetic as a zero, the method does not merely lose information about those people. It quietly asserts something false about them: that nobody holding the name belongs to the group whose share was too small to publish.

The argument this method is having

The method is validated and the validation is narrow. Elliott and colleagues (2009) introduced the combination of surname and location for health research. They reported agreement with self-reported race far higher than either ingredient alone. Imai and Khanna (2016) extended it to voter files, added first names and party registration, and released the software most people now use. The published performance is real.

The published performance is also reported as one overall figure. On a problem this lopsided, an overall figure mostly reports the lopsidedness rather than the method. That is the strongest available criticism. It is not that BISG is unreliable. It is that its reliability varies in a patterned way: by group, by how rare the name is, and by how segregated the place is. And that variation is almost never reported next to the estimate.

Refinements exist and do not change the kind of thing it is. Imai, Olivella and Rosenman (2022) have since built a fuller version. It handles the Census file’s own problems and adds further name lists, improving accuracy for exactly the groups the simple method handles worst. It is better in degree. It is still a probability about a person assembled from facts about populations.

And the alternative is not neutrality. Some states do collect race on registration; coverage is partial, non-response is high and non-random, and most states collect nothing. Abolishing the estimate does not produce a world in which race is irrelevant to voting. It produces a world in which racial vote dilution cannot be proven.

What a name can testify to

The strengths of this evidence are worth stating first. Both files are authoritative federal products covering the entire country. The surname file covers nearly every American name: all of them held by 100 or more people. And unusually for a racial classification, it reports proportions rather than a label. So the uncertainty is visible in the numbers themselves, instead of being hidden inside a category. A file that simply called Williams a “Black surname” would have concealed the very ambiguity this chapter is about.

What it cannot support begins with its own vintage. The surname file is from 2010 and the county file from 2024, and the method multiplies them together as though they described the same country. 32.4% of the cells in the surname file are withheld, and treating a withheld cell as zero is a modeling decision the data does not endorse and which falls hardest on rare names. County is in any case a coarse geography: the method as practiced uses census blocks, where the geographic signal is far stronger — and where, correspondingly, the inference leans even harder on segregation. Six categories flatten everyone who is both Hispanic and Black, or two races and Hispanic. And a name absent from the file cannot be scored at all, which is not a defect distributed evenly across groups.

The deepest limitation is not any of those. Nothing here has been checked against a known answer. Every number in this chapter describes what the method does, not how often it is right, and the two are different questions. What can be said about correctness comes from the structure rather than from a test. Of surnames held by more than a thousand people, 9,394 are more than 90% white and 178 are more than 90% Black. So for the group §2 exists to protect, the surname says almost nothing and the county does the work.

Nor can any of this be said about an individual. The output is a statement about a population: of all the Smiths in the Bronx, roughly 0.0% are Black. Every use of the method takes a number that is true on average and attaches it to somebody in particular.

Three questions are left genuinely open.

The first is what a court should be told. Not that the method is unreliable, which it is not. Rather, that its precision varies with how rare the name is and how segregated the place is. Any estimate offered as evidence ought to carry that variation group by group, rather than one headline accuracy figure. The second is whether accuracy borrowed from segregation is acceptable evidence in a case about segregation. That question is not technical and has no technical answer.

The third is whether states should simply collect race on registration. That would remove the need to guess. It would also create a government list of citizens by race. Both of those consequences are real, and choosing between them is not a statistical decision.

What remains is the shape of the thing. A federal table lists what a name implies about the person carrying it. For most of the country that implication is weak. It is weakest precisely for the descendants of the people the Voting Rights Act was written to protect, because their ancestors were given the names of the people who owned them. The method compensates by asking where they live, and it succeeds to the exact degree that the answer to that question is still predictable. The estimate is accurate because the country is segregated, and it is used to prove that the country is segregated.

WHY NOT. The chapter is built on U.S. Census Bureau, "Frequently
Occurring Surnames from the 2010 Census" — every surname held by 100 or
more people, with the share of its bearers in each of six race and
ethnicity categories. In August 2026 its address,
https://www2.census.gov/topics/genealogy/2010surnames/names.zip
answered as if everything were fine, but what came back was a 247-byte
page reading "Request Rejected" instead of the zip — while everything
else in the same directory, and the whole 2020 release, arrived
normally. A server that refuses one file and serves its neighbors may
change its mind without notice, in either direction. So try the address
first: if a real zip arrives (about 13 MB, holding the table as a CSV
and a spreadsheet), you hold the genuine article, and the numbers below
will tell you so. The mind-change has in fact happened once already: on
16 August 2026 the same address served the real zip, and that capture —
the Bureau's own artifact, not a local re-assembly — ships with this
chapter's data as names.zip, beside the CSV extracted from it. If the
address refuses you, nothing is lost.
