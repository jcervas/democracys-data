Surname-Based Race Inference

A federal table of names, a county, and one line of arithmetic that decides who counts as a Black voter

The United States Census Bureau publishes a table about surnames. For any surname held by at least a hundred Americans, it gives the share of the people carrying it who are white, Black, Asian, American Indian, Hispanic, or of two or more races. It is free, it requires no login, and between them its rows account for 298,898,231 people.

Multiply that table by a second public table, the racial make-up of the county somebody lives in, and out comes a probability that a named person belongs to a racial category. The procedure is called Bayesian Improved Surname Geocoding, BISG for short: surname because it starts from the name table, geocoding because the address is turned into a place on the census map, and Bayesian because the two are combined by Bayes’ rule, the arithmetic for revising a probability when new evidence arrives. Elliott and colleagues (2009) developed it for health research,1 Imai and Khanna (2016) brought it into political science,2 and it is now routine evidence in federal voting-rights and lending-discrimination cases.3 Courts use it to decide how many Black voters a district contains.

Nothing in that description is a euphemism. Somebody’s race is being inferred, without their knowledge, from their name and their address, by multiplying two spreadsheets published by their own government. So ask the plain question underneath. Can a name tell you someone’s race?

The test

Two federal tables answer it, because the method has exactly two ingredients and these are the published versions of both. The first is the Census Bureau’s Frequently Occurring Surnames tabulation: the 2020 edition, which everything below runs on, downloadable as a spreadsheet at https://www2.census.gov/topics/genealogy/2020surnames/, and the 2010 edition it replaced (https://www2.census.gov/topics/genealogy/2010surnames/names.zip), kept beside it because the two handle small numbers differently. The second is the Bureau’s population estimate for every county as of July 2024 — the 2020 count carried forward by births, deaths and moves — downloadable at https://www2.census.gov/programs-surveys/popest/datasets/2020-2024/counties/asrh/: 3,144 counties in the same six categories.

There is no third public table that would do better, and what would genuinely be better is not a table at all. The honest input is a record of what people say about themselves. No such record exists in a voter file, and the reason is not cost: American states decided not to ask. The BISG check brief works in one of the few counties where a state did.

The two tables are joined through a person, which is where the assumptions stack up. Join Keys and Crosswalks is the reading on what a join key is and how one fails. It also covers the repair when two files carry different keys: a crosswalk, a table that translates one set of identifiers into another.

The data

Neither table was made for this. The surname file sits under genealogy on the Bureau’s servers, and its intended reader is somebody tracing a family. The county file is a different kind of number altogether: one is a count taken on a single day, the other a model carried forward a year at a time. The method multiplies them as though they were the same sort of object.

One row of the surname file looks like this.

Surname Rank People holding it White Black Asian/PI Am. Indian Two or more Hispanic
SMITH 1 2,369,644 67.9949 22.618 0.681073 0.833205 4.37243 3.5004

The columns are name, rank, count, and the six shares pctwhite, pctblack, pctapi, pctaian, pct2prace and pcthispanic, which are mutually exclusive and sum to 100. The county file carries fips, county, state, pop and the same six groups.

The join runs on two keys and neither is a person. The surname, upper-cased, matches somebody to a row of the name table; the five-digit county code matches them to a row of the county table. Nothing joins the two tables to each other. A person is what makes them meet, and the assumption that both lists describe the same population at the same moment is a third assumption on top of each file’s own.

Both tables needed cleaning, and one decision is the whole brief. In the 2010 file a cell too small to publish safely is written (S). Read carelessly that is a zero; the table used here keeps it as a missing value instead, preserving the difference between nobody and too few to say. 315,341 of the 973,524 cells in the six percentage columns are withheld, 32.4% of them, and dropping incomplete rows would throw away most American surnames.

The 2020 edition answers the same problem in the opposite way. It suppresses nothing, and instead publishes every count with noise added to protect the people in it. Noise added to a small count can push it below zero, so the Bureau ships the trimmed table and, beside it, the same table with the negatives left in. 54,147 of the 156,621 surnames carry at least one:

Last name Category Published With negatives People with that name
CHEUNG aian 0 -2 19,577
FENG aian 0 -3 16,357
FU aian 0 -1 12,092
XIAO aian 0 -1 10,322
GUAN aian 0 -2 10,141
TA aian 0 -2 9,217

Read the third and fourth columns together. The published file says no American Indian or Alaska Native person is named CHEUNG; the other, same office and same release, says minus two. Minus two people is not a mistake. It is what the protection costs, made visible. The method is most confident about a name in proportion to how lopsided that name is, so it is most sure of itself exactly where the file is least able to support it.

The county figures are the Bureau’s estimate for 1 July 2024, all ages and both sexes together, with the Bureau’s categories collapsed to the surname file’s six.

Before you read on, write a ratio down.

Of the 25,800 surnames held by more than a thousand people, some number are more than 90% white and some number are more than 90% Black. For every surname that is more than 90% Black, how many do you think are more than 90% white? Four to one? Ten to one? Fifty to one?

One ratio, on paper, before your eye reaches the figures below.

What it says about democracy

Start with the thousand most common surnames, on the two shares that between them account for nearly all of their bearers.

Surnames held by more than a thousand people. The axis is logarithmic, so each gridline is ten times the one before it and equal distances are equal MULTIPLES, not equal counts: white to Black is 53 to 1. two or more is a true zero and cannot be placed on a logarithmic axis at all, so it is marked with an open circle at the left edge.

Figure 2. How many surnames are more than 90% one group. The axis is logarithmic, so equal distances are equal multiples rather than equal counts, which is why these are dots to be read by position and not bars to be read by length.

9,394 surnames are more than 90% white. 178 are more than 90% Black. That is a ratio of about 53 to 1, for a group that is roughly 13% of the country rather than one-hundredth of it. Compare it with the ratio you wrote down. Four or five to one is the reasonable expectation, and it is wrong by more than an order of magnitude. The group the Voting Rights Act was written to protect is the group whose surnames carry the least information.

That is not a quirk of the data and not a flaw in the Bureau’s processing. It is history sitting in a spreadsheet. Enslaved people were given, or took, the surnames of enslavers, and after emancipation those names stayed. Black and white Americans have shared a common stock of surnames for a hundred and sixty years. The exceptions show the mechanism from the other side. Washington is the most identifiably Black common surname in America, 83.6% of the 172,075 people who carry it, and historians’ best explanation is that freed people chose the names of presidents associated with liberty.4

So what carries the inference for everybody else? The address. Bayes’ rule combines the two tables in one line. Start from what the name alone says, the prior: the probability before the address is considered. Multiply it by how much more, or less, common each group is in the person’s county than in the country. Rescale the six results so they add to 100 and you have the posterior: the probability after the evidence is in. In symbols, the probability that a person of surname ss in county gg belongs to group rr is

P(r∣s,g)∝P(r∣s)P(r∣g)P(r)P(r \mid s, g) \;\propto\; \frac{P(r \mid s)\,P(r \mid g)}{P(r)}

Dividing by the national share P(r)P(r) stops the arithmetic double-counting the fact that some groups are large everywhere. That national share is the base rate, and it does more work than anything else here. Here is the whole calculation, by hand, for the commonest name in the country placed in the Bronx:

Group SMITH, by name (%) The Bronx (%) The country (%) Bronx ÷ country Name × ratio Posterior (%)
white 68.0 8.9 57.5 0.15 10.5 13.8
Black 22.6 29.6 12.6 2.35 53.1 69.8
Asian/PI 0.7 4.7 6.7 0.70 0.5 0.6
Am. Indian 0.8 0.3 0.7 0.42 0.4 0.5
two or more 4.4 1.1 2.5 0.45 1.9 2.6
Hispanic 3.5 55.4 20.0 2.77 9.7 12.7

Read the Black row. Smith is 22.6% Black by name. The Bronx is 29.6% Black against 12.6% for the country, 2.35 times the national share, so the name’s 22.6 becomes 53.1. Do the same in the other five rows, add the six products, and Black’s share of the total is 69.8%. White runs the other way: 68.0% by name, times 0.15, comes out at 13.8%. Nothing about the name changed. The same arithmetic, for two names in three counties:

Surname and place White Black Asian/PI Am. Indian Two or more Hispanic
SMITH in Allegheny County, PA 75.5 19.7 0.4 0.1 3.8 0.5
SMITH in the Bronx, NY 13.8 69.8 0.6 0.5 2.6 12.7
LEE in Allegheny County, PA 45.2 16.3 33.5 0.2 4.4 0.4
LEE in Honolulu County, HI 2.6 0.8 87.8 0.1 8.4 0.3

Smith is 68.0% white nationally. A Smith in the Bronx is 69.8% Black. The name did not change; the arithmetic stopped ignoring where the person lives. Lee runs the other way, resolving to 45.2% white in Allegheny County and 87.8% Asian in Honolulu. Three letters, opposite conclusions.

The tan end is the name alone; the teal end is the name plus one county. Hover a rod for both figures and the change between them.

Figure 3. How much six surnames move when one county is added. Each rod is one surname: the hollow end is the strongest claim the name alone will support, the solid end is the strongest claim it supports once the person is placed in the Bronx.

NGUYEN barely moves. WILLIAMS moves +38.6 points. That is Bayes’ rule behaving correctly, because a sharp starting guess is hard to move and a flat one is easy. But follow the consequence. The names that need help are the common ones: Smith, Johnson and Williams are three of the five most frequent surnames in America. Nguyen and Garcia are precise, and describe a much smaller share of it.

So for most Americans this is substantially a method for inferring race from an address. Told only the name, you would learn almost nothing; told only the county, you would learn most of what the model produces. The consequence is worth saying rather than implying. The method is accurate because American neighbourhoods are segregated. It works well in the Bronx precisely to the extent that the Bronx is not integrated, so the estimate borrows its precision from the condition the Voting Rights Act exists to address.

What this brief cannot tell you

Nothing here has been checked against a known answer. Every number above describes what the method does, not how often it is right, and those are different questions. The BISG check brief is the test; this one is the anatomy.

Nor can any of it be said about an individual. The output is a statement about a population: of all the Smiths in the Bronx, roughly 69.8% are Black. Every use of the method takes a number true on average and attaches it to somebody in particular.

The two tables describe different years. The surname list is a count from a census; the county file is a model carried forward to 2024. Anyone born or arrived in between sits in one file and not the other, and no column in either says how much that costs.

And a name absent from the file cannot be scored at all. The Bureau publishes no surname held by fewer than 92 people, so Americans with the rarest names are not classified badly by this method. They are outside it. County is also coarse: the standard software works down to the census block, the smallest area the Bureau reports and often a single city block, where the inference leans even harder on segregation.

What you should have learned

  • The whole method is one line of arithmetic: what a name implies, times what a county implies, divided by the national share of each group.
  • The name carries almost nothing for most Americans. 0 of the thousand commonest surnames are more than 90% Black, and for 23.6% of the people this file covers no group reaches 60%.
  • Of the 25,800 surnames held by more than a thousand people, 9,394 are more than 90% white and 178 more than 90% Black, a ratio of about 53 to 1 for a group that is 13% of the country.
  • Geography supplies what the name cannot. Smith is 68.0% white nationally and 69.8% Black in the Bronx, and the name that gains most from one county gains +38.6 points.
  • Neither edition of the file can give you a small cell you can trust: 2010 withholds 315,341 cells outright, and 2020 publishes noised counts that go negative in 54,147 of 156,621 surnames.

Extensions

None of these is answered above, and every one can be answered by sorting or grouping the tables the Sources section hands over.

  • census_surnames.csv and census_surnames_2010.csv hold the same names fourteen years apart. Join them on name and find the surnames whose pctblack moved most. Is that people changing, or the table changing?
  • surnames_2020_negatives.csv shows cells published as zero that are negative in the research copy. Pick a row and explain what a negative count of people is.
  • county_race.csv gives each county’s racial shares. Take a surname from census_surnames.csv, apply the arithmetic above to one county, then find a county where the answer is very different.
  • census_surnames.csv covers only names held by enough people to publish. Sort by count and work out roughly how many Americans have a name too rare to appear.

Two stretch ideas point past the spreadsheet. The wru package, a free add-on for the R statistical language written by Imai’s group (https://github.com/kosukeimai/wru), implements the full version, with first names, age, sex and geography down to the census block. Run it on one name, and see how much of the extra sharpness comes from the finer geography. And the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau published its own version of this method in 2014 for lending cases; read what it says about error rates against what the tables above imply.

ethnicity categories. In August 2026 its address,
https://www2.census.gov/topics/genealogy/2010surnames/names.zip
answered as if everything were fine, but what came back was a 247-byte

else in the same directory, and the whole 2020 release, arrived
normally. A server that refuses one file and serves its neighbors may
change its mind without notice, in either direction. So try the address
first: if a real zip arrives (about 13 MB, holding the table as a CSV
and a spreadsheet), you hold the genuine article, and the numbers below
will tell you so. The mind-change has in fact happened once already: on
16 August 2026 the same address served the real zip, and that capture —
the Bureau's own artifact, not a local re-assembly — ships with this
chapter's data as names.zip, beside the CSV extracted from it. If the
address refuses you, nothing is lost.
