Say “the Republicans are in trouble” and no result can contradict you. Say “the Democrats have a 90% chance of the House and will most likely win 244 seats” and you have written down something November can refute. A forecast is the only thing in political commentary that can be wrong, which is why a citizen should want one. So the question is not who wins. It is what such a number is made of.

This brief builds a model of the 2026 House from files this book already holds, then takes it apart. It is a model with stated assumptions, not a prophecy. Everything in it is computed except one number, and that guess moves the answer more than the data does.

The test

Four sources are combined. The polls come from six private outfits that each average the generic ballot, a survey question asking which party’s candidate you would support in your district, without naming anybody. Political scientists have shown that this one question, asked months out, predicts the national House vote well enough to forecast seats from.1 No agency publishes such an average and none of the six publishes a readable file. They are read instead from the table that puts them side by side on the Wikipedia article “2026 United States House of Representatives elections” (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2026_United_States_House_of_Representatives_elections).

The returns — the vote counts in every district — come from the Clerk of the House’s Statistics of the Congressional Election for 2004–2024, with the presidential vote inside each district beside them. The base rate, how the president’s party has fared at past midterms with nothing else known, comes from the House Historian’s record of party divisions back to 1858. The Senate roster, which senator holds which seat and when it is next up, comes from the congress-legislators file kept by volunteers on GitHub, and a published professional forecast is quoted for comparison.

Those sources answer the question because a forecast is a join between a national number and a local one, and both halves exist here in files with signatures on them. The key is the district, a state plus a district number, carried by the returns and the presidential figures alike. Every join takes on the assumptions of both files plus one more about how they line up, which is the subject of Join Keys and Crosswalks. That chapter also covers the crosswalk, a table that translates one set of areas into another.

baseline.csv holds one row per district. It carries district_id, the 2024 Democratic share of the two-party House vote in dv, the presidential share in dpres, the starting share the model uses in base, and the flags imputed, no_totals and uncontested, which mark a starting share that was estimated rather than observed, a district the Clerk printed no vote total for, and a race with only one major-party candidate. 38 districts had only one major-party candidate in 2024, so they have no Democratic share of a two-party vote. They cannot be dropped, because a forecast ignoring 9% of the House is not a forecast of the House. Each gets a starting share predicted from the presidential vote inside it, which now tracks the House vote to within 2.5 points. Two further districts arrive with no vote total at all, because the Clerk prints a footnote marker where an unopposed candidate’s count would go. Both are assigned to the party that carried the district for president. That is a rule rather than a lookup.

67 seats start within five points of the line and the other 368 decide nothing. The model itself is three lines. First, the swing: subtract 2024’s 48.65% from the national two-party vote you assume, which the polls make +5.08 points. Second, add that swing to every district’s starting share — a uniform swing, the same shift everywhere — plus two random errors: one drawn once for the country, one drawn separately for each seat. Third, count every district above 50. Doing that once is one imagined election; doing it 20,000 times, with fresh errors each time, is the simulation, and the share of those runs in which the Democrats reach 218 is the probability the forecast reports.

Follow one district through. PA-07 finished 2024 at 49.5% Democratic of the two-party vote, so that is where it starts. Add the swing and it stands at 54.6% before any error is drawn. A national error of two points against the Democrats would pull it back to 52.6%, a district error is then drawn on top, and the seat is counted Democratic in any run where the total ends above 50. This district matters because it is the 218th when all 435 are sorted by starting share: the seat that delivers a majority.

The national error is one number applied to every district, standing for the polls being off or the last three months going unexpectedly. The district error is drawn fresh for each seat, standing for a retirement, a scandal, a local issue. The rest of this brief is what follows from that difference.

Before you read on, write two numbers down. The polls imply a national Democratic share of 53.73%. How wide is the 80% interval around the seat count that implies — how many seats separate the 10th percentile from the 90th? And what probability would you put on the Democrats winning the House? Commit to both before you look.

What it says about democracy

Before any error is added the model is a plain function: give it a national vote share, get back a seat count. That function is the map, and political scientists call it the seats–votes curve.2

Seats the model gives the Democrats at each national vote share, with no error term switched on. The step at each district is one seat changing hands. Hover anywhere on the plot.

Figure 1. Seats against votes on the 2024 map. A step curve fits because seats are won one at a time. Each step is a single district crossing 50%, so the line shows where the map is dense with close seats and where it is empty. The vertical axis is Democratic seats, the horizontal axis the national two-party Democratic share of the House vote, and the dashed line at 218 is a majority.

The Democrats reach 218 seats at 49.2% of the national vote, a margin of R+1.6 — a margin being the gap between the two parties’ shares, here with the Republicans ahead. They can lose that vote by 1.6 points and still hold the chamber, and the seat delivering it is PA-07. The curve runs at about 4.52 seats per point, which is the exchange rate between a poll and a chamber: two points of polling error is roughly 9 seats.

The distribution of Democratic seats over 20,000 simulated elections. Move any dial. Everything below it recomputes.

Figure 2. 20,000 simulated elections. Each bar is a seat count and its height is how often that count came up, with bars at or above 218 a Democratic House. At the polling average the model gives the Democrats a median of 244 seats, an 80% interval of 218 to 275, and a 90% chance of a majority.

Move the first slider and you ask a different question of the same model. Move the other two and you change what the model believes about its own reliability, without touching any data.

Error terms switched on 10th percentile 90th percentile Width, seats Chance of a Democratic House
national error only 222 266 44 94%
district error only 239 250 11 100%
both 218 275 57 90%

Turn off the district error and the interval barely narrows, from 57 seats to 44. Turn off the national error instead and it collapses to 11. Nearly all the uncertainty in a 435-seat forecast comes from one number drawn once. The reason is arithmetic, not politics. The district error is drawn independently 435 times and independent errors cancel; the national error cannot, because there is only one of it. When the polls are two points off, they are off everywhere at once.

That is why professional forecasts rarely report 99%, and why adding district-level detail does less than it looks like it should. Better candidate data shrinks the small term; nothing shrinks the large one except better polls.

Does it work?

The model can be run on elections that already happened, which is the only way to find out. The test is generous by design: on two consecutive elections with unchanged district lines, the model is handed the second’s actual national vote and asked only for the seat count.

The model missed by 9 seats on average, and assuming nothing changed missed by 19.9. So the machinery earns its keep, by less than 435 moving parts might suggest. Two failures there are not noise. It understates waves: the correlation between the national swing and its error is -0.66, because when the country moves hard, competitive districts move harder than safe ones. And its errors are correlated within a year, arriving as whole elections wrong together. The spread of those 8 errors, divided by 4.52 seats per point, is 2.21 points of vote, which is added to the polling-error guess.

Every model that states a probability lands between 85% and 90%. One is a single sentence about midterms — the president’s party usually loses House seats, a regularity measured since the 1970s;3 another is a professional forecast built on polls, money, candidate files and race ratings, the Lean, Likely and Toss-up labels the next brief takes apart. What the data buys is a narrower answer, not a different one: the base rate’s interval is 76 seats wide and the professional model’s is 42. This model sits 14 seats more Democratic and calls a wider interval. The optimism is mostly incumbency, the edge a sitting member has over a challenger, which the professional model measures and this one cannot see.

Both chambers, one election

The Senate is on the same ballot, and running both in one simulation answers a question neither answers alone.

Outcome One national error, shared A separate error for each chamber
Democratic House 90% 90%
Democratic Senate 59% 59%
Both chambers 59% 53%
Neither chamber 9% 4%
One of the two 32% 43%

Both chambers comes out at 59% when the two share a national error and 53% when they do not. Treating them as independent means multiplying the two probabilities, which gives 53%, so the right answer is 5 points higher. A polling miss is not a House event. If the polls are two points too Republican, they are two points too Republican in Georgia and Maine as well as in Pennsylvania’s 7th. The Senate is also the harder chamber, arriving at 54.4% of the national vote against 49.2% for the House.

The redraws removed 14 competitive seats. The partisan effect roughly cancels; the effect on this model does not, because all its uncertainty lives in exactly those seats.

Which of its inputs is a judgment. The national error is 3.0 points of two-party share, of which 2.21 was measured from the backtest and 2.00 was assumed.

Assumed polling error Median Democratic seats 80% interval Width Chance of a Democratic House
±0.0 points of margin 244 224 to 267 43 seats 96%
±1.0 points of margin 244 224 to 267 43 seats 95%
±2.0 points of margin 244 222 to 269 47 seats 94%

±4.0 points of margin 245 218 to 275 57 seats 90%

Turning that dial from zero to the default widens the interval by 14 seats and moves the probability by 5 points. The guess matters far more to the range than to the headline, and only the headline is repeated.

Who is running. Every seat’s starting share carries the incumbent who won it, and incumbents retire. Over fifty members have said they will not stand again,5 and each of those seats reverts toward its underlying partisanship unseen.

Whether the forecast was any good. In November one thing will happen, and no single outcome shows that 90% was right or wrong. The Senate half is weaker still: its baseline carries no incumbency, and 33 seats cancel their independent errors far less than 435 do.

What you should have learned

  • A forecast claims a distribution, not an outcome: 244 seats in the middle, 218 to 275 at the 80% interval, 90% for a majority.
  • The map sets the terms. Democrats reach 218 seats at 49.2% of the national vote, so they can lose it by 1.6 points and hold the chamber, and only 67 of 435 districts are close enough to matter.
  • Almost all the uncertainty comes from one number drawn once. Switching off the district error narrows the interval from 57 seats to 44; switching off the national error collapses it to 11.
  • More data buys a narrower answer, not a different one. Every model here stating a probability lands between 85% and 90%, and one of them is a single sentence.
  • The chambers are not independent. Both comes out at 59% rather than the 53% multiplication gives, because a polling miss happens to a country.

Extensions

None of these is answered above, and every one can be answered from the tables Sources hands over.

  • generic_ballot.csv holds six aggregators’ numbers in margin and two_party_dem. Which would you feed the model, and what does their disagreement cost at 4.52 seats per point?
  • calibration.csv puts err beside nochange_err for eight past elections. Is the model beating the assumption that nothing changes, and in which years does it fail?
  • sensitivity.csv varies assumed_poll_error_margin against width and p_house. Find where the answer stops being confident.
  • baseline.csv flags districts as imputed, uncontested and no_totals. Count them, and say what share of a national forecast is a guess.
  • Stretch. redistricting.csv names the states with new maps. Pick one, find its new lines, and work out how many of curve.csv’s steps near 50% the redraw removes.
