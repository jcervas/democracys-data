“Forty-two percent of Americans say…”

Somebody asked some people, and the answer became a fact about a country of hundreds of millions. Everything worth knowing is in the machinery between those two things.

The Cooperative Election Study ran that machinery over 60,000 people in 2024, which makes it one of the largest political surveys in the world. It also publishes what almost no survey publishes: not only the answers, but the adjustments applied to them afterwards. Beside it, for scale, sit 19 people who answered the same questions, in the same words, with the same answer options, in a single room on the first day of a term.

A caveat about the class column, before any of it is read. The class file currently committed to this folder is class_responses_EXAMPLE.csv. It is placeholder data. The responses in it are invented and are not yours. They exist so that the tables have the right shape while the real first-day survey is being processed. Everything drawn from the CES is real and can be checked; nothing in the class column is, until the real file replaces the placeholder. Where this document reports a class figure, treat it as an illustration of the arithmetic and not as a fact about this room.

That caveat costs less than it looks like it costs, because the room is not the object of study here. The comparison is — and the most important thing the comparison turns up happens entirely inside the CES, on data that is real and checkable.

The only continuous measurement of what a public wants

Survey estimates are the only continuous measurement of what a public wants. Elections happen every two years and answer one question; surveys run constantly and answer thousands. Legislators, courts and journalists all treat them as the voice of the public, and there is no rival instrument.

So it matters a great deal whether the number that reaches the reader is what the researchers collected, or something they built afterwards. It is the second, and the difference is not small.

Size is not design

The CES is not a random sample of Americans. It is an opt-in online panel, matched to a target sample drawn from a probability frame, and then weighted. Sixty thousand respondents sounds like rigor, but size is not design.

Defenders argue that a large, well-matched, transparently weighted panel now beats a probability sample with a 5% response rate, because the probability sample’s advantage is theoretical once almost nobody answers. Critics argue that weighting a self-selected group repairs only the imbalances somebody thought to measure. Weighting means counting some answers more than others, so that a sample which does not look like the country behaves as though it did. Both positions are held by serious people, and everything below is compatible with either.

Two very different reasons a file exists

Nobody is required to run the Cooperative Election Study. It exists because dozens of university teams each buy a module of it and share a common core. It is published afterwards on Harvard Dataverse with a DOI attached, under the same obligation that governs any academic dataset: none, beyond the authors’ willingness to be checked. That shapes what is in it.

The common content asks what political scientists want to know, in the wording political scientists have agreed on, and it would stop the year the money did. The release used here is the 2024 Common Content, Data Release 2: about 60,000 respondents and 694 variables in a file of 184 MB. Those three figures are typed rather than computed, because that file is not shipped here and is too large to put there. They come from its documentation.

What is kept here is derived from it, and everything else on this page is computed from that. It is 39 rows, one per answer category of 7 questions, each carrying a headcount and the same share computed twice.

The other file has no producer at all in that sense. It is a room of students who answered the same seven questions on the first day of class, recoded to the CES’s own numeric codes so that the two can be set side by side. It has no sampling frame, so it has no margin of error. A margin of error says how far off a sample’s answer could be from the whole population’s, and this is a complete count of a room rather than a sample of anything. That is not a weakness to apologize for. The room is not what is being studied here; the comparison is, and the interesting result turns out to live entirely on the CES side of it.

The CES is the right instrument for that question for a reason that has nothing to do with its size. What this chapter needs is a survey that publishes its weighted numbers, documents how the weights were built, and still lets you recover the unweighted numbers underneath them. The third of those is the rare one. Almost every poll reported anywhere is weighted, and almost none of them ships the raw margin beside the adjusted one. So the single most important step between the interviews and the headline is normally invisible. Here it is two columns of the same table.

The difficulty worth stating in advance is what weighting can and cannot do, because it is a limit on the method rather than a fault in the file. A weight pulls a sample toward known population totals: how many Americans are over sixty-five, how many finished college. Those totals come from the census and from federal surveys, which are themselves estimates. More to the point, a weight can only correct an imbalance on something that exists in both the panel and the target.

Whatever made someone join an opt-in online panel in the first place is not a column in any census table, so it cannot be weighted away directly. It can only be corrected as far as it happens to travel with the demographics that are measured. Keep that in mind when the size of the adjustments arrives.

The cleaning was small and one piece of it nearly went wrong in a way worth recording. The CES ships numeric codes, and the codes mean nothing without the official Guide. The Guide displays the race categories in a different order than their numeric codes run. It lists Middle Eastern sixth, while the category that actually carries code 6 is “two or more races.” A build script that read the labels off the printed order would have produced a table that looked perfectly reasonable, and mislabeled two groups. Codes are therefore mapped to labels by matching category counts rather than by position, because a count is the only part of that mapping that can be checked.

Beyond that, 7 variables were kept out of the several hundred on offer. Nothing was imputed, meaning no missing answer was filled in by guessing, and people who skipped a question stayed skipped.

Which is a small amount of work, and that is the point worth ending on. Almost none of the labor behind the CES column happened here. Ansolabehere, Schaffner and Pope’s teams drew the target sample, matched an opt-in panel onto it, wrote the question wordings, assigned the numeric codes and computed the weight. The file was pulled off Dataverse with every one of those decisions already made. Only the last few steps were done here: pick seven variables, count, tabulate.

So the weighted column is not this chapter’s arithmetic. It is somebody else’s model, shipped as a column of numbers that looks exactly like the column of counts beside it. That is what a researcher-assembled file costs. It arrives usable because a team spent years making it so, and their judgments arrive with it, unlabeled and inherited whole.

The race codes are the visible instance: a printed order that disagrees with the numeric one, caught only because counts can be checked. The invisible instances are the ones to hold in mind when the size of the weighting adjustment arrives.

Five decisions turned the digits into that table, and one of them is the reason this chapter exists.

Six hundred and ninety-four columns became seven. Nothing was cleaned out. Seven questions were chosen because they are the ones on which a room can be compared to a country. Every other question in the survey is still in the file and is simply not asked here: vote choice, policy, media use, the university modules.

Sixty thousand rows became eight. One row per answer category, holding a count. The individual respondents are gone, which is the trade this file makes: you can read the margins of any question and you can no longer cross one against another. A summary is a decision about which questions can still be asked later, and it is made before anyone knows what they will want.

A number became a word, and the mapping is where it nearly went wrong. In this file, code 6 means “two or more races”. The official Guide lists Middle Eastern sixth. A build script that read labels off the printed order would have attached “Middle Eastern” to code 6 and “two or more races” to code 8, and this is what that table would have said:

Code By the Guide’s printed order By matching counts Respondents
6 Middle Eastern Two or more races 1,947
8 Two or more races Middle Eastern 166

1,947 people called Middle Eastern and 166 called two or more races — a 12-fold error inside one category, in a table that would have looked entirely normal. Codes are mapped to labels by matching the published category counts instead, because the count is the only part of that mapping that can be checked against anything.

Every category is counted twice, and one of the two counts is not a count. % unweighted is respondents divided by the total: arithmetic on the digits. % weighted is the sum of a weight column over the same people. Nobody was asked twice and nobody was added. The two numbers sit in adjacent columns, formatted identically, and one of them is a model. Reading across the race rows, the gap between them is never more than 1.5 points; the rest of this chapter is about the question where it is 19.0 points.

A skipped question stayed skipped. Nothing was imputed. Two of the seven questions therefore have fewer respondents than the other five, and the file records that by having smaller counts rather than by saying so.

Two columns, one set of people

Look at the last two columns and ask what could possibly separate them. They are not two surveys. They are not two years. They are the same 60,000 people, counted twice.

A weight is a number attached to each respondent saying how many people like them the sample ought to have contained. If the panel caught half as many people without a high-school diploma as the country has, each one is counted roughly twice. The unweighted column is what the researchers collected. The weighted column is what gets published, quoted and cited.

Every CES figure anybody has ever seen has been through this.

The largest adjustment in the file is voter registration. 91.5% of CES respondents said they were registered to vote. The weights cut that to 72.5% — 19.0 points.

Nobody lied. That is the point. The adjustment is not correcting an answer; it is correcting a sample. People who agree to join an online political panel are far more likely to be registered than people who do not, and the weight is a formal confession of that fact.

The same mechanism runs through the rest of the table. Strong Democrats fall 5.7 points; people who follow the news “most of the time” fall 4.8. People with no high-school diploma, the group least likely to opt into a political panel, rise 3.9.

The sample the CES collected is D+13.5. The sample it publishes is D+1.9. The weights move the headline partisan balance of the largest political survey in America by 11.6 points.

That is not a scandal — the weighted figure is very likely the better estimate of the country. It is a demonstration that the benchmark you are about to compare a classroom against is itself a construction, built by people who had to decide which variables mattered.

A room is a census of itself

The class survey has no weights. Nobody built any, and nobody could: a weight requires a population the sample is a sample of, and there is no population of which this room is a sample. The room is a census of itself.

So the class column is the equivalent of the unweighted CES column — raw, and describing whoever was there. But there is a second and larger problem.

Two different failures

Put the two halves of this chapter side by side, because they are not the same problem and the reflex to conflate them is the thing worth unlearning.

Problem What goes wrong Where you saw it Does a bigger sample fix it Does weighting fix it
Bias the sample is systematically unlike the population CES: registration off by 19.0 points before weighting No Only for variables you measured
Imprecision the sample is too small to resolve anything this class: ±22.5 points on every estimate Yes, slowly No

Neither one fixes the other. A bigger sample makes you more precise about whatever your sample happens to be. Weighting corrects the imbalances somebody thought to look for, and nothing else.

The argument about opt-in panels

Ansolabehere and Rivers (2013) set out the case for the design the CES uses. Cooperatively organized, very large opt-in samples, matched to a probability frame and weighted, can support analyses that no affordable probability sample can — subgroups, small states, rare populations. They argue the matched-sample design recovers most of what random selection was supposed to deliver.

The AAPOR review of the 2016 election polls (Kennedy et al., 2018) is the strongest evidence for the other side, and it is specifically about weighting. State polls in 2016 systematically over-represented college graduates and mostly did not weight on education. That single unadjusted variable accounts for a large share of the error. The lesson is precisely the one Figure 1 draws: weighting works on the variables somebody thought of.

Groves (2006) explains why the response rate on its own tells you little. Non-response bias depends on the relationship between answering and the thing you are measuring, not on how many people answered. A panel with terrible response can be nearly unbiased about age and badly biased about political interest — which is exactly the shape of the CES adjustment.

And there is a defensible contrarian position on the small survey, worth stating because nothing above makes it. A room is the population of interest for some questions — how these particular people think, what they will argue about on Thursday. Measured that way there is no sampling error at all, because there is no sampling. The ±22.5 appears only once somebody decides the room is a sample of something larger, and that decision belongs to the analyst rather than to the data.

What a room and a panel can testify to

The evidence assembled here is unusually transparent on one side and unusually thin on the other, and the two halves fail in different directions.

The CES side is transparent because the file carries 60,000 respondents and seven questions whose wording is checked against the official guide. And, the rare part, it carries both the collected and the published percentages, so the adjustment is visible rather than assumed. Most surveys publish only the second column, and a reader of those surveys has no way to know how far the numbers travelled.

It is thin in five specific ways. The benchmark file is a summary rather than individual records, so there is no cross-tabulation and no way to ask whether a gap survives holding age constant. The CES is not a probability sample but an opt-in panel, matched and weighted, so nothing here can be read as a guarantee that the weighted column is right. The class side has 19 respondents, no weights, and no comparison classroom. Mode differs between the two: the CES is taken alone at home while the class survey was taken in a room where an instructor could see the screen — identical words, a different instrument. And item non-response is present and invisible throughout: 1,478 people skipped the news-attention question and no published figure mentions them.

To which one more must be added here: the class responses in use are placeholder data, invented to give the tables their shape. Every class figure in this document is illustrative only.

So the sentences this chapter will bear are about the CES rather than the room. The published CES figures differ a great deal from the responses collected: by 19.0 points on voter registration, and by 11.6 points on the collapsed partisan margin. That is documented openly, which is more than most surveys manage. Sharper: the adjustment is largest on exactly the variable that describes political engagement, which is precisely what one would predict if the panel’s problem is self-selection into political interest.

What cannot be said is anything confident about how a class of 19 differs from the country. That is not because the room is unusual. It is because 19 respondents cannot resolve a difference smaller than about 22.5 points, and almost every interesting difference is smaller than that. Nor can anybody say what the room would look like weighted, because there is no population to weight it to.

Three things stay open. Whether matched opt-in panels are now better than degraded probability samples: Ansolabehere and Rivers against the AAPOR reviews, unsettled and likely to stay that way. Which variables the CES weights are still missing: by design the answer is not in the file, and it never will be.

And whether a university class differs from the country in any way that is not simply structural. Education and age certainly differ, nothing else shows up at this sample size, and the design has no comparison group that would separate being at a particular university from being twenty.

The useful thing to carry away is not a number about either group. It is that the 19.0-point registration adjustment and the ±22.5-point margin are two entirely different kinds of wrongness. No amount of one repairs the other, and only one of them gets mentioned when a survey result reaches a newspaper.
