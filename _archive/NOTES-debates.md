# Tuesday debates — rebuilt on the book's four standards

*Superseded the earlier mapping in Aug 2026, once the Sides et al. text was
readable. The history below is kept because it records why the old scheme was
dropped.*

## What the debates are now

Every Tuesday debate names **two of the four standards from Chapter 1** — free
choice, political equality, deliberation, free speech — and a question that
forces a trade between them. The standards, and how a debate runs, are in
**The Four Standards: A Reference Sheet** at the end of the syllabus.

Why this beats the old scheme:

- **It is the book's own.** Chapter 1 sets the four out (Table 1.1 says what
  each demands) and most chapters close with an "Evaluating…" section applying
  them. Students hear the same four words for thirteen weeks instead of a new
  pair of scholarly camps each time.
- **The standards conflict**, which is what makes them debatable. Nearly every
  reform that serves one costs another. The old sheet's debates were empirical
  disputes ("is polarization elite- or mass-driven?"), which are settled by
  evidence rather than argued — the wrong shape for a 30-minute floor debate.
- **It generalises past the textbook.** Unit V (Census, VRA, BISG, RPV,
  redistricting) is not in Sides et al. at all, but all five weeks score cleanly
  against political equality. The framework carries where the book stops.

|---|---|---|
| 2 | Ch 1 | Free choice vs. political equality — the framework itself |
| 3 | Ch 2 | Political equality vs. free choice — districts, apportionment, federalism |

# Historical note — the superseded mapping


*Working note, not part of the syllabus. First pass Aug 2026; revised after
working through the gaps that pass identified.*

Every `**Debate:**` line in `readme.Rmd` now resolves to a named entry on the
reference sheet at the end of the syllabus. Verified mechanically: 21
references, 19 distinct debates, no dangling names.

## What changed in this pass

| Week | Was | Now | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| **2** (Ch 1) | *Media Effects: Minimal vs. Strong* (§7), read broadly | *Partisan Identity as Social Identity* (§1) | The old mapping stretched a media debate to cover campaign effects, and duplicated Week 9, where the same debate fits properly. Partisan identity puts the minimal-effects question directly — is partisanship a standing identity or a running judgment? — and was otherwise unused. |
| **4** (Ch 3) | *Economic vs. Cultural Polarization* (§1) | **Moderate vs. Extreme Candidates** (§8, new) | The old mapping reframed the chapter rather than asking its question. Ch. 3 is the party-centred → candidate-centred story; the new debate asks what that system rewards. |
| **6** (Ch 5) | *Rational Voter vs. Low-Information Voter* (§1) | *Median Voter Theorem vs. Party Polarization* (§6) | Persuasion vs. mobilization is Ch. 5's central strategic choice, and is this debate in working clothes. Also frees the duplication with Week 10, which is where Rational Voter belongs. |
| **10 Tue** | *Rational Voter* alone | *Rational Voter* + *Economic vs. Cultural Polarization* (§1) | Economic-vs-cultural, displaced from Week 4, lands on the reading that actually tests it — the multiracial-right piece. |
| **11 Thu** | *(none)* | *Centralization vs. Decentralization* (§3) | Tuesday is Democracy Day, so the debate moves to Thursday. It is Option B of the election-night lab, answerable from the returns rather than the armchair. |
| **12** (Census) | *Racial Threat vs. Contact* (§1) | **Racial Categories: Constructed vs. Discovered** (§5, new), with *Racial Threat vs. Contact* alongside | The real dispute in Scott is whether categories are built by the counting or found by it, and nothing on the sheet covered it. Racial Threat stays, attached to the Brennan Center reading, which assumes the change is real and asks what it produces. |
| **15 Thu** | *(none)* | **Electoral System Design: Plurality, RCV, and PR** (§8, new) | The wrap needed the "compared to what?" question. Districts have to be drawn only because we elect one person per district. |

Unchanged: Weeks 3, 5, 7, 9, 13, 14, 15 Tue. Those mappings were sound.

**Two debates now run on Thursday, not Tuesday.** Adding the two in-class tests
took Tue Oct 20 (Week 9) and Tue Nov 10 (Week 12). In both weeks the chapter
discussion and its debate move to that week's Thursday and run ahead of the lab,
which is on the same subject in both cases — Ch. 8 before the media lab, the
Census readings before the Census lab. So the debate survives; it just shares a
session. Budget about 25 minutes rather than the usual 30.

## Added to the reference sheet

**§8 Elections & Electoral Systems** — a new section. A campaigns-and-elections
course whose debate sheet had no elections section was the largest hole in it.

- **Moderate vs. Extreme Candidates** — Canes-Wrone, Brady & Cogan (2002);
  Ansolabehere, Snyder & Stewart (2001); Hall (2015)
- **Electoral System Design: Plurality, RCV, and PR** — Duverger (1954);
  Lijphart (1994); Cox (1997); Drutman (2020)

**§5** gained **Racial Categories: Constructed vs. Discovered** — Omi & Winant
(1986); Nobles (2000); Hochschild & Powell (2008); Prewitt (2013). Nobles is
already assigned in Week 13, so students meet one of these directly.

## Still unused

*Policy Diffusion* (§3) — one entry out of twenty. It has no natural home in a
federal-elections course; a state-policy week is where it would belong, and
there isn't one. Leave it on the sheet as a reference students may want for the
policy proposal, which is often about state-level reform.

## What is still open

These mappings rest on chapter titles and topics, not on the text itself.
Nothing structural should move once the Sides et al. chapters can be read — the
debates are matched to subject matter the table of contents already fixes. What
the text will sharpen is the *why this debate* sentence under each one, which
states the question in general terms rather than in the chapter's own terms.
Worth a pass before Week 2, and cheap: seven or eight sentences.
