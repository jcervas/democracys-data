Democracy’s Data:
a map of the book

Sixty-four chapters, six instruments, and the counts that can be derived by reading the folder rather than asserted.

Rebuilt on 12 August 2026 by listing F26/labs/ and opening every *-brief.Rmd in it. This map describes what the book contains today. Nothing that was cut, archived, or superseded appears in it — the counts below are the directory, not a running tally.

Between the books that teach inference and the books that teach tools sits the thing neither is about: the dataset. Who made it, why they bothered — which was almost never your question — what they were obliged to write down, and which of your questions the file destroyed before you arrived.

So the book teaches one skill: how to interrogate a dataset. Open an unfamiliar file and, before computing anything, say who produced it and under what obligation, what exactly one row is, what the denominator would have to be, and which of your questions the file has already made unanswerable.

How each figure was obtained, and where to distrust it. Chapters, folders, data/ and data/raw/ directories: by listing the directory, so those four are exact. Inline expressions, prediction prompts and Sources headings: by pattern over the source files, also reliable because the patterns are literal strings the chapters actually contain. A prediction prompt is a blockquote opening > **Before you… or > **Stop here… — before you read on, before you turn the page, before you look at the table, before you read the count. Counting the family rather than one phrasing moved the figure from 52 to 62, and the last two chapters without one (electoral-map, lobbying) have since been given one, so the convention is now universal.

The limits count used to be the soft one. It is now exact. Chapters do not share a heading for their closing caveats, so counting by string under-reported it for a while. The rule is structural, not lexical: the limits section is the section immediately before ## Sources, and on that rule every one of the 64 chapters has one. The headings fall into three families plus five one-offs — 35 are “What <X> can testify to” (the ledger, the ground, the ballots, `r n(NPREC)` precincts), 12 are “What <X> will bear”, 12 are the plain “What this chapter cannot tell you”, and the remainder are one-offs (What the crosswalk licenses, What the arithmetic will and will not carry, What remains genuinely open, What a perfect record is worth, What a mandatory filing is worth). That is 65 headings across 64 chapters, because bellwether carries both a plain limits section and a closing essay.

The introduction is the only brief with no limits section, which is correct rather than a gap: it has no findings to qualify. Its nearest equivalent is “What this book is not”, and the section before its ## Sources is “Where to start”.

One figure is deliberately absent. No word or page count is given, because an .Rmd whose numbers are generated at build time has no fixed length until it is knitted.

Axis I — the spine

By the instrument the chapter reads

The book now has six chapters whose subject is a source rather than a finding: what an instrument records, what it structurally cannot, and the decisions between the record and the number. Each is listed here with the chapters that read that instrument. Assignment is by the chapter’s primary source; several read two, and those appear twice. The six source chapters are marked in solid type.

Surveys

12 chapters

Asks a sample of people questions and records what they say, in the categories the question allowed.

Only instrument that can record an attitude — no administrative file anywhere contains whether a person trusts the government. Cannot describe a block at any sample size: a typical state in one ANES holds 26 respondents, ±19 points.

The census

14 chapters

Attempts to count everyone, for a constitutional purpose, and records what a person is in the categories the form allows.

Resolves Georgia into 232,717 blocks, 2,457 of them holding exactly one person — the capability nothing sampled has, and the reason disclosure avoidance became necessary. Every consistency check on the noised file passes.

Election returns

17 chapters

The certified statement of who won. Complete, official, and produced by thousands of separate offices.

Permanently silent about who voted how — the secret ballot is the limit, not a gap in record-keeping. Ecological inference graded against cast vote records returns 194% and 233%, both outside the range a percentage may occupy.

Voter files

5 chapters

An operational record kept so a poll worker can decide whether you may vote today. Every research use is a borrowing.

The only instrument that can check a survey: self-reported turnout is unfalsifiable from inside a survey. Has no past tense — 13,803 people recorded voting in 2020 are already gone from the file.

Roll calls

5 chapters

Recorded legislative votes — entered on the Journal only when one-fifth of members present demand it.

A record the recorded chose to make. 69% of 113,512 roll calls have no note of what the vote was about, and the gap is everything before 1989: complete about behaviour, empty about content.

Campaign finance

4 chapters

Disclosure filings, made by the regulated party about itself, published by the agency without verification.

The only source in the book written by its own subject. Sixteen unverified rows worth $44.9bn reverse the sign of the headline finding, from 94.5% supportive to 57.6% opposing.

  • finance-source
  • campaign-finance
  • independent-expenditures
  • lobbying

Ten chapters read none of the six, and that is worth knowing rather than smoothing away: eavs (an EAC survey of election offices), disenfranchisement (an advocacy organisation’s compilation), turnout-denominator (a table printed in a 2001 journal article, plus the researcher-maintained series that continues it), gotv (a synthesis of field experiments), jury-selection (court records and a simulation), media-attention (Wikipedia), models-markets (a prediction market), campaign-visits (a wire service tracker), data-sources (a join between two of the six), and primary-defeats (returns plus roll calls). The instrument taxonomy covers most of American political data and not all of it.

What a reader of this map should know is thin

The public-opinion group depends almost entirely on two instruments. Of the nine chapters, six read ANES and one reads GSS. The Cooperative Election Study is present only through derived benchmarks — the 184 MB respondent file is not in the corpus, so the chapter that would show what 60,000 respondents can do and 3,000 cannot has not been built.

Two states carry the individual-level work. Georgia supplies the voter file, the precinct returns, the cast vote records and the block-level census extracts; New Jersey supplies the second voter file. Every claim in this book that rests on individual records rests on those two states, and the chapters say so, but the map should say it too.

Twenty-six of sixty-five briefs commit a raw capture. The rest describe what arrives without shipping a copy of it, usually because the source is too large to redistribute or is not redistributable at all. That is defensible in each case and it means most chapters cannot be re-derived from what is in the folder alone.

One chapter’s dependent variable is not in any federal file. sweet-spot asks whether a district elected a Black or Latino member, and no government dataset records that — not the Clerk’s returns, not Voteview, not the Bioguide bulk data, not congress-legislators. The chapter takes it from the House Historian’s photo gallery, recovering identifiers from image filenames, because that is the only machine-reachable form the attribute has. The list has no published inclusion rule and no margin of error, and three members of the 118th House appear on both the Black and the Hispanic list. It is the only place in the book where the outcome variable is less well-documented than the predictor.
